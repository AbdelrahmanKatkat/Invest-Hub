Value investing

The questionYou estimate a company is worth $80 a share. It trades at $55. How much of a buffer does that give you if your estimate is wrong?

Intuition

Value investing means buying below an estimated intrinsic worth, with a margin of safety — the gap between estimated value and price — as a cushion against estimation error.

Worked example

(80 − 55)/80 = 31.25% margin of safety.

\text{Margin of safety}=\frac{\text{Intrinsic value}-\text{Price}}{\text{Intrinsic value}}
Try this
  1. Bring the price close to the estimated value: margin of safety shrinks toward 0.
Common traps
  • An intrinsic-value estimate is still an estimate — the margin of safety cushions estimation error, it doesn't eliminate it.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Classic value-investing commentary about buying "a dollar for fifty cents."

Check yourself

1. What is margin of safety?
The gap between estimated intrinsic value and price, as a cushion against error.
2. Intrinsic value $100, price $70. Margin of safety?
30%.
3. Does a margin of safety guarantee you're right about the value estimate?
No — it cushions error, it doesn't eliminate the risk of a wrong estimate.

Takeaways

  • Value investing buys below an estimated intrinsic worth.
  • Margin of safety cushions, but doesn't eliminate, estimation error.

Growth investing

The questionA stock trades at a P/E of 30 and is expected to grow earnings 20% a year. Is that multiple "expensive"?

Intuition

Growth investing accepts a higher P/E (Investment Fundamentals Lesson 3.3) in exchange for faster expected earnings growth. The PEG ratio relates the multiple to the growth rate as a rough cross-check.

Worked example

30/20 = 1.5 PEG. A PEG near or below 1 is sometimes used as an (imprecise) rule-of-thumb reference point, though it's not a hard rule.

PEG=\frac{P/E}{\text{Expected growth rate }(\%)}
Try this
  1. Raise expected growth with P/E fixed: PEG falls — the same multiple looks more reasonable against faster growth.
Common traps
  • Growth forecasts are uncertain — PEG is only as good as the growth estimate feeding it.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Growth-oriented funds concentrated in fast-expanding industries.

Check yourself

1. What does PEG relate?
The P/E ratio to the expected earnings growth rate.
2. P/E 40, growth 25%. PEG?
40/25 = 1.6.
3. Is a low PEG a guarantee of a good investment?
No — it depends entirely on the growth estimate being accurate.

Takeaways

  • Growth investing pays more per dollar of current earnings for expected future growth.
  • PEG is a rough cross-check, not a precise rule.

Momentum investing

The questionA stock has risen steadily for 6 months. Does that trend, by itself, say anything useful about the next 6 months?

Intuition

Momentum investing buys what has recently trended up, on evidence that trends have historically tended to persist for some period before reversing — directly related to the trend-slope idea from Investment Fundamentals Lesson 3.5, now used as a selection rule across many stocks rather than read off one chart.

Worked example

A stock rising from $40 to $52 over 6 months has a trailing 6-month return of (52−40)/40 = 30% — a momentum strategy would rank this favorably versus a flat or declining peer.

\text{trailing return}=\frac{P_{\text{now}}-P_{\text{6mo ago}}}{P_{\text{6mo ago}}}
Try this
  1. Make the two prices equal: trailing return goes to 0% — no momentum signal either way.
Common traps
  • Momentum can reverse sharply and without warning — past persistence is not a guarantee.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Momentum factor funds and trend-following strategies.

Check yourself

1. What does momentum investing buy?
What has recently trended upward, expecting the trend to persist for a while.
2. Price $30 six months ago, $36 now. Trailing return?
20%.
3. Is a trend guaranteed to continue?
No — momentum can reverse sharply.

Takeaways

  • Momentum investing bets on the persistence of recent trends.
  • It carries real reversal risk.

Income investing

The questionYou bought a stock at $50. Its dividend has since grown to $3/year. What's your yield relative to what you actually paid?

Intuition

Income investing prioritises current cash income over price appreciation. Yield on cost measures the current dividend against your original purchase price, which can look very different from the yield a new buyer sees today (the current-price dividend yield from Investment Fundamentals Lesson 2.1).

Worked example

3/50 = 6% yield on cost — potentially much higher than the stock's current-price dividend yield if the price has also risen since purchase.

\text{Yield on cost}=\frac{\text{Current dividend}}{\text{Original purchase price}}
Try this
  1. Raise the dividend with purchase price fixed: yield on cost rises — a longer holding period with dividend growth compounds this.
Common traps
  • A high yield on cost reflects a long, successful holding period — it says nothing about buying the stock fresh today at its current price.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Dividend-growth portfolios commonly cited as a retirement-income approach.

Check yourself

1. What does yield on cost use as its denominator?
The original purchase price, not the current price.
2. Dividend $4, purchase price $40. Yield on cost?
10%.
3. Does a high yield on cost mean the stock is a good buy today?
Not necessarily — it reflects the original purchase, not today's price.

Takeaways

  • Yield on cost measures income against your original price, not today's.
  • It can look very different from a fresh buyer's current yield.

Passive indexing

The questionIf markets are reasonably efficient (Financial Markets Lesson "market-efficiency") and active management costs more (Investment Fundamentals Lesson 2.7), what's the simplest alternative?

Intuition

Passive indexing gives up trying to beat a benchmark and instead tracks it as cheaply as possible. It directly combines two earlier lessons: the market-efficiency argument that beating the market consistently is hard, and the fee-drag math showing how much costs matter over decades.

Worked example

Reusing Investment Fundamentals Lesson 2.7's numbers: $10,000 at 7% gross for 30 years grows to about $75,485 at a 0.03% fee versus about $57,435 at a 1% fee — the same math, now framed as the core argument for passive indexing.

Try this
  1. Revisit the fee-drag calculator in Investment Fundamentals Lesson 2.7 and vary the years held — the passive-vs-active cost gap grows with time.
Common traps
  • "Passive" doesn't mean risk-free — an index fund still carries the full risk of the market it tracks.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Broad-market index funds and ETFs are the standard passive vehicle.

Check yourself

1. What two earlier ideas does passive indexing combine?
Market efficiency (hard to beat consistently) and the cost of fees compounding over time.
2. Does passive indexing eliminate market risk?
No — it still carries the full risk of the tracked market.
3. Why does the active-vs-passive fee gap grow with more years held?
Because the fee difference compounds, the same way returns compound (Investment Fundamentals Lesson 0.1).

Takeaways

  • Passive indexing trades the attempt to beat the market for low, predictable costs.
  • It still carries full market risk.
