Reading macro news

The questionInflation comes in at 3.5%. Markets jump. Economists had expected 3.2%. Why does a number everyone expected to be high still move markets?

Intuition

Markets react to the surprise relative to expectations, not the raw number alone. A "high" inflation reading that was already fully expected moves markets far less than a smaller number that surprises relative to consensus.

Worked example

Actual 3.5%, expected 3.2%: surprise = 3.5 − 3.2 = +0.3 percentage points — the part that actually moves prices.

\text{Surprise}=\text{Actual}-\text{Expected}
Try this
  1. Set actual equal to expected: surprise goes to 0 — in theory, little market reaction from this release alone.
Common traps
  • Reacting to the headline number without checking what was already expected.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • CPI and jobs reports routinely move markets based on the surprise versus consensus forecasts.

Check yourself

1. What actually moves markets about a data release?
The surprise relative to what was already expected, not just the raw number.
2. Actual 4.1%, expected 4.4%. Surprise?
−0.3 percentage points.
3. Would a "high" but fully-expected number move markets much?
Generally less than a smaller number that surprises consensus.

Takeaways

  • Surprise relative to expectations drives the market reaction, not the raw figure.

Earnings season

The questionA company reports EPS of $1.35 when analysts expected $1.20. Is that a "big" beat?

Intuition

An "earnings beat" is relative to consensus estimates, not some absolute bar. The same actual EPS can be a big beat against one estimate and a miss against a higher one.

Worked example

(1.35 − 1.20)/1.20 = +12.5% beat.

\text{Surprise \%}=\frac{\text{Actual}-\text{Estimate}}{\text{Estimate}}
Try this
  1. Raise the estimate above the actual: the surprise turns negative — a miss, even with the same actual result.
Common traps
  • A single quarter's "beat" or "miss" says little without the context of the full-year outlook and guidance.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Quarterly earnings reports moving individual stocks and sometimes whole sectors.

Check yourself

1. What is an "earnings beat" relative to?
Consensus analyst estimates, not an absolute number.
2. Actual EPS $2.00, estimate $1.80. Surprise %?
(2.00-1.80)/1.80 ≈ 11.1%.
3. Can the same actual EPS be a beat against one estimate and a miss against another?
Yes — it entirely depends on which estimate it's compared to.

Takeaways

  • A beat or miss is always relative to an estimate.
  • Context (guidance, full-year outlook) matters beyond one quarter.

Geopolitics & markets

The questionA country's bonds normally yield 4%. Rising political instability pushes buyers to demand more. How is that extra demand expressed in price terms?

Intuition

Political risk can add a required-return premium — investors demand extra compensation for added uncertainty, the same spread logic as Asset Classes Lesson "fixed-income-types," just driven by politics instead of credit quality.

Worked example

Baseline yield 4% + political risk premium 1.5% = required yield 5.5%.

\text{Required yield}=\text{Baseline yield}+\text{Risk premium}
Try this
  1. Raise the political risk premium: required yield rises, and (per Lesson 2.2) bond prices would fall to deliver it.
Common traps
  • Geopolitical effects are often sector- or country-specific, not a blanket move across every asset.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Energy and defense sectors reacting to specific geopolitical events; sovereign bond spreads reflecting political risk.

Check yourself

1. How does political risk typically show up in bond yields?
As an added risk premium on top of the baseline yield.
2. Baseline 3%, premium 2%. Required yield?
5%.
3. Does a geopolitical event typically move every asset the same way?
No — effects are often sector- or country-specific.

Takeaways

  • Political risk adds a required-return premium, like a credit spread.
  • Effects are usually specific, not universal.

Currency & FX basics

The question$1 buys €0.92. You convert $1,000. How many euros do you get, and what does an interest-rate difference between two countries have to do with this?

Intuition

An exchange rate converts one currency into another, directly feeding Asset Classes Lesson "international-assets"'s currency-adjusted return. Interest-rate differentials between countries relate to carry — the incentive to borrow in a low-rate currency and hold a higher-rate one, though this carries its own currency risk.

Worked example

$1,000 × 0.92 = €920.

\text{Converted amount}=\text{Amount}\times\text{Exchange rate}
Try this
  1. Raise the exchange rate: the same dollar amount converts to more of the other currency.
Common traps
  • A "carry" trade's extra yield can be wiped out (or worse) by an adverse currency move.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Converting a foreign investment's return back to a home currency (Asset Classes Lesson "international-assets").

Check yourself

1. $500 converted at a rate of 0.85. Result?
€425.
2. What is a "carry" trade built around?
Interest-rate differentials between two currencies.
3. Can currency moves offset a carry trade's extra yield?
Yes — an adverse currency move can erase or exceed the extra yield.

Takeaways

  • Exchange rates directly convert returns between currencies.
  • Carry trades add currency risk on top of the rate differential.

Signal vs. noise

The questionIf you reacted to every market headline, how often would you actually be changing your long-term plan for a good reason?

Intuition

Most daily headlines are noise relative to a long-term plan; a smaller number carry genuine signal. A disciplined, scheduled review process (Investment Fundamentals Module 4) filters this, instead of reacting to every story as it breaks.

Worked example

If roughly 1 in 20 headlines genuinely changes a reasonable long-term outlook, that's a 5% signal rate — a rough, illustrative way to frame "most of it is noise," not a precise measured statistic.

\text{signal rate}=\frac{\text{headlines that genuinely matter}}{\text{total headlines}}
Try this
  1. Raise the number of headlines with the "genuinely matters" count fixed: the signal rate falls — more noise diluting the same signal.
Common traps
  • Treating every headline as equally actionable is a recipe for the behavioural mistakes in Behavioral Finance.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • A written, scheduled review process vs. reacting to every news cycle.

Check yourself

1. Is most daily financial news signal or noise, relative to a long-term plan?
Mostly noise.
2. What defends against over-reacting to noise?
A disciplined, scheduled review process instead of reacting to every headline.
3. 2 "matters" headlines out of 40 total. Signal rate?
5%.

Takeaways

  • Most headlines are noise relative to a long-term plan.
  • A scheduled process filters signal from noise better than constant reaction.
