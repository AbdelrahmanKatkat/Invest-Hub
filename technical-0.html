Chart types

The questionA line chart, a bar chart and a candlestick chart can all show the same stock. What's actually different between them?

Intuition

A line chart plots just the closing price — simple, but it hides a period's range. A bar or candlestick chart shows the open, high, low and close for each period, revealing how much a price swung even within one day.

Worked example

A day opens $48, dips to $46, rises to $51, closes $50. A line chart would only show the "50" endpoint; a candlestick shows the full $46–$51 range that happened along the way.

Try this
  1. Picture plotting only the close each day for a week versus plotting the full daily range — notice how much intraday detail the line chart loses.
Common traps
  • A line chart can look deceptively smooth compared to what the price actually did intraday.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Candlestick charts on virtually every trading platform.

Check yourself

1. What does a line chart typically plot?
Just the closing price for each period.
2. What four values does a candlestick show for one period?
Open, high, low, close.
3. Which chart type hides intraday range: line or candlestick?
Line.

Takeaways

  • Line charts simplify; candlesticks show more of what actually happened.

Moving averages

The questionPrices over 5 days: 10, 12, 11, 13, 14. What's the 5-day moving average, and what does it smooth away?

Intuition

A moving average replaces day-to-day noise with the average of the last N periods, making the underlying trend easier to see. A crossover (e.g. a short average crossing above a longer one) is a commonly watched, debated trend signal.

Worked example

(10+12+11+13+14)/5 = 60/5 = 12.

MA_N=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}P_i
Try this
  1. Confirm the average is 12, then raise one price a lot — notice how much a single outlier can move a short average.
Common traps
  • A short moving average reacts to noise more than a long one — neither is simply "better".
Where it shows up in real investing
  • 50-day/200-day moving-average crossovers discussed in market commentary.

Check yourself

1. What does a moving average do to day-to-day noise?
Smooths it out by averaging over several periods.
2. Prices 20,22,18,24,26. 5-day average?
(20+22+18+24+26)/5 = 22.
3. Does a shorter or longer moving average react faster to new prices?
A shorter one.

Takeaways

  • Moving averages trade noise for smoothness.
  • Shorter vs. longer averages trade off responsiveness and stability.

Momentum indicators

The questionA stock's average recent gain is 1.5%, its average recent loss is 1.0%. What does the Relative Strength Index (RSI) say?

Intuition

RSI compares the size of recent gains to recent losses, scaled to 0–100. High readings (often around 70+) are commonly called "overbought"; low readings (often around 30−) "oversold" — descriptive labels, not guarantees of a reversal.

Worked example

RS = 1.5/1.0 = 1.5. RSI = 100 − 100/(1+1.5) = 100 − 40 = 60.

RS=\frac{\text{avg gain}}{\text{avg loss}}\qquad RSI=100-\frac{100}{1+RS}
Try this
  1. Raise average loss toward average gain: RS falls toward 1 and RSI moves toward 50 — neutral.
Common traps
  • "Overbought" doesn't mean "must fall now" — a strong trend can stay overbought for a long time.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • RSI commonly plotted under a price chart as an overbought/oversold gauge.

Check yourself

1. What does RSI compare?
The size of recent average gains to recent average losses.
2. Avg gain 2, avg loss 2. RSI?
RS=1; RSI=100−50=50 (neutral).
3. Does "overbought" guarantee a price drop?
No — it's descriptive, not a guarantee.

Takeaways

  • RSI scales recent gain/loss strength to 0–100.
  • Extremes are descriptive labels, not certainties.

Volume analysis

The questionA stock breaks above a resistance level on very high volume. Does that mean something different from the same breakout on thin volume?

Intuition

Volume — how many shares traded — is often used to confirm or question a price move. A move on heavy volume is commonly viewed as more "convincing" (more participants agreeing with it) than the same move on thin volume, though this is a heuristic, not a law.

Worked example

Average daily volume 1,000,000 shares; breakout day volume 2,500,000 shares: volume ratio = 2,500,000/1,000,000 = 2.5× average — a notably heavier-than-usual day.

\text{volume ratio}=\frac{\text{day's volume}}{\text{average volume}}
Try this
  1. Bring the day's volume down toward the average: the ratio approaches 1 — an unremarkable volume day.
Common traps
  • Volume confirms conviction, not direction by itself — a heavy-volume move can still reverse.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • "Breakout on high volume" is a commonly cited (and debated) pattern.

Check yourself

1. What does volume measure?
How many shares (or contracts) traded over a period.
2. Day volume 3,000,000, average 1,500,000. Ratio?
2×.
3. Does heavy volume guarantee a price move continues?
No — it's a heuristic about conviction, not a guarantee.

Takeaways

  • Volume is used to gauge conviction behind a price move.
  • It's a heuristic, not a certainty.

The debate around technical analysis

The questionIf markets are even roughly efficient (Financial Markets, Lesson "market-efficiency"), should chart patterns work at all?

Intuition

Technical analysis has enthusiastic practitioners and serious academic skeptics; weak-form market efficiency specifically questions whether past-price patterns offer a reliable edge. Neither side has fully "won" — it remains genuinely debated evidence.

Worked example

If a pattern "works" only 52% of the time versus a 50% coin flip, that's a real but very small edge — easily erased by trading costs (Investment Fundamentals Lessons 1.1–1.2) unless the edge is large and consistent.

\text{edge}=\text{win rate}-50\%
Try this
  1. Raise the win rate: the edge grows. Push trading costs up alongside: a small edge can be erased entirely.
Common traps
  • A small statistical edge can be fully erased by trading costs and taxes.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Academic market-efficiency research is routinely cited both for and against technical trading approaches.

Check yourself

1. What does weak-form efficiency specifically question?
Whether past price patterns offer a reliable trading edge.
2. Win rate 55%, two round-trip costs of 0.3pp each. Net edge?
55 − 50 − 0.6 = 4.4 percentage points.
3. Is the debate over technical analysis fully settled?
No — it remains genuinely debated.

Takeaways

  • Technical analysis is debated, not settled, evidence.
  • A small edge can be erased by trading costs.
