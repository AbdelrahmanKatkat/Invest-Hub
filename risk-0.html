Drawdown & VaR

The questionA portfolio peaked at $120,000, then fell to $84,000 before recovering. How bad was that fall, in a single number?

Intuition

Maximum drawdown measures the worst peak-to-trough decline. Value-at-Risk (VaR) is a different, forward-looking idea: an estimated loss threshold that shouldn't be exceeded more than some stated percentage of the time, based on a model's assumptions.

Worked example

(84,000 − 120,000)/120,000 = −30% maximum drawdown.

\text{Drawdown}=\frac{\text{Trough}-\text{Peak}}{\text{Peak}}
Try this
  1. Bring the trough closer to the peak: drawdown shrinks toward 0%.
Common traps
  • VaR says nothing about how bad a loss could be beyond its threshold — only that the threshold is estimated not to be crossed, at a stated frequency.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Fund fact sheets reporting "maximum drawdown" as a risk measure.

Check yourself

1. What does maximum drawdown measure?
The worst peak-to-trough decline.
2. Peak $50,000, trough $40,000. Drawdown?
−20%.
3. Does VaR describe how bad a loss could be beyond its threshold?
No — it only estimates that the threshold shouldn't be crossed more than some stated frequency, based on model assumptions.

Takeaways

  • Drawdown measures an actual worst peak-to-trough decline.
  • VaR is a model-based, forward-looking threshold, not a worst-case guarantee.

Options basics

The questionYou buy a call option, strike $50, paying a $3 premium. At expiry the stock is $65. What's your profit?

Intuition

A call option gives the right (not obligation) to buy at the strike price. Its payoff at expiry is the stock price minus the strike, if positive, and zero otherwise — the premium paid is the most a buyer can ever lose.

Worked example

Payoff = max(0, 65 − 50) = 15. Profit = 15 − 3 (premium) = $12 per share.

\text{Payoff}=\max(0,\,S-K)\qquad \text{Profit}=\text{Payoff}-\text{Premium}
Sstock price at expiry
Kstrike price
Try this
  1. Bring the stock price below the strike: payoff goes to 0 and profit becomes exactly −premium (the most a buyer can lose).
Common traps
  • A call buyer's maximum loss is the premium — but a call seller's loss is, in principle, unlimited on the upside.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Call options used to bet on or participate in a stock's upside with limited downside.

Check yourself

1. Strike $40, stock at expiry $55, premium $4. Profit?
max(0,55-40)-4 = 15-4 = $11.
2. What's the most a call buyer can lose?
The premium paid.
3. Is a call seller's potential loss also capped?
No — in principle it's unlimited as the stock price rises.

Takeaways

  • A call's payoff is max(0, stock price − strike).
  • Buyer risk is capped at the premium; seller risk is not.

Futures basics

The questionA futures contract controls $100,000 of notional value, but you only need to post $5,000 of margin. What does that imply about risk?

Intuition

A futures contract is an agreement to exchange an asset at a set price later (the same basic idea as Investment Fundamentals Lesson 2.5's futures/spot relationship), but trading it requires only a small margin deposit relative to the full contract value — creating substantial leverage.

Worked example

100,000/5,000 = 20× leverage — a 5% move in the underlying asset moves your margin by roughly 100%.

\text{Leverage}=\frac{\text{Notional value}}{\text{Margin posted}}
Try this
  1. Raise required margin: leverage falls — less risk amplification per dollar posted.
Common traps
  • Leverage amplifies losses exactly as much as gains, the same warning as margin accounts (Investment Fundamentals Lesson 1.4).
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Commodity and index futures used by institutions and speculators alike.

Check yourself

1. Notional $200,000, margin $10,000. Leverage?
20×.
2. Does leverage amplify gains only, or losses too?
Both, equally.
3. What's the relationship between futures and the spot/futures basis from Investment Fundamentals Lesson 2.5?
The futures price there is exactly this kind of contract's agreed future price.

Takeaways

  • Futures require only a fraction of notional value as margin, creating leverage.
  • Leverage amplifies gains and losses equally.

Hedging with derivatives

The questionYou own a stock at $100 and are worried about a near-term drop. A put option, strike $95, costs $4. How does that protect you?

Intuition

A put option gives the right to sell at the strike price, so it gains value as the stock falls — like insurance against a decline. Buying a put alongside owning the stock is hedging: using a derivative to offset risk rather than to speculate.

Worked example

Below $95, losses on the stock are offset by gains on the put; the net effective floor is strike minus premium = 95 − 4 = $91, versus an unprotected position that could keep falling further.

\text{Effective floor}=\text{Strike}-\text{Premium paid}
Try this
  1. Raise the strike (buy more protection): the effective floor rises, typically at a higher premium cost in reality.
Common traps
  • A hedge has a cost (the premium) even if the drop never happens — like any insurance.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Portfolio managers buying index puts ahead of an uncertain event as portfolio insurance.

Check yourself

1. What does a protective put do?
Offsets losses on a held stock below the strike, acting like insurance.
2. Strike $80, premium $3. Effective floor?
$77.
3. Does a hedge cost anything if the feared drop never happens?
Yes — the premium is spent either way, like any insurance.

Takeaways

  • A protective put sets an effective floor below which losses are offset.
  • Hedging has a real, ongoing cost.

The Greeks, briefly

The questionAn option has a delta of 0.5. The stock moves up $2. Roughly how much does the option's price move?

Intuition

Delta approximates how much an option's price moves per $1 move in the underlying stock — a quick, approximate sensitivity measure (other "Greeks" measure sensitivity to time, volatility and rates, beyond this basic pass).

Worked example

0.5 × $2 = $1 estimated option price move.

\Delta\text{Option price}\approx \text{Delta}\times\Delta\text{Stock price}
Try this
  1. Raise delta toward 1: the option moves more like the stock itself, dollar for dollar.
Common traps
  • Delta itself changes as the stock price moves — the approximation is only accurate for small moves.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Delta is quoted alongside every listed option.

Check yourself

1. What does delta approximate?
How much an option's price moves per $1 move in the underlying stock.
2. Delta 0.3, stock moves $5. Estimated option move?
$1.50.
3. Is delta constant as the stock price changes?
No — it changes too, so the approximation is best for small moves.

Takeaways

  • Delta is a quick sensitivity approximation for an option's price.
  • It's most accurate for small underlying price moves.
