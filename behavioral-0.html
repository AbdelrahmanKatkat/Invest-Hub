Anchoring & framing

The questionA stock you bought at $100 is now $70. Does "it's down 30% from where I bought it" matter to what it's worth going forward? And if you're negotiating a car starting from a $25,000 sticker price, does that number change what you're willing to pay — even though the car's actual worth hasn't moved either way?

Intuition

Anchoring is letting an often-irrelevant reference point — your purchase price, a past 52-week high, a sticker price — skew a current judgement. The anchor doesn't have to be rational or even related to the decision: in a well-known experiment, people who first wrote down the last two digits of their own phone number gave systematically different (higher or lower) guesses for an unrelated question, like the number of countries in Africa. Framing is a close cousin: how the same fact is phrased changes the decision even though the underlying facts are identical — "90% survival" feels different from "10% mortality," though they describe exactly the same outcome.

Two patterns are worth telling apart:

  • Anchoring in valuation — holding onto a stale reference price (what you paid, a past high) as if it still carries information about future value, when it generally doesn't.
  • Anchoring in negotiation — the first number spoken (a listing price, an opening offer) pulls the final agreed number toward it, even when both sides privately know the opening number was a tactic rather than a fair-value estimate.
Worked example 1 — valuation anchoring

A $100 purchase now at $70 is, going forward, simply a $70 asset — its future prospects don't know or care what you paid. Anchoring to $100 can cause holding on "to get back to even" for a reason unrelated to the asset's actual current value.

Build it up: insufficient adjustment, and how hard an anchor pulls

Anchoring isn't just "a number sits in your head" — it specifically causes insufficient adjustment away from the anchor, even when people consciously try to move away from it. A classic demonstration: ask one group to quickly estimate 1×2×3×4×5×6×7×8, and another group to estimate 8×7×6×5×4×3×2×1. Both products are exactly the same number, but people given the ascending order (which starts small) tend to guess much lower than people given the descending order (which starts large), because each group anchors on the first few numbers it sees and then adjusts too little to reach the true total.

Worked example 2 — the multiplication-chain anchor

1×2×3×4×5×6×7×8 = 8×7×6×5×4×3×2×1 = 40,320, exactly, regardless of the order you multiply in — yet a quick mental estimate starting from "8×7=56…" tends to run higher than one starting from "1×2=2…," purely from which number anchors the first impression.

The same mechanism drives negotiation. A simple, illustrative model: the final agreed price lands between the opening anchor and your own fair-value estimate, weighted by an "anchoring weight" k that captures how strongly the anchor pulls you.

Worked example 3 — an anchored negotiation

Fair value $20,000, opening offer (anchor) $16,000, anchoring weight k = 0.4: final price ≈ 16,000×0.4 + 20,000×0.6 = $18,400 — pulled noticeably toward the anchor, even though the anchor was below fair value and a purely rational buyer would have no reason to pay less than fair value just because the seller opened low.

\text{anchoring gap}=\text{reference price}-\text{current price}\qquad \text{Final price}\approx k\cdot\text{Anchor}+(1-k)\cdot\text{Fair value}
reference price, current pricethe original/past value anchored to, and the actual current value
kanchoring weight, 0–1 (0 = anchor fully ignored; 1 = final price equals the anchor exactly)
Try this
  1. Change the reference price: the "gap" changes, but the asset's actual current value hasn't.
  2. In the negotiation plot, set k=0: the final-price line goes flat at the fair value regardless of anchor (full adjustment). Set k=1: it becomes a diagonal — final price equals the anchor exactly, no adjustment at all.
Common traps
  • "Getting back to even" is an anchor-driven goal, not a forward-looking investment reason.
  • Believing a round, salient number (a stock's prior 52-week high, a retailer's listing price) carries information about future value just because it's memorable.
  • Assuming that knowing about anchoring makes you immune to it — awareness measurably reduces the effect in experiments, but rarely eliminates it entirely.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Holding a losing position specifically to "break even" rather than reassessing it fresh.
  • A "was $100, now $70" retail markdown tag anchoring perceived discount size regardless of the item's actual worth.
  • Opening listing prices in real estate (e.g., a home listed at $450,000) pulling offers toward that number even when comparable sales suggest a different fair value.

Check yourself

1. What is anchoring?
Letting an often-irrelevant reference point skew a current judgement.
2. 1×2×3×...×8 and 8×7×...×1 give the exact same number. Why might two groups estimating them quickly still guess differently?
Both equal exactly 40,320 — but each group anchors on the first number(s) it sees (small vs. large) and adjusts insufficiently from there, landing on different estimates despite the identical true answer.
3. Fair value $30,000, opening anchor $24,000, anchoring weight k = 0.5. Modeled final price?
24,000×0.5 + 30,000×0.5 = $27,000 — still pulled toward the anchor.

Takeaways

  • Anchoring attaches too much weight to an often-irrelevant reference point, and adjustment away from it tends to be insufficient.
  • Framing changes decisions without changing the underlying facts.
  • A simple weighted model (k) shows how strongly an anchor can pull a final decision — useful for recognizing when you're negotiating against an anchor rather than against fair value.

Overconfidence

The questionYou're 90% sure a stock will rise. How often would predictions you're "90% sure" about actually need to come true for that confidence to be justified? And if being overconfident makes you trade more often, how would you put a number on what that extra trading actually costs?

Intuition

Overconfidence is being more certain of your own judgement than your actual track record justifies. If your "90% confident" calls are only right 70% of the time, your confidence is miscalibrated — a commonly observed pattern in both everyday judgement and investing. Researchers usually split it into a few related flavors:

  • Overprecision — stating a narrower range of possible outcomes than your actual accuracy supports (e.g. "I'm sure this stock lands between $48 and $52" when your track record says your ranges miss far more often than that).
  • Overplacement — the "better than average" effect: in surveys, a majority of self-directed investors rate their own stock-picking skill as above the median, which is mathematically impossible for the whole group.
  • Overestimation — overrating your own ability to control or predict an outcome, such as the timing of a market top or bottom.
Worked example 1 — the calibration gap

Stated confidence 90%, actual accuracy 70%: miscalibration gap = 90 − 70 = 20 percentage points of overconfidence.

Build it up: the cost of acting on it — overtrading

Overconfidence doesn't just leave a miscalibrated number on paper — it has a measurable behavioral consequence. Investors more confident in their own stock-picking or market-timing ability tend to trade more often (higher portfolio turnover). Trading isn't free: every round trip carries costs — commissions, the bid-ask spread, taxes on realized gains — and even a small per-trade cost compounds into a large return drag over years, regardless of whether any individual trade turned out to be a good call.

Worked example 2 — the cost of turnover

Portfolio turnover of 200%/year (the whole portfolio effectively bought and sold twice over) at a 0.5% round-trip cost: annual drag = 2.0 × 0.005 = 1.0%/year. Compounded over 20 years, (1 − 0.01)²⁰ ≈ 0.818 — meaning roughly 18.2% of the portfolio's ending value is given up purely to trading friction, before even asking whether the extra trades picked better stocks.

\text{miscalibration}=\text{stated confidence}-\text{actual accuracy}\qquad \text{annual drag}=\text{turnover}\times\text{cost per round trip}
turnoverfraction of portfolio value traded per year (2.0 = 200%, the whole portfolio turned over twice)
cost per round tripcommissions + spread + tax impact, as a % of the amount traded
Try this
  1. Bring actual accuracy up to match stated confidence: the gap closes to 0 — well-calibrated.
  2. In the turnover calculator, push turnover down toward 0: the cumulative cost over 20 years shrinks toward 0%. Push it up toward 5× (a very active trader): the cost consumes a large share of the ending value.
Common traps
  • Feeling certain is not the same as being accurate — the two are only loosely related without tracking a real history.
  • More trades ≠ more skill — turnover is a cost in itself, independent of whether any individual trade was a good call.
  • Assuming experience makes you immune — overconfidence is often strongest in one's own area of familiarity, not weakest.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Investors overestimating their own ability to time market tops and bottoms.
  • Actively self-directed, high-turnover trading accounts versus a low-turnover index-fund approach (Investment Fundamentals Module 2, Technical Analysis path).
  • Surveyed self-directed traders rating their own skill as above-average far more often than is mathematically possible for the group as a whole.

Check yourself

1. What is overconfidence, precisely?
Being more certain of a judgement than your actual accuracy justifies.
2. A trader has 150% annual turnover and pays 0.4% round-trip cost. Annual drag, and cumulative cost over 10 years?
Drag = 1.5 × 0.004 = 0.6%/year. Over 10 years: 1 − (0.994)¹⁰ ≈ 5.84% of ending value given up to trading costs.
3. Is feeling very certain proof of being right?
No — confidence and accuracy are only loosely related without a tracked record.

Takeaways

  • Overconfidence is a measurable gap between stated certainty and actual accuracy.
  • It commonly shows up behaviorally as overtrading, which carries a real, compounding cost regardless of whether the extra trades were good calls.
  • Tracking a real record — and your own turnover and costs — is the only way to check both calibration and its price.

Mental accounting

The questionA $500 tax refund and $500 of extra salary are both exactly $500. Do people treat them the same way? And if you're earning 1% interest on a savings account while carrying a balance on a 20% credit card, is keeping the two accounts separate costing you anything real?

Intuition

Mental accounting is treating money differently depending on its source or mental "label" — a windfall often gets spent more freely than equivalent earned income, even though both spend identically. Money is fungible: a dollar from a bonus buys exactly the same thing as a dollar from a paycheck, yet people routinely build separate mental "buckets" that don't interact with each other. A few recognizable patterns:

  • The house-money effect — treating gains already earned as "the market's money," and taking bigger risks with it than you would with your own original capital, even though it's all the same net worth now.
  • Labeled buckets — a "serious" retirement account treated with real caution while a "fun money" brokerage account is traded far more speculatively, despite both drawing on the same overall risk capacity.
  • Not netting — holding a low-yield asset and a high-rate liability side by side without weighing them against each other, because they sit in different mental (or literal) accounts.
Worked example 1 — windfall vs. salary

If 80% of a $500 windfall gets spent versus 20% of $500 in regular salary, that's a 60-percentage-point gap in spending behavior for an identical dollar amount.

Build it up: keeping separate buckets even when it costs you money

Many households simultaneously hold a low-yield savings account and carry a balance on high-interest debt. Classical finance says this should rarely happen deliberately — you're effectively "borrowing" at a high rate to "save" at a low rate — but mental accounting explains why it's common: the savings account is labeled "emergency fund, don't touch," and the debt is labeled "pay down gradually," and the two are never let to interact even though they're part of the same net worth. (Keeping some cash for genuine emergency liquidity is a legitimate, deliberate tradeoff — the bias is keeping the full balance separate without ever weighing that tradeoff explicitly.)

Worked example 2 — the cost of not netting

Savings $5,000 at 1% ⇒ $50/year earned. Debt $5,000 at 20% ⇒ $1,000/year paid. Net annual cost of keeping both "buckets" open rather than netting them = $1,000 − $50 = $950/year — a concrete, dollar price for treating fungible money as if it weren't.

\text{spending gap}=\%\text{ spent (windfall)}-\%\text{ spent (salary)}\qquad \text{net annual cost}=\text{debt}\times r_{\text{debt}}-\text{savings}\times r_{\text{savings}}
debt, savingsbalances held in each "bucket"
r_debt, r_savingsthe interest rate paid on the debt and earned on the savings, respectively
Try this
  1. Bring both spending percentages together: the gap shrinks to 0 — the money is being treated the same regardless of label.
  2. In the netting calculator, set debt to 0: the "cost" turns into pure savings income — the cost only exists because a low-yield asset and a high-rate liability are being held side by side.
Common traps
  • Treating a "play money" investing account differently from a "serious" one, even though both carry identical real-world risk.
  • Treating a "house money" gain as less painful to lose than original capital, leading to riskier bets with money that is, in fact, now simply yours.
  • Keeping a full emergency fund in cash while carrying revolving high-interest debt without ever explicitly comparing the two rates.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Treating dividend income as "spendable" while treating an equal capital gain as "off-limits," though both are the same net worth.
  • Keeping an emergency fund earning near-zero interest while carrying revolving high-interest debt, rather than netting the two.
  • Separate "fun money" brokerage accounts traded more speculatively than a retirement account, despite both drawing on the same overall risk capacity.

Check yourself

1. What is mental accounting?
Treating money differently based on its source or label, even though it's fungible.
2. Savings $8,000 at 1.5%, revolving debt $8,000 at 22%. Net annual cost of keeping them as separate buckets?
8,000 × 0.22 − 8,000 × 0.015 = $1,760 − $120 = $1,640/year.
3. Why is keeping a low-yield savings account open while carrying higher-rate debt a mental-accounting bias rather than always a deliberate liquidity choice?
Because the decision often isn't explicitly weighing the cost (paying more in debt interest than you earn in savings) against the benefit of accessible cash — it's instead driven by treating the two as separate, non-interacting buckets.

Takeaways

  • Money is fungible; mental labels shouldn't change its real value.
  • This bias can creep into how accounts or "buckets" are treated.
  • Simultaneously holding low-yield savings and high-interest debt has a measurable net cost — a concrete price mental accounting can impose, beyond just "a different vibe" about the money.

Prospect theory

The questionA $1,000 loss and a $1,000 gain are the same size. Do they feel the same size? And does a gain from $100 to $200 feel the same size as a gain from $1,100 to $1,200?

Intuition

Prospect theory describes how people weigh gains and losses relative to a reference point, rather than in terms of total wealth — and finds two separate effects at once:

  • Loss aversion — losses are commonly felt more strongly than equivalent gains, a pattern consistent with the recovery-math asymmetry from Investment Fundamentals Lesson 0.9 (a 20% loss needs a 25% gain to undo).
  • Diminishing sensitivity — the subjective impact of a gain or loss shrinks as its size grows farther from the reference point, in both directions — the same logic as diminishing marginal utility, but applied to changes relative to a reference point rather than to absolute wealth.
Worked example 1 — the simple loss-aversion multiplier

If losses are felt about twice as strongly as equivalent gains (an illustrative, commonly-cited finding, not a universal constant), a $1,000 loss might feel subjectively like "losing" roughly $2,000 worth of good feeling compared to a $1,000 gain's positive feeling.

Build it up: the full value function — loss aversion and diminishing sensitivity together

The simple λ-multiplier captures loss aversion but misses the second effect. A widely-cited illustrative model (Kahneman & Tversky, 1992) combines both: gains are weighed as x^{\alpha}, losses as -\lambda(-x)^{\alpha}, with commonly-cited illustrative parameters α ≈ 0.88 and λ ≈ 2.25. The exponent α < 1 is what produces diminishing sensitivity — each side of the curve flattens out as it moves away from zero.

Worked example 2 — checking the loss-aversion ratio

v(100) = 100^0.88 ≈ 57.54. v(−100) = −2.25 × 100^0.88 ≈ −129.47. The ratio |−129.47| / 57.54 ≈ 2.25 — exactly λ, confirming the loss side is scaled up by precisely the loss-aversion multiplier.

Worked example 3 — diminishing sensitivity in action

v(200) ≈ 200^0.88 ≈ 105.90, so v(200) − v(100) ≈ 105.90 − 57.54 = 48.36. Compare that to v(100) − v(0) = 57.54 − 0 = 57.54. The second $100 of gain (48.36) feels smaller than the first $100 (57.54) — even though both are exactly $100 in dollar terms.

v(x)=\begin{cases}x^{\alpha}, & x\geq 0\\ -\lambda(-x)^{\alpha}, & x<0\end{cases}
xa gain or loss relative to the reference point
αdiminishing-sensitivity exponent, 0<α≤1 (illustrative commonly-cited value ≈0.88)
λloss-aversion multiplier (commonly cited estimates are roughly 2–2.25, illustrative and debated)
Try this
  1. Set λ to 1 in the first calculator: losses and gains are treated symmetrically — no loss aversion.
  2. In the value-function plot, compare how steeply the curve falls just left of zero (losses) versus just right of zero (gains) — that's the loss-aversion kink. Then compare how flat the curve becomes far from zero in either direction — that flattening is diminishing sensitivity.
Common traps
  • Treating λ as a precise, universal number — estimates vary across studies and contexts.
  • Assuming diminishing sensitivity means large losses "don't matter much" — it means each additional dollar matters less at the margin, not that the total subjective impact is small.
  • Treating the reference point as fixed — it can shift (e.g., after a large prior gain, a subsequent smaller loss may not even register as a loss against the original capital), which can itself skew further risk-taking.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Why a drawdown often feels worse than an equivalent earlier gain felt good — directly motivating Lesson 0.9's panic-selling example.
  • The disposition effect: selling winning positions too early (locking in a gain whose further upside feels less and less exciting) while holding losing positions too long (avoiding realizing a disproportionately painful loss).
  • Asymmetric-payoff products (e.g., some structured notes) priced partly around known asymmetries in how people weigh small probabilities of large gains or losses (Asset Classes, Risk & Derivatives paths).

Check yourself

1. According to prospect theory, are losses or gains typically felt more strongly?
Losses.
2. Using v(100) ≈ 57.54 and v(−100) ≈ −129.47, what does their ratio confirm?
|−129.47| / 57.54 ≈ 2.25, matching λ exactly — the loss side is scaled up by precisely the loss-aversion multiplier.
3. Does a gain from $100 to $200 feel twice as good as a gain from $0 to $100?
No — diminishing sensitivity means v(200)−v(100) ≈ 48.36 is smaller than v(100)−v(0) ≈ 57.54, even though both are exactly $100 moves.

Takeaways

  • Losses are commonly felt more strongly than equivalent gains.
  • The full value function adds diminishing sensitivity: equal-sized changes feel smaller farther from the reference point, in both directions.
  • This compounds with the mathematical asymmetry of recovering from a loss (Investment Fundamentals Lesson 0.9), making a loss-then-recovery sequence feel worse than it mathematically should.

Debiasing techniques

The questionIf biases are built into how people think, can anything practical actually defend against them? And specifically — does stepping out of the market during a scary downturn, intending to get back in once things feel calmer, actually protect returns?

Intuition

Checklists, rules written in advance, and automation reduce the room for an in-the-moment biased decision — you're deciding the rule when calm, not deciding the trade when emotional. This is the same principle behind Investment Fundamentals Module 4's automated contributions and written plan. A few concrete forms this takes:

  • Checklists — a fixed list of conditions to verify before acting, so a decision isn't made purely on a felt impulse.
  • Pre-committed rules — deciding in advance, e.g. "I will rebalance back to my target weights whenever an asset class drifts more than 5 percentage points," rather than deciding in the moment whether today "feels right."
  • Automation — scheduled contributions and rebalancing that execute on their own, removing the decision point (and the emotion attached to it) entirely.
Worked example 1 — an illustrative process-discipline score

An illustrative "decision quality" score: a plan made with a written checklist followed consistently might score 85/100 for process discipline, versus 40/100 for ad-hoc, in-the-moment decisions — a stand-in for "a documented process tends to reduce impulsive mistakes," not a literal measurement.

Build it up: the cost of stepping out at the wrong time

One of the most common real-world failures of debiasing is exactly the scenario in the opening question: pausing contributions or fully exiting the market after a scary drawdown — loss aversion (Lesson 0.4) makes this feel protective in the moment — and only getting back in once it "feels safe" again, which is often after much of a recovery has already happened. Automation and a written plan specifically target this exact failure mode by removing the in-the-moment decision entirely.

Worked example 2 — quantifying the gap

$10,000 invested steadily for 20 years at an illustrative 7%/year average return grows to 10,000 × 1.07²⁰ ≈ $38,696.84. If instead an investor's attempts to "time" re-entries lower their realized annualized return to 4.5%/year (a stand-in for repeatedly being late back into recoveries — illustrative, not a specific historical dataset), the same starting amount grows to only 10,000 × 1.045²⁰ ≈ $24,117.14 — a gap of roughly $14,580 from the exact same starting capital and the exact same 20 years.

Pprincipal (starting amount)
rthe annualized return actually realized — automated/steady vs. behaviorally-timed
nyears

Common traps
  • Having a plan doesn't help if it's abandoned whenever it's inconvenient — the discipline is the point.
  • Believing that simply "deciding to be more disciplined" next time (without a specific pre-committed rule or automation) reliably works — intentions made in a calm moment often don't survive an emotionally charged one without a concrete mechanism.
  • Treating a debiasing setup as one-and-done — a plan still needs deliberate, scheduled review (e.g., annually), rather than being either ignored forever or renegotiated in the heat of the moment.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Investment policy statements and automated contributions (Investment Fundamentals Module 4).
  • Automatic portfolio rebalancing rules (sell high, buy low, by a pre-set rule) that fire regardless of how an investor currently feels about the market, directly countering the biases in Lessons 0.1–0.4.
  • A pre-committed "cooling-off" rule before a large discretionary trade (e.g., a self-imposed 24-hour waiting period) — a personal, everyday analogue to a written checklist.

Check yourself

1. Why does deciding a rule "in advance" help?
It removes the in-the-moment, emotionally-charged decision that biases tend to exploit.
2. Name a concrete debiasing technique.
Any of: a written checklist, a pre-committed rule, automated/scheduled contributions or rebalancing.
3. $20,000 compounding at 7%/year for 15 years vs. 5%/year for 15 years. Roughly what's the ending-value gap?
20,000×1.07¹⁵ ≈ $55,180.63 versus 20,000×1.05¹⁵ ≈ $41,578.56 — a gap of about $13,602, purely from the realized annual rate over the same 15 years.

Takeaways

  • Pre-committed rules and automation reduce room for in-the-moment bias.
  • A plan only helps if it's actually followed.
  • Behaviorally-timed exits and re-entries can quietly cost a large, compounding share of ending wealth relative to simply staying automated — even using the same starting capital and the same number of years.
