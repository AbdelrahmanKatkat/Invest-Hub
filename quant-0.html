Factor models, briefly

The questionCan a stock's expected return be broken into pieces tied to measurable, shared characteristics — rather than treated as one unexplained number, or as a single bet on "the market" as a whole?

Intuition

A factor model expresses expected return as a base rate plus exposures to shared, measurable characteristics (factors) — the same idea introduced less formally in Portfolio Management's factor-investing lesson, now treated as a tool for building systematic signals. Commonly cited factors include:

  • Value — cheapness relative to fundamentals (e.g. a low price-to-earnings ratio).
  • Size — smaller companies, which have historically shown different average returns than larger ones.
  • Momentum — recent relative strength or weakness in price.
  • Quality — profitability and balance-sheet strength.
  • Low volatility — historically steadier price behaviour.

Each factor gets its own coefficient (how much it contributes per unit of exposure), and a stock's total expected return is just the sum of a base rate plus every factor's contribution.

Worked example 1 — a simple two-factor model

E[R] = 2% + 0.08×(factor 1 score) + 0.05×(factor 2 score). With scores of 10 and 6: 2 + 0.8 + 0.3 = 3.1% over the base rate's own period.

Build it up: the single-factor special case (CAPM), and generalizing it

The oldest and simplest factor model is the CAPM (Capital Asset Pricing Model): expected return equals a risk-free rate plus one single factor exposure — beta — times the market's own excess return (the "market risk premium"). A multi-factor model is just this same idea generalized to more than one factor at once.

Worked example 2 — CAPM as a one-factor model

Risk-free rate 2%, market risk premium 6%, beta 1.2: E[R] = 2 + 1.2×6 = 9.2%.

Worked example 3 — adding a second factor on top of CAPM

Keep the same CAPM term (9.2%) and add a size-factor exposure of 0.5 with its own 3% premium: E[R] = 2 + 1.2×6 + 0.5×3 = 9.2 + 1.5 = 10.7% — exactly the multi-factor pattern from Worked example 1, now built on top of a real, named single-factor baseline instead of generic "factor 1/factor 2" scores.

E[R]=a+b_1x_1+b_2x_2\qquad E[R]_{\text{CAPM}}=R_f+\beta(R_m-R_f)
x₁, x₂factor exposure scores
b₁, b₂how much each factor contributes
Rfthe risk-free rate
β (beta)exposure to the single market factor in CAPM
Rm − Rfthe market risk premium — the market's expected excess return over the risk-free rate
Try this
  1. Raise a factor's coefficient: its contribution to expected return grows proportionally to its score.
  2. In the CAPM line below, push beta above 1: expected return rises faster than the market premium itself — the stock is modeled as riskier than the market.
Common traps
  • A factor model fits historical data by construction — it doesn't guarantee the same relationship holds going forward.
  • Treating beta (or any single factor) as the whole story — real multi-factor models commonly combine several factors precisely because one factor alone leaves much of a stock's return unexplained.
  • Assuming a factor's historical premium is a fixed constant — factor premiums (like the market risk premium itself) move over time and across market regimes.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Multi-factor risk models used by quantitative funds.
  • CAPM beta, still widely quoted for individual stocks and used as a starting point before adding further factors.
  • "Smart beta" / factor exchange-traded funds built explicitly around named factors such as value, size, momentum or low volatility (illustrative categories, not a recommendation of any specific fund).

Check yourself

1. What does a factor model add to a simple base rate, and what is CAPM an example of?
Exposures to shared, measurable characteristics (factors), each with its own coefficient; CAPM is the simplest case, with a single factor (market beta).
2. Rf=1%, beta=1.5, market premium=4%. CAPM E[R]?
1 + 1.5×4 = 7%.
3. Does a factor model — single- or multi-factor — guarantee future performance?
No — it's fit to historical data (premiums and exposures alike) and may not hold going forward.

Takeaways

  • Factor models decompose expected return into measurable exposures, from the single-factor CAPM up to many factors at once.
  • Fit to the past doesn't guarantee the future, for any number of factors.
  • Multi-factor models are a direct generalization of CAPM's one-factor idea, not a wholly different concept.

Backtesting basics

The questionYou test a trading rule on 10 years of data and it looks great. Is that proof it will work going forward — and does testing it on one single held-out period really settle the question?

Intuition

Backtesting splits data into an in-sample period (used to build/tune the rule) and an out-of-sample period (used to test it, untouched during development). A rule that only looks good in-sample may simply be fit to that specific data's noise rather than a real, repeatable pattern.

  • In-sample performance answers: "does this rule fit the data I built it on?" — almost any rule can be made to fit its own training data well enough, given enough tuning.
  • Out-of-sample performance answers the question that actually matters: "does this rule still work on data it never saw?"
Worked example 1 — a simple static split

10 years of data split 70/30: 7 years in-sample, 3 years out-of-sample = 30% of the data reserved purely for honest testing.

Build it up: one split isn't enough — walk-forward testing

A single static split has a weakness of its own: the one out-of-sample period you happened to choose could itself be an unusually easy or unusually hard stretch, by chance. Walk-forward (rolling-window) testing fixes this by repeating the split many times: train on a window of years, test on the next, then slide the whole window forward and repeat — producing several independent out-of-sample results instead of just one.

Worked example 2 — walk-forward folds

15 years of data, a 5-year training window, tested 2 years at a time, sliding forward with no overlap: (15 − 5) / 2 = 5 separate out-of-sample test folds, each built from a rule trained only on the years before it — a far more demanding (and more honest) test than one lucky or unlucky 30% slice.

\text{out-of-sample years}=\text{total years}\times(1-\text{in-sample fraction})\qquad \text{folds}\approx\frac{\text{total years}-\text{training window}}{\text{test window}}
training windowyears of data the rule is built/tuned on, per fold
test windowyears of data the rule is then tested on, untouched during that fold's training
Try this
  1. Raise the in-sample fraction toward 100%: almost no data is left for an honest, untouched test.
  2. In the walk-forward calculator, shrink the test window: more, shorter folds — more independent checks, each on less data.
Common traps
  • Peeking at out-of-sample data while still tuning the rule quietly turns it into in-sample data.
  • Treating a single out-of-sample period as the final word — one held-out stretch can itself be an easy or hard draw; walk-forward testing across several folds is a sturdier (though still imperfect) check.
  • Ignoring look-ahead bias — using information that would not actually have been available at the time (e.g. a restated financial figure, or a stock universe defined using hindsight) silently leaks future information into a "backtest."
Where it shows up in real investing
  • A strategy that looked great in-sample but failed once tested out-of-sample.
  • Walk-forward (rolling-window) backtests used by systematic funds to continually re-validate a rule as new data arrives, rather than relying on one fixed historical test.
  • Index-reconstitution and restatement effects that can introduce look-ahead bias into a careless backtest of, say, a rule applied to "the S&P 500" without accounting for which companies were actually in the index at each historical date.

Check yourself

1. What's the purpose of an out-of-sample period, and why might even that not be enough on its own?
An honest test on data the rule wasn't built or tuned on; but one single out-of-sample period can itself be an easy or hard draw by chance, which is why walk-forward testing uses several.
2. 20 years of data, 80% in-sample. Out-of-sample years? Separately, 20 years total with a 6-year training window and a 2-year test window, non-overlapping: how many walk-forward folds?
4 years out-of-sample; (20 − 6) / 2 = 7 folds.
3. What is look-ahead bias, concretely?
Using information in a backtest that would not actually have been available at that historical point in time, which makes the backtest's results unrealistically good.

Takeaways

  • Backtesting needs a genuinely untouched out-of-sample period.
  • In-sample success alone is not proof of a real strategy — and even one out-of-sample success can be a lucky single draw.
  • Walk-forward testing and avoiding look-ahead bias are two separate, necessary disciplines for an honest backtest.

Signal construction

The questionA stock scores 85 on some measure, with a universe average of 70 and a standard deviation of 10. How "extreme" is that, in a comparable unit — and what do you do once you have several different measures, each on its own unrelated scale?

Intuition

A z-score normalises a raw measure into "how many standard deviations from the average" — using the same standard-deviation idea from Investment Fundamentals Lesson 0.5 — so very different measures (valuation, momentum, quality) can be compared and combined on one common scale.

Worked example 1 — a single z-score

(85 − 70)/10 = 1.5 — one and a half standard deviations above average.

Build it up: combining several signals into one composite score

Real systematic processes rarely rank stocks on one measure alone. Once a valuation measure and a momentum measure are each converted to their own z-score, they live on the same scale and can be combined into a single composite score — typically a weighted average, with the weights reflecting how much conviction the process places in each signal.

Worked example 2 — a composite score

A stock's valuation z-score is 1.5 (cheap) and its momentum z-score is (40 − 50)/20 = −0.5 (weak recent momentum). Weighted 60% valuation / 40% momentum: composite = 0.6×1.5 + 0.4×(−0.5) = 0.9 − 0.2 = 0.7 — a modestly positive overall score, pulled down from the valuation signal alone by weaker momentum.

z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\qquad \text{composite}=w_1z_1+w_2z_2
xthe raw measure
μ, σthe universe's average and standard deviation
z1, z2each signal's own z-score
w1, w2the weight placed on each signal in the composite
Try this
  1. Set x equal to the average: z becomes 0 — exactly typical.
  2. In the composite calculator, push one weight to 1 and the other to 0: the composite collapses to that single signal's z-score exactly.
Common traps
  • A z-score depends on the comparison universe chosen — the same raw value can have different z-scores in different universes.
  • Combining z-scores with arbitrary weights is itself a modeling choice — picking weights by searching for whichever combination looked best historically reintroduces the overfitting risk of Lesson 0.5.
  • A raw measure with extreme outliers can produce an extreme z-score that overwhelms a composite — many practitioners cap ("winsorize") raw values before scoring them.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Ranking stocks by a standardised valuation or momentum score.
  • Multi-factor composite scores — combining, say, value, quality and momentum z-scores into one ranking — used by systematic equity strategies and "smart beta" style indices.
  • Winsorizing/clipping extreme raw values, a standard data-cleaning step before scoring, used at index providers and quant research desks alike.

Check yourself

1. What does a z-score measure, and why would you compute it for several different raw measures before combining them?
How many standard deviations a value is from the average; different raw measures live on different scales, so z-scoring puts them on one comparable scale before a weighted combination makes sense.
2. x=60, μ=50, σ=5. Z-score? Also, two signals' z-scores are 2 and −1, weighted 50/50 — composite?
z = 2; composite = 0.5×2 + 0.5×(−1) = 0.5.
3. Why could choosing composite weights by testing many weight combinations and picking the best-looking historical result be risky?
That's exactly the overfitting mechanism of Lesson 0.5 — with enough weight combinations tried, one will look good purely by chance, without necessarily reflecting a real, repeatable edge.

Takeaways

  • Z-scores put different measures on one comparable scale.
  • They depend on the chosen comparison universe.
  • Combining several z-scored signals into a composite is standard practice, but the combination weights are themselves parameters that can be overfit.

Systematic vs. discretionary

The questionTwo managers both use valuation and momentum signals. One follows them mechanically every time; the other uses them as one input among several judgement calls. Same strategy? And if ten different systematic funds all built similar rule-based signals independently, are their results actually independent of each other?

Intuition

A systematic process follows fixed, pre-defined rules consistently; a discretionary one leaves room for human judgement on top of (or instead of) the rules. This is really the same process-vs-impulse theme from Behavioral Finance's debiasing lesson, applied at the level of an entire investment process.

Worked example 1 — override rate

If a systematic process follows its rules on 100% of decisions and a discretionary one overrides the rule-based signal on, say, 30% of decisions, that 30% is exactly where human judgement (and human bias) can enter.

Build it up: "independent" systematic processes can still move together

A common misconception is that a fully systematic process is automatically safer because it removes human judgement. But many different managers can independently arrive at similar systematic rules — similar valuation and momentum signals, for instance — and when they do, their positions become crowded: different funds, running what looks on paper like separate strategies, end up holding highly correlated books. If one is forced to unwind quickly (say, from a margin call), its selling pushes prices against all the others at the same time — even though none of them shares a single common manager making a single common decision.

Worked example 2 — crowding shown through correlation

Two systematic strategies each have the same 20% annual volatility, blended 50/50. At a low correlation of ρ=0.2 between them: σ_P = √(0.5²×0.2² + 0.5²×0.2² + 2×0.5×0.5×0.2×0.2²) = √0.024 ≈ 15.5% — meaningfully below the 20% either strategy carries alone. Raise the correlation to ρ=0.9 (crowded, moving together) with nothing else changed: √(0.5²×0.2² + 0.5²×0.2² + 2×0.5×0.5×0.9×0.2²) = √0.038 ≈ 19.5% — almost no diversification benefit left, even though each strategy's own volatility never changed.

\text{override rate}=\frac{\text{decisions overriding the rule}}{\text{total decisions}}\qquad \sigma_P=\sqrt{w^2\sigma^2+(1-w)^2\sigma^2+2w(1-w)\rho\sigma^2}
wweight in strategy A (the rest, 1−w, sits in strategy B)
σeach strategy's own volatility (assumed equal here, for a clean comparison)
ρcorrelation between the two strategies' returns — higher means more crowded, moving together

  2. In the diversification lab below, treat the two return series as two different systematic strategies rather than two asset classes, and drag correlation up toward 1: the blended line's swings grow closer to either strategy alone — the crowding effect, visualized.
Common traps
  • Neither approach is automatically superior — systematic removes bias but can miss context; discretionary can adapt but reopens the door to bias.
  • Assuming "systematic" automatically means "uncorrelated with other systematic strategies" — independently built rules can still converge on similar signals and become crowded.
  • Treating a systematic/discretionary blend ("quantamental") as automatically the worst — or best — of both; its outcome depends entirely on how and when the discretionary overlay is actually applied.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Rules-based quant funds vs. traditional active managers.
  • Documented episodes of correlated systematic strategies unwinding together over a short period (sometimes called a "quant crash"), commonly cited as historical examples of crowding risk — illustrative history, not investment advice.
  • "Quantamental" investment processes that combine systematic screens with a discretionary portfolio manager's final judgement.

Check yourself

1. What's the key difference between systematic and discretionary processes?
Systematic follows fixed rules consistently; discretionary allows judgement calls on top.
2. 10 overrides out of 50 decisions. Override rate? Separately, if two "independent" systematic funds' returns actually have ρ=0.95, how much diversification benefit should you expect from holding both?
20%; very little — at ρ close to 1 the blended portfolio's volatility sits close to either fund's own volatility, since they are effectively moving together.
3. Why can two systematic funds built by two completely separate teams still create concentrated risk for an investor holding both?
If their independently-built rules are similar enough, their positions correlate highly, so both funds can suffer losses at the same time — crowding, not shared management, is what creates the concentration.

Takeaways

  • Systematic and discretionary processes trade off consistency and adaptability.
  • Overrides are exactly where human judgement (and bias) re-enters.
  • "Systematic" does not mean "uncorrelated with other systematic strategies" — independently-built rules can crowd into the same trades and move together.

Overfitting risk

The questionA strategy has 20 tunable parameters fit on only 50 historical observations. Should that make you nervous — and if you tested 1,000 different parameter combinations instead of just one, would that make the best-looking one more trustworthy, or less?

Intuition

Overfitting happens when a model has enough free parameters relative to the data that it starts fitting noise rather than a real, repeatable pattern — the single biggest risk in quant strategy design, and a sharper version of the factor-model warning in Lesson 0.1.

Worked example 1 — observations per parameter

50 observations ÷ 20 parameters = 2.5 observations per parameter — far below commonly-cited rules of thumb suggesting at least 10 observations per parameter as a bare minimum.

Build it up: trying many combinations manufactures "winners" from nothing

There's a second, sneakier version of overfitting that has nothing to do with how many parameters a single model has: data snooping (or "multiple testing"). If you test enough different rules or parameter combinations against the same historical data, one of them will look great purely by chance — even if every single one of them has zero real skill. A standard statistical rule of thumb says that among N independent, equally noisy, no-skill trials, the expected best-looking result (in standard deviations above zero) grows roughly with √(2 ln N) — slowly, but it never stops growing as N grows.

Worked example 2 — the cost of a bigger search

√(2 ln 10) ≈ 2.15 "standard deviations" of apparent edge expected from the best of just 10 random, no-skill trials, purely by chance. √(2 ln 1,000) ≈ 3.72 — trying 100× more combinations doesn't multiply the apparent edge 100×, but it does manufacture a meaningfully stronger-looking "winner" out of pure noise, with no real skill anywhere in the search. (This is an illustrative approximation for the general shape of the effect, not an exact formula for any specific performance statistic.)

A third, closely related symptom: an overfit strategy typically shows a large gap between its in-sample and out-of-sample performance (Lesson 0.2) — a hallmark that the in-sample result was substantially noise.

Worked example 3 — in-sample vs. out-of-sample decay

A strategy shows an in-sample Sharpe ratio of 2.5 but only 0.3 once genuinely tested out-of-sample: 0.3 / 2.5 = 12% of the in-sample performance survived contact with new data — the rest was the model fitting the training period's own noise.

\text{obs. per parameter}=\frac{\text{observations}}{\text{parameters}}\qquad E[\text{best of }N]\approx\sqrt{2\ln N}
Nnumber of independent rules/parameter combinations tried against the same data
lnthe natural logarithm

Common traps
  • A strategy with many parameters can look spectacular in-sample precisely because it's fitting noise, not signal.
  • Judging a strategy by only its best historical configuration after trying many variations — the "best of many tries" is expected to look good from chance alone, independent of any real skill.
  • Treating a strategy's in-sample Sharpe ratio as a forecast of its future Sharpe ratio — a wide in-sample/out-of-sample gap is a specific, checkable warning sign of overfitting.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • The "more parameters than data can support" trap in strategy design, closely related to Lesson 0.2's backtesting discipline.
  • Data-snooping / multiple-testing concerns that quantitative researchers specifically try to guard against (e.g. holding out data no parameter combination was ever allowed to touch) before trusting a historical "best" rule.
  • Published academic "anomalies" and factor premiums that have shrunk or disappeared after publication — a widely discussed, illustrative case of apparent historical edges not fully surviving out-of-sample scrutiny.

Check yourself

1. What is overfitting, in both its "too many parameters" and "too many trials" forms?
A model (or search process) fitting noise rather than a real, repeatable pattern — either because it has too many free parameters relative to its data, or because many rules/combinations were tried and the best-looking one was kept.
2. 200 observations, 10 parameters. Observations per parameter? Separately, roughly how many "standard deviations" of apparent edge would you expect purely by chance from the best of 100 no-skill trials?
20; √(2 ln 100) ≈ 3.03.
3. Does a great in-sample result rule out overfitting? What specific follow-up check would help tell the difference?
No — overfitting specifically produces great-looking in-sample results; comparing in-sample to genuinely out-of-sample performance (Lesson 0.2) is the direct check, since a large gap is the classic warning sign.

Takeaways

  • Overfitting is the central risk of the whole quant field, and it shows up both as "too many parameters" and "too many trials."
  • Too many parameters relative to data, and testing too many combinations against the same data, are two distinct but related mechanisms for manufacturing a fake "winner."
  • A wide gap between in-sample and out-of-sample performance is overfitting's clearest symptom — treat it as a required check, not an afterthought.
