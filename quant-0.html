Factor models, briefly

The questionCan a stock's expected return be broken into pieces tied to measurable characteristics, rather than treated as one unexplained number?

Intuition

A factor model expresses expected return as a base rate plus exposures to shared, measurable characteristics (factors) — the same idea introduced less formally in Portfolio Management's factor-investing lesson, now treated as a tool for building systematic signals.

Worked example

E[R] = 2% + 0.08×(factor 1 score) + 0.05×(factor 2 score). With scores of 10 and 6: 2 + 0.8 + 0.3 = 3.1% over the base rate's own period.

E[R]=a+b_1x_1+b_2x_2
x₁, x₂factor exposure scores
b₁, b₂how much each factor contributes
Try this
  1. Raise a factor's coefficient: its contribution to expected return grows proportionally to its score.
Common traps
  • A factor model fits historical data by construction — it doesn't guarantee the same relationship holds going forward.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Multi-factor risk models used by quantitative funds.

Check yourself

1. What does a factor model add to a simple base rate?
Exposures to shared, measurable characteristics (factors), each with its own coefficient.
2. a=1, b1=0.1, x1=5. E[R] from this one term plus a?
1 + 0.1×5 = 1.5%.
3. Does a factor model guarantee future performance?
No — it's fit to historical data and may not hold going forward.

Takeaways

  • Factor models decompose expected return into measurable exposures.
  • Fit to the past doesn't guarantee the future.

Backtesting basics

The questionYou test a trading rule on 10 years of data and it looks great. Is that proof it will work going forward?

Intuition

Backtesting splits data into an in-sample period (used to build/tune the rule) and an out-of-sample period (used to test it, untouched during development). A rule that only looks good in-sample may simply be fit to that specific data's noise.

Worked example

10 years of data split 70/30: 7 years in-sample, 3 years out-of-sample = 30% of the data reserved purely for honest testing.

\text{out-of-sample years}=\text{total years}\times(1-\text{in-sample fraction})
Try this
  1. Raise the in-sample fraction toward 100%: almost no data is left for an honest, untouched test.
Common traps
  • Peeking at out-of-sample data while still tuning the rule quietly turns it into in-sample data.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • A strategy that looked great in-sample but failed once tested out-of-sample.

Check yourself

1. What's the purpose of an out-of-sample period?
An honest test on data the rule wasn't built or tuned on.
2. 20 years of data, 80% in-sample. Out-of-sample years?
4 years.
3. What happens if you tune a rule using out-of-sample data?
It stops being a true out-of-sample test — it's effectively become in-sample.

Takeaways

  • Backtesting needs a genuinely untouched out-of-sample period.
  • In-sample success alone is not proof of a real strategy.

Signal construction

The questionA stock scores 85 on some measure, with a universe average of 70 and a standard deviation of 10. How "extreme" is that, in a comparable unit?

Intuition

A z-score normalises a raw measure into "how many standard deviations from the average" — using the same standard-deviation idea from Investment Fundamentals Lesson 0.5 — so very different measures (valuation, momentum, quality) can be compared and combined on one common scale.

Worked example

(85 − 70)/10 = 1.5 — one and a half standard deviations above average.

z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}
xthe raw measure
μ, σthe universe's average and standard deviation
Try this
  1. Set x equal to the average: z becomes 0 — exactly typical.
Common traps
  • A z-score depends on the comparison universe chosen — the same raw value can have different z-scores in different universes.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Ranking stocks by a standardised valuation or momentum score.

Check yourself

1. What does a z-score measure?
How many standard deviations a value is from the average.
2. x=60, μ=50, σ=5. Z-score?
2.
3. Can the same raw value have different z-scores?
Yes — it depends on the comparison universe's average and standard deviation.

Takeaways

  • Z-scores put different measures on one comparable scale.
  • They depend on the chosen comparison universe.

Systematic vs. discretionary

The questionTwo managers both use valuation and momentum signals. One follows them mechanically every time; the other uses them as one input among several judgement calls. Same strategy?

Intuition

A systematic process follows fixed, pre-defined rules consistently; a discretionary one leaves room for human judgement on top of (or instead of) the rules. This is really the same process-vs-impulse theme from Behavioral Finance's debiasing lesson, applied at the level of an entire investment process.

Worked example

If a systematic process follows its rules on 100% of decisions and a discretionary one overrides the rule-based signal on, say, 30% of decisions, that 30% is exactly where human judgement (and human bias) can enter.

\text{override rate}=\frac{\text{decisions overriding the rule}}{\text{total decisions}}
Try this
  1. Push the override rate to 0%: the process becomes fully systematic by this measure.
Common traps
  • Neither approach is automatically superior — systematic removes bias but can miss context; discretionary can adapt but reopens the door to bias.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Rules-based quant funds vs. traditional active managers.

Check yourself

1. What's the key difference between systematic and discretionary processes?
Systematic follows fixed rules consistently; discretionary allows judgement calls on top.
2. 10 overrides out of 50 decisions. Override rate?
20%.
3. Is one approach automatically better than the other?
No — each has trade-offs between bias-resistance and contextual adaptability.

Takeaways

  • Systematic and discretionary processes trade off consistency and adaptability.
  • Overrides are exactly where human judgement (and bias) re-enters.

Overfitting risk

The questionA strategy has 20 tunable parameters fit on only 50 historical observations. Should that make you nervous?

Intuition

Overfitting happens when a model has enough free parameters relative to the data that it starts fitting noise rather than a real, repeatable pattern — the single biggest risk in quant strategy design, and a sharper version of the factor-model warning in Lesson 0.1.

Worked example

50 observations ÷ 20 parameters = 2.5 observations per parameter — far below commonly-cited rules of thumb suggesting at least 10 observations per parameter as a bare minimum.

\text{obs. per parameter}=\frac{\text{observations}}{\text{parameters}}
Try this
  1. Cut the parameter count while holding observations fixed: observations-per-parameter rises — a less overfitting-prone setup.
Common traps
  • A strategy with many parameters can look spectacular in-sample precisely because it's fitting noise, not signal.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • The "more parameters than data can support" trap in strategy design, closely related to Lesson 0.2's backtesting discipline.

Check yourself

1. What is overfitting?
A model fitting noise in the data rather than a real, repeatable pattern, usually from too many free parameters relative to data.
2. 200 observations, 10 parameters. Observations per parameter?
20.
3. Does a great in-sample result rule out overfitting?
No — overfitting specifically produces great-looking in-sample results.

Takeaways

  • Overfitting is the central risk of the whole quant field.
  • Too many parameters relative to data is the classic warning sign.
