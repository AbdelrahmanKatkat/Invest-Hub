Reading a cash flow statement

The questionA company reports $100,000 of net income, $150,000 of operating cash flow — and still ends the year with less cash in the bank than it started, because it spent heavily building a new factory and paid down debt. How can profit, operating cash flow, and the actual cash balance all move in different directions at once?

Intuition

The cash flow statement is the one core financial statement (alongside the balance sheet and income statement, Investment Fundamentals Lessons 3.1–3.2) that tracks actual cash moving in and out, not accounting profit. It splits every cash movement into three buckets:

  • Operating cash flow (OCF) — cash generated by the core business: collections from customers minus payments to suppliers, employees and taxes.
  • Investing cash flow — cash spent on or raised from long-term assets: buying equipment or a factory (an outflow), or selling off a division (an inflow).
  • Financing cash flow — cash from or to lenders and shareholders: borrowing or repaying debt, issuing or buying back stock, paying dividends.

Net income (from the income statement) includes non-cash items like depreciation — an accounting expense that spreads an asset's cost over its useful life, with no matching cash outflow in the period it's recorded. That's why operating cash flow starts from net income and adds depreciation back.

Worked example 1 — from net income to operating cash flow

Net income $100,000 + depreciation add-back $60,000 − working-capital increase $10,000 = $150,000 operating cash flow.

Build it up: reconciling the whole year's cash, and free cash flow

The three sections don't just describe cash flow in the abstract — added together, they explain exactly why the cash balance on the balance sheet changed from the start of the year to the end:

\text{Ending cash}=\text{Starting cash}+\text{OCF}+\text{Investing CF}+\text{Financing CF}

This is exactly how a profitable, cash-generative company can still end a year with less cash than it started: strong operating cash flow can be outweighed by heavy investing outflows (building that new factory) and financing outflows (paying down debt) — none of which reduce net income the way depreciation does. A closely related figure investors track is free cash flow (FCF): operating cash flow left over after the capital expenditure (capex) needed to maintain and grow the business.

\text{FCF}=\text{OCF}-\text{CapEx}

FCF matters because it's the cash genuinely available to pay dividends, buy back shares, or pay down debt once the business has reinvested in itself — a number net income alone never shows.

Worked example 2 — the full year's cash, and free cash flow

Starting cash $200,000 + operating CF $150,000 − capital expenditure $80,000 (investing) − debt repayment $30,000 (financing) = $240,000 ending cash — still higher than it started, but only after a healthy operating cash flow absorbed two real outflows. Free cash flow = $150,000 operating CF − $80,000 capex = $70,000, the cash left over before any financing moves at all.

OCFoperating cash flow — cash generated by day-to-day business activity
CapExcapital expenditure — cash spent on long-term assets like equipment or buildings
FCFfree cash flow — OCF minus CapEx; cash available after reinvesting in the business
Try this
  1. Raise depreciation in the first calculator: operating cash flow rises above net income further.
  2. In the cash-reconciliation calculator, make capex larger than operating cash flow: ending cash falls below starting cash even though the business is profitable and cash-generative at the operating level.
Common traps
  • A profitable company can still run short of actual cash if profit isn't converting to cash — or if it's spending heavily on growth and debt repayment.
  • A positive investing cash flow isn't automatically good news — it can mean a company is selling off assets rather than investing in growth.
  • Treating capex as always optional — maintenance capex (keeping existing operations running) is very different from growth capex (expanding the business), and cutting the wrong one can starve it.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Analysts comparing net income to operating cash flow as a quality-of-earnings check.
  • Capital-intensive businesses — airlines, telecom network operators, oil & gas producers — reporting heavy investing outflows and sometimes negative free cash flow during build-out phases, without that implying operating weakness.
  • Dividend sustainability assessed against free cash flow coverage, since dividends are ultimately paid from cash, not reported profit.

Check yourself

1. Name the three sections of a cash flow statement, and what each is about.
Operating (core business cash), investing (long-term asset purchases/sales), financing (debt, equity and dividend flows).
2. Net income $50,000, depreciation $20,000, no working-capital change. Operating CF?
$70,000.
3. A company has $150,000 operating cash flow and spends $200,000 on capex. What's its free cash flow, and does that number alone prove something is wrong?
FCF = 150,000 − 200,000 = −$50,000. Not necessarily wrong — it depends on whether the capex is funding profitable growth or merely maintaining existing operations.

Takeaways

  • Operating cash flow can differ from net income due to non-cash items like depreciation.
  • The three sections reconcile exactly to the change in cash on the balance sheet.
  • Free cash flow — OCF minus capex — is the cash genuinely available after the business reinvests in itself.

Ratio analysis

The questionA company earns $80,000 on $400,000 of equity and $1,000,000 of total assets. A near-identical competitor earns the same $80,000 on the same $1,000,000 of assets, but only $200,000 of equity. Both have the same ROA — so why does the second company's ROE look so much stronger, and should that impress an investor?

Intuition

Return on equity (ROE) measures profit relative to owners' capital; return on assets (ROA) measures it relative to everything the company controls, debt included. Two companies can have identical ROA but very different ROE purely because one funds the same pool of assets with much more borrowed money — exactly the case above.

Worked example 1

Company A: ROE = 80,000/400,000 = 20%; ROA = 80,000/1,000,000 = 8%. Company B: same net income and assets, but equity of only $200,000, so ROE = 80,000/200,000 = 40% — double Company A's ROE on identical ROA, purely from using more debt to fund the same assets.

ROE=\frac{\text{Net income}}{\text{Equity}}\qquad ROA=\frac{\text{Net income}}{\text{Total assets}}

Build it up: the DuPont decomposition

A headline ROE number alone doesn't say whether it reflects genuine operating skill or just leverage. The DuPont decomposition splits ROE into three multiplied pieces, each readable straight off the income statement and balance sheet (Investment Fundamentals Lessons 3.1–3.2):

ROE=\underbrace{\frac{\text{Net income}}{\text{Revenue}}}_{\text{net margin}}\times\underbrace{\frac{\text{Revenue}}{\text{Assets}}}_{\text{asset turnover}}\times\underbrace{\frac{\text{Assets}}{\text{Equity}}}_{\text{equity multiplier (leverage)}}

Net margin captures pure profitability per dollar of sales; asset turnover captures how efficiently assets are used to generate sales; the equity multiplier captures how much leverage is amplifying the result. The same ROE can come from a highly profitable, low-leverage business or a thin-margin, heavily-leveraged one — very different risk profiles behind an identical headline number.

Worked example 2 — DuPont decomposition

Revenue $1,000,000, net income $80,000, assets $1,000,000, equity $400,000 (Company A above): net margin = 80,000/1,000,000 = 8%; asset turnover = 1,000,000/1,000,000 = 1.0×; equity multiplier = 1,000,000/400,000 = 2.5×. ROE = 8% × 1.0 × 2.5 = 20% — the same answer as the direct calculation, but now visibly built from an average margin and average efficiency amplified by real leverage, not an exceptionally efficient business.

net marginnet income ÷ revenue — profitability per dollar of sales
asset turnoverrevenue ÷ assets — how efficiently assets generate sales
equity multiplierassets ÷ equity — how much leverage is funding those assets
Try this
  1. Raise total assets with equity fixed: ROA falls, but ROE stays the same — more debt-financed assets, same equity profitability.
  2. In the DuPont calculator, lower net margin while raising the equity multiplier to hold ROE roughly constant — see that very different businesses (high margin/low leverage vs. low margin/high leverage) can land on the same ROE.
Common traps
  • A high ROE driven mainly by heavy debt is a different (riskier) story than one driven by genuine operating efficiency.
  • Comparing ROE across industries with structurally different normal leverage — banks and utilities typically run far more leverage than software companies — without adjusting for that.
  • Share buybacks shrink equity mechanically, which raises ROE on its own even with no operational improvement at all.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • ROE commonly compared across companies in the same industry.
  • DuPont decomposition used by analysts to diagnose exactly what's driving a change in ROE year over year.
  • Return on invested capital (ROIC), a related measure that adjusts for capital structure, used alongside ROE especially for capital-intensive sectors.

Check yourself

1. Net income $60,000, equity $300,000. ROE?
20%.
2. Why might ROE be high while ROA is only modest?
Heavy use of debt can inflate ROE relative to ROA, which reflects all assets regardless of financing.
3. Using DuPont, net margin 10%, asset turnover 0.8×, equity multiplier 3×. What's ROE, and what does the multiplier tell you?
ROE = 10% × 0.8 × 3 = 24%. The multiplier of 3× shows a meaningful chunk of that return is leverage, not just margin and efficiency.

Takeaways

  • ROE and ROA answer related but different questions.
  • A gap between them can signal heavy leverage.
  • DuPont splits ROE into margin, turnover and leverage, showing which driver explains a company's return.

Economic moats

The questionTwo companies both earn high profits today. One can raise prices 10% with little customer loss; the other loses customers at a 2% price rise. Which has the wider moat — and can that advantage actually be put into a number, rather than just asserted?

Intuition

An economic moat is a durable advantage that protects long-run profits from competition. Common sources, each defending profit in a different way:

  • Brand — customers pay more for a trusted name even when a cheaper substitute performs identically.
  • Network effects — a product gets more valuable as more people use it, making it hard for a smaller rival to match on value alone.
  • Switching costs — changing suppliers is expensive, risky or time-consuming, so customers stay even if a rival is marginally cheaper.
  • Scale / cost advantage — size lets a company spread fixed costs over more volume, underpricing smaller rivals without sacrificing margin.

The company that can raise prices without losing much business has pricing power — a classic sign of a moat, whatever its specific source.

Worked example 1

If a 10% price rise costs almost no customers, the implied "customer loss per 1% price rise" is near 0; at the other company it's roughly 2% lost per 1% price rise — a rough, illustrative way to compare pricing power.

\text{price sensitivity}\approx\frac{\%\ \text{customers lost}}{\%\ \text{price increase}}

Build it up: quantifying switching costs with customer lifetime value

Pricing power is one observable clue to a moat, but a switching-cost moat specifically shows up in how long and how profitably a company keeps its customers. Customer lifetime value (LTV) estimates the total profit a company expects from one customer over the whole relationship:

\text{LTV}=\frac{\text{Annual revenue per customer}\times\text{Gross margin}}{\text{Annual churn rate}}

A low churn rate (the fraction of customers who leave each year) is exactly what a genuine switching-cost moat produces — and it mechanically multiplies LTV, since a shrinking denominator makes the whole fraction larger.

Worked example 2 — switching costs and lifetime value

$100/year revenue per customer, 70% gross margin, 10% annual churn: LTV = (100 × 0.70) / 0.10 = $700. If switching became easier and churn rose to 25% with nothing else changed: LTV = (100 × 0.70) / 0.25 = $280 — a 60% drop in lifetime value purely from weaker switching costs, with pricing and margin untouched.

LTVcustomer lifetime value — expected total profit from one customer relationship
churn ratethe fraction of customers who stop buying each year
Try this
  1. Raise customer loss for the same price increase: sensitivity rises — weaker pricing power, a narrower moat.
  2. In the lifetime-value calculator, raise churn while holding revenue and margin fixed: LTV falls sharply — a direct, quantifiable cost of a weakening moat.
Common traps
  • A moat can narrow over time — past pricing power doesn't guarantee future pricing power.
  • Assuming high current profits alone prove a moat — a company facing imminent regulation or a disruptive new competitor can show strong profits right before the moat erodes.
  • Treating pricing power or LTV as proof rather than a proxy — they're observable clues to a moat's strength, not a guaranteed measurement of its durability.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Companies widely cited for strong brand, network-effect, or switching-cost moats.
  • Subscription software and similar high-switching-cost business models frequently discussed in terms of churn and lifetime value on earnings calls.
  • "Quality" or "wide-moat" investing strategies that explicitly screen for durable competitive advantages (Investment Strategies path).

Check yourself

1. Name a type of economic moat.
Any of: brand, network effect, switching costs, scale.
2. Which is a sign of pricing power: losing many customers at a small price rise, or losing few?
Losing few customers at a price rise signals stronger pricing power.
3. A subscription service cuts its annual churn from 15% to 5% through a bundled contract, with revenue per customer and margin unchanged. By what multiple does lifetime value change?
LTV scales with 1/churn, so the ratio is 15/5 = 3× — lifetime value roughly triples.

Takeaways

  • A moat is a durable competitive advantage, with several distinct common sources.
  • Pricing power is one observable clue to a moat's strength.
  • Customer lifetime value quantifies switching-cost moats directly, through the churn rate.

Red flags in financials

The questionA company's revenue grows 10% this year, but its accounts receivable (money owed by customers) grows 40%. Should that raise an eyebrow — and how would you track whether it's a one-year blip or a worsening pattern?

Intuition

A red flag is a warning sign that invites closer scrutiny, not an automatic verdict. Common ones include:

  • Receivables growing much faster than revenue — a potential sign of sales being booked before cash is actually collected.
  • Recurring "one-time" or "non-recurring" charges that show up again every year — if it keeps happening, it isn't really one-time.
  • Profit and operating cash flow trending in opposite directions over several periods (Lesson 0.1) — a persistent, widening gap is more concerning than a single year's blip.
Worked example 1

Receivables growth (40%) ÷ revenue growth (10%) = 4× — receivables are growing four times faster than sales, worth investigating.

\text{red-flag ratio}=\frac{\%\ \text{growth in receivables}}{\%\ \text{growth in revenue}}

Build it up: turning a one-off ratio into a trend — Days Sales Outstanding

The receivables-to-revenue growth ratio is a useful quick check, but it only compares one year's growth rates. Days Sales Outstanding (DSO) converts receivables into a more intuitive, trackable number: roughly how many days of sales are currently sitting uncollected.

\text{DSO}=\frac{\text{Accounts receivable}}{\text{Revenue}}\times 365

Tracking DSO across several years turns a single red flag into a trend — a rising DSO means collections are genuinely slowing down, even when year-to-year growth-rate comparisons look noisy.

Worked example 2 — DSO rising over two years

Year 1: receivables $100,000, revenue $1,000,000 → DSO = (100,000/1,000,000) × 365 = 36.5 days. Year 2: receivables $150,000 (+50%), revenue $1,100,000 (+10%) → DSO = (150,000/1,100,000) × 365 ≈ 49.8 days — collections have slowed by roughly 13 days, confirming the same red flag the simple growth-rate ratio (50%/10% = 5×) already hinted at, now in a unit (days) that's directly comparable across years and companies.

DSODays Sales Outstanding — average days of revenue currently sitting uncollected in receivables
Try this
  1. Bring receivables growth down toward revenue growth: the ratio approaches 1 — a less worrying pattern.
  2. In the DSO calculator, raise receivables while holding revenue fixed: DSO rises — collections are taking longer relative to sales.
Common traps
  • One red flag isn't proof of a problem — it's a prompt to look closer, not an automatic conclusion.
  • Comparing DSO across companies with very different payment terms or industries (e.g. a company selling on 90-day corporate terms vs. a retailer collecting cash immediately) without adjusting for that difference.
  • Looking only at receivables growth in dollar terms instead of converting to DSO — a growing company can show rising receivables that are entirely healthy if DSO itself is flat.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Analysts flagging aggressive revenue recognition during due diligence.
  • DSO trend-watching alongside revenue growth as a standard part of quality-of-earnings analysis.
  • Recurring "non-recurring" charges and aggressive accounting flagged in short-seller reports and auditor commentary.

Check yourself

1. What might fast-growing receivables relative to revenue suggest?
Sales being booked before cash is actually collected.
2. Revenue +8%, receivables +32%. Ratio?
32/8 = 4×.
3. Receivables $80,000, revenue $800,000 — what's DSO? If revenue then grows to $880,000 with receivables unchanged at $80,000, what happens to DSO and why is that reassuring?
DSO = (80,000/800,000) × 365 = 36.5 days. At $880,000 revenue: (80,000/880,000) × 365 ≈ 33.2 days — DSO falls because revenue grew faster than receivables, meaning collections are actually keeping pace or improving, not worsening.

Takeaways

  • Red flags are prompts to investigate, not verdicts.
  • Comparing growth rates across statement lines can reveal mismatches.
  • DSO converts receivables into a comparable, trackable trend rather than a single noisy ratio.

Qualitative analysis

The questionTwo companies have near-identical financial ratios, and both appear to have moats of similar strength on paper (Lesson 0.3). Can the numbers alone tell you which is the better investment — or does something else have to break the tie?

Intuition

Numbers describe the past; qualitative analysis helps judge whether they're likely to hold up or improve. Three commonly weighed factors:

  • Management track record — has leadership allocated capital well and kept past promises, or repeatedly missed targets?
  • Industry position — is the company a clear leader, a fast follower, or a struggling also-ran in its space?
  • Governance — is the board independent enough to hold management accountable, and are incentives aligned with long-term shareholders rather than short-term stock moves?

The best fundamental analysis combines both the numbers and this kind of judgement.

Worked example 1

A simple illustrative "confidence score": starting from a 50% baseline, add 10 points for a strong management track record, add 10 for a leading industry position, subtract 15 for weak governance — net score moves with the qualitative inputs, not with any new financial ratio.

\text{score}=\text{base}+\sum(\text{qualitative adjustments})

Build it up: blending the quantitative and qualitative pictures

In practice, analysts don't treat qualitative judgement and the financial ratios (Lessons 0.1–0.4) as two separate, disconnected exercises — they blend them into one overall view, explicitly weighting how much each contributes:

\text{Overall score}=w\times\text{Quantitative score}+(1-w)\times\text{Qualitative score}

The weight w is itself a judgement call: an analyst confident in clean, comparable financial data might weight the quantitative score heavily; one analyzing an early-stage or fast-changing business might lean more on qualitative judgement, since the numbers alone say less about where the company is headed.

Worked example 2 — a blended score

A quantitative score of 75 (out of 100, built from ratios like ROE and the red-flag checks of Lessons 0.2 and 0.4) and a qualitative score of 65 (management, industry position, governance), weighted 60% quantitative / 40% qualitative: overall = 0.60 × 75 + 0.40 × 65 = 71 — between the two inputs, pulled closer to whichever one carries more weight.

wthe weight placed on the quantitative score, between 0 and 1 (1 − w goes to the qualitative score)
Try this
  1. Push the governance adjustment very negative in the first calculator: the overall score falls even if nothing in the financials changed.
  2. In the blended-score calculator, push the weight toward 100%: the overall score converges on the quantitative score alone, and qualitative judgement stops mattering at all.
Common traps
  • Qualitative judgement is inherently more subjective than a ratio — it should supplement, not replace, the numbers.
  • Treating the blend weight as fixed or objective, when it's actually a judgement call that should vary with how reliable the available numbers are.
  • Letting a compelling qualitative story (charismatic management, a trendy industry narrative) override clear warning signs already flagged by the ratios (Lessons 0.2, 0.4).
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Management track record and strategy routinely discussed on earnings calls.
  • Governance ratings and board-independence disclosures referenced by institutional investors.
  • "Quality" investment strategies that explicitly combine financial criteria with qualitative moat and management assessments (Investment Strategies path), rather than ranking on ratios alone.

Check yourself

1. Name a qualitative factor in fundamental analysis.
Any of: management quality, industry position, governance.
2. Can two companies with identical ratios be different investments?
Yes — qualitative factors can differ even when the numbers look alike.
3. Quantitative score 80, qualitative score 50, weighted 50/50 — overall score? If the weight shifts to 80% quantitative, does the overall score rise or fall, and why?
At 50/50: 0.5×80 + 0.5×50 = 65. At 80% quantitative: 0.8×80 + 0.2×50 = 74 — it rises, because more weight shifts onto the higher of the two inputs (80 vs. 50).

Takeaways

  • Numbers and qualitative judgement both matter.
  • Identical ratios can still hide very different businesses.
  • Blending the two via an explicit weight makes the trade-off transparent rather than an unstated gut feeling.
