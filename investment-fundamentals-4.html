4.1

Asset allocation models

The questionHow much of a portfolio "should" be in stocks versus bonds at age 30 versus age 60?

Intuition

Simple rules of thumb translate time horizon into an allocation: a longer horizon can better absorb the volatility from Lesson 0.5, since there's more time to recover from a downturn. One widely-cited (but rough) rule: stock % = 110 − age. These rules are starting points, not personalized advice — actual risk tolerance (0.6), goals and other income sources matter too.

Worked example

At age 30: 110 − 30 = 80% stocks, 20% bonds/cash. At age 60: 110 − 60 = 50% stocks, 50% bonds/cash — a steadily more conservative mix over time.

The formal definition

\text{stock \%}=\max(0,\min(100,\,110-\text{age}))
agethe investor's current age
max/minkeep the result sensibly between 0% and 100%
Try this
  1. Slide age from 20 to 80: the suggested stock percentage steadily declines.
Common traps
  • These are rough heuristics, not personalized financial advice — they ignore actual risk tolerance and circumstances.
  • Many variants exist (100−age, 120−age); none is uniquely "correct".
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Target-date retirement funds roughly follow an age-based glide path like this, automatically becoming more conservative over time.

Check yourself

1. Using 110−age, what's the suggested stock % at age 45?
110 − 45 = 65%.
2. Why does a longer horizon typically support a higher stock allocation?
There's more time to recover from the volatility of stocks (Lesson 0.5) before the money is needed.
3. Is 110−age a personalized recommendation?
No — it's a rough starting heuristic; actual risk tolerance, goals and circumstances should adjust it.

Takeaways

  • Age/horizon-based rules are simple starting points, not laws.
  • Longer horizons can typically absorb more volatility.
  • Personal risk tolerance and goals should adjust any generic rule.
4.2

Rebalancing

The questionYou start at a 60/40 stocks/bonds split. Stocks then rise 20% while bonds stay flat. Are you still 60/40?

Intuition

Price moves cause a portfolio to drift away from its target weights automatically, as winners grow to a larger share. Rebalancing — selling some of what grew and buying what lagged — restores the target mix. It also mechanically enforces "sell (relatively) high, buy (relatively) low", the opposite of the instinct described in Lesson 0.9.

Worked example

Start: $60,000 stocks, $40,000 bonds. Stocks rise 20% → $72,000; bonds stay at $40,000; total $112,000. New weights: stocks 72,000/112,000 ≈ 64.3%, bonds ≈ 35.7% — drifted from the 60/40 target. To rebalance back to 60%, sell 72,000 − (0.60 × 112,000) = $4,800 of stocks and move it into bonds.

The formal definition

\text{drifted weight}=\frac{\text{component value}}{\text{total value}}\qquad\qquad \text{trade}=\text{current value}-(\text{target weight}\times\text{total value})
component valuethe current dollar value of one holding (e.g. stocks)
target weightthe originally chosen allocation percentage
Try this
  1. Confirm the drifted weights and trade amount match the worked example.
  2. Raise the stock return further: drift and the rebalancing trade both grow.
Common traps
  • Rebalancing isn't free — trading costs and, in taxable accounts, potential taxes are real considerations.
  • Rebalancing on every tiny move can cost more than the drift it corrects; most plans use a schedule or a drift threshold instead.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Target-date funds rebalance automatically on an internal schedule.
  • "Calendar" (e.g. annual) and "threshold" (e.g. rebalance if drift exceeds 5%) are two common rebalancing policies.

Check yourself

1. Why does a portfolio drift from its target weights over time?
Different holdings grow at different rates, so the faster-growing ones become a larger share of the total.
2. Start $50,000 stocks/$50,000 bonds (50/50); stocks rise 30%, bonds flat. New stock weight?
Stocks = 65,000; total = 115,000; weight = 65,000/115,000 ≈ 56.5%.
3. Name one reason not to rebalance after every tiny price move.
Trading costs (and potential taxes) can exceed the benefit of correcting a very small drift.

Takeaways

  • Prices moving at different rates causes portfolio drift.
  • Rebalancing restores the target mix and enforces a disciplined "sell high, buy low" pattern.
  • Rebalancing has real costs, so it's usually done on a schedule or threshold, not constantly.
4.3

Dollar-cost averaging

The questionYou invest a fixed $100 every month for 3 months, at prices of $10, $8 and $12. Does investing the same dollar amount each time, regardless of price, help or hurt?

Intuition

Dollar-cost averaging (DCA) invests a fixed amount on a fixed schedule. Because a fixed dollar amount buys more shares when the price is low and fewer when it's high, the average cost per share can end up below the simple average of the prices paid. DCA's bigger benefit may be behavioural: it removes the "when should I invest?" decision that Lesson 0.9's biases tend to answer badly — but it is not a guarantee against loss, and simply investing a lump sum immediately has often outperformed spreading it out, in periods when markets trended upward.

Worked example

$100 at $10 → 10 shares; $100 at $8 → 12.5 shares; $100 at $12 → 8.33 shares. Total: 30.83 shares for $300 invested, average cost = 300/30.83 ≈ $9.73/share — below the simple average price of the three, ($10 + $8 + $12)/3 = $10, because more shares were bought during the $8 dip.

The formal definition

\text{Average cost}=\frac{\sum_i \text{investment}_i}{\sum_i \left(\text{investment}_i/\text{price}_i\right)}
investmentᵢthe fixed amount invested in period i
priceᵢthe price paid in period i
Try this
  1. Make all three prices equal: average cost equals that price exactly, and matches the simple average.
  2. Make one price much lower than the others: average cost drops further below the simple average.
Common traps
  • DCA doesn't protect against a sustained decline — it changes the entry price pattern, not whether the underlying investment does well.
  • Holding cash on the sidelines specifically to "DCA in" has often underperformed investing it immediately, in periods when markets trended upward.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Automatic monthly contributions to a retirement account are a common real-world form of DCA.

Check yourself

1. Does DCA buy more or fewer shares when the price is low?
More — the same fixed dollar amount buys more shares at a lower price.
2. $100/month for 2 months at $5 and $20. Average cost per share?
Shares = 100/5 + 100/20 = 20 + 5 = 25; average cost = 200/25 = $8.
3. Does DCA guarantee a better outcome than investing a lump sum immediately?
No — in periods when markets trend upward, investing immediately has often outperformed spreading contributions out, since DCA delays some money getting invested.

Takeaways

  • DCA buys more shares when price is low, fewer when it's high, automatically.
  • Its main behavioural benefit is removing a hard, bias-prone timing decision.
  • It is not a guarantee against loss or a guaranteed outperformer versus a lump sum.
4.4

Taxes & fees basics

The questionBeyond the expense ratio from Lesson 2.7, what else quietly drags on long-term returns?

Intuition

Taxes are a second, separate drag alongside fees. In a taxable account, dividends and realized capital gains can be taxed along the way; a tax-advantaged retirement account defers or reduces that drag, letting more of the return compound (Lesson 0.1) with less friction. Expense ratios and tax drag stack on top of each other — both matter, and both compound.

Worked example

$50,000 at a 7% gross return for 25 years: at a 0.03% fee (net 6.97%) it grows to ≈ $269,476; at a 1% fee (net 6%) it grows to ≈ $214,594 — a $54,882 gap from fees alone, before even considering tax treatment.

The formal definition

FV=P\,(1+r_{\text{gross}}-\text{fee})^{t}
r_grossreturn before fees
feethe annual expense ratio (a stand-in for any recurring drag)
Try this
  1. Confirm the two ending values and the gap match the worked example at the defaults.
  2. Push years further out: the dollar gap keeps growing faster than years do.
Common traps
  • Fees and taxes each "feel small" year to year, which is exactly why their compounded effect is easy to underestimate.
  • Tax treatment varies by account type and is not identical everywhere — this lesson stays general rather than jurisdiction-specific.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Tax-advantaged retirement accounts versus ordinary taxable brokerage accounts is a foundational account-type decision.
  • Every fund discloses its expense ratio in its prospectus or fact sheet.

Check yourself

1. Name the two drags on long-term returns discussed here.
Fees (expense ratios) and taxes.
2. Why can a tax-advantaged account meaningfully help long-term compounding?
By deferring or reducing tax drag along the way, more of the return stays invested and compounds.
3. Does a lower fee always fully offset less favorable tax treatment, or vice versa?
Not necessarily — both are separate drags, and both should be considered together rather than assuming one compensates for the other.

Takeaways

  • Taxes and fees are two separate, compounding drags on returns.
  • Tax-advantaged accounts can reduce tax drag specifically.
  • Small annual percentages become large dollar amounts over decades.
4.5

Capstone: build and defend a portfolio

The questionAge 30, moderate risk tolerance, a 35-year horizon, $10,000 to start and $300/month ongoing. Using everything in this path, what's a simple, defensible first portfolio and plan?

Intuition

This lesson doesn't introduce new math — it combines what came before into one decision, the same way Lesson 0.10 combined Module 0: start from an age/horizon-based allocation (4.1), diversify across asset classes so it isn't one concentrated bet (0.7), keep costs low (2.7, 4.4), automate contributions (4.3), rebalance on a schedule (4.2), and write the plan down so it survives the biases from 0.9.

Worked example

Age 30 suggests roughly 80% stocks by the 110−age rule (4.1). A sample plan: 80% stocks, 15% bonds, 5% cash; $300/month auto-invested (4.3); reviewed and rebalanced once a year (4.2); low-cost index funds only (2.7). Set the lab below to 80/15/5/0 to see this mix's illustrative expected return and risk.

The formal definition

E[R_p]=\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i\,E[R_i]
wᵢthe weight placed in asset class i
E[Rᵢ]that class's expected return
Try this
  1. Set the sliders to roughly 80% Stocks, 15% Bonds, 5% Cash, 0% Alternatives (matching the age-based suggestion) and read off the expected return and illustrative risk.
  2. Compare that to a far more conservative 30/60/10/0 mix — notice both numbers fall together.
Common traps
  • Treating this one allocation as permanent, instead of a living plan to revisit as circumstances change.
  • Skipping the "write it down" step and relying on memory or mood during the next downturn (Lesson 0.9).
Where it shows up in real investing
  • A short written investment policy statement — the plan, the allocation, and the rules for changing it — is commonly recommended by financial professionals.

Check yourself

1. Name three ideas from this path that a finished portfolio plan should combine.
Any three of: an age/horizon-based starting allocation, diversification, low costs, automated contributions, a rebalancing schedule, and a written plan to defend against biases.
2. Why write the plan down rather than just "knowing" it?
A written plan is harder to abandon impulsively during a stressful market move than an unwritten intention.
3. Should a portfolio plan, once written, never change?
No — it should be revisited periodically as goals, horizon and risk tolerance change, on a schedule rather than in reaction to short-term market moves.

Takeaways

  • A finished portfolio plan combines allocation, diversification, cost, automation and discipline.
  • Writing the plan down is itself a defence against behavioural mistakes.
  • A plan should be revisited on a schedule, not abandoned in a moment of stress.
