Chart types

The questionA line chart, a bar chart and a candlestick chart can all show the same stock over the same days. But two very different trading days — one where price grinds quietly up to a close, another where it spikes sharply higher and then sells most of it back off before the close — can land on the exact same closing price and therefore plot as the exact same point on a line chart. What is the line chart hiding, and how would you recover it?

Intuition

Three chart types describe the same underlying trades with different amounts of detail:

  • A line chart plots just the closing price for each period, connected into a curve — clean and easy to read, but it keeps only one number per day.
  • A bar chart (OHLC bar) draws a vertical line from the low to the high, with a small tick left for the open and right for the close — four numbers per day instead of one.
  • A candlestick chart shows the same four numbers (open, high, low, close) as a "body" (the open-to-close box, typically shaded differently for an up day vs. a down day) with thin "wicks" above and below for the high and low.

Candlesticks and bars contain identical information — they are just two different drawing conventions for the same open/high/low/close (OHLC) data. A line chart, by contrast, genuinely throws information away: it cannot distinguish a day that opened at the close from a day that whipsawed wildly and only ended up near where it started.

Build it up: what a single candle encodes beyond "up or down"

A candle's shape carries more than its color. Two useful numbers, computed directly from the same four prices every candle already has:

  • Body = close − open. Positive means the period closed above where it opened (commonly shaded green/white); negative means it closed below (commonly shaded red/black).
  • Close location value (CLV) = where the close sits within that period's full high-to-low range, scaled from −1 (closed exactly at the low) to +1 (closed exactly at the high). A long wick with a close near one end tells a very different story from a close sitting in the middle of a wide range.
Worked example 1 — body and range

A day opens $48, dips to a low of $46, rises to a high of $51, and closes at $50. Body = 50 − 48 = +$2 (a modestly up day). Range = 51 − 46 = $5 — five times the net body, meaning the day did a lot of back-and-forth before settling only $2 higher. A line chart would show only the final "$50" point and miss that entire round trip.

Worked example 2 — same close, different story

Day A: open $48, high $51, low $46, close $50 → CLV = ((50−46)−(51−50))/(51−46) = (4−1)/5 = 0.6 (closed well into the upper part of its range — buyers were in control late in the day). Day B: open $52, high $54, low $47, close $50 → CLV = ((50−47)−(54−50))/(54−47) = (3−4)/7 ≈ −0.14 (closed slightly below the middle of its range, after being rejected on a push up to $54). Both days close at exactly $50 — identical on a line chart — yet Day A is a bullish candle with a strong close near its high, while Day B is a bearish candle that gave back most of an intraday rally.

\text{body}=\text{close}-\text{open}\qquad \text{CLV}=\frac{(\text{close}-\text{low})-(\text{high}-\text{close})}{\text{high}-\text{low}}
open, high, low, closethe four prices a single bar or candle records for one period
bodyclose − open; positive = up candle, negative = down candle
CLVclose location value, from −1 (closed at the low) to +1 (closed at the high)

  1. Picture plotting only the close each day for a week versus plotting the full daily range — notice how much intraday detail the line chart loses.
  2. In the CLV calculator below, keep close and open fixed but widen the gap between high and low — watch CLV shrink toward 0 as the same close becomes a smaller fraction of a now-much-wider range.
Common traps
  • A line chart can look deceptively smooth compared to what the price actually did intraday.
  • Two candles can share the exact same closing price — and therefore the exact same point on a line chart — while having very different bodies and CLVs; the close alone doesn't tell you how the price got there.
  • A long wick isn't automatically bullish or bearish on its own — it has to be read together with the candle's body direction and its place in the surrounding trend, not in isolation.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Candlestick charts on virtually every trading platform.
  • Close-location-value-style logic underlies popular volume/price indicators such as the Accumulation/Distribution line — the same idea reappears, applied to volume, in Lesson 0.4.
  • Named candle patterns like "hammer" or "shooting star" are defined specifically by where the close sits within the day's range — language that traces directly back to CLV.

Check yourself

1. A candle has open $40, high $46, low $38, close $44. What are its body, range, and CLV?
Body = 44 − 40 = +4. Range = 46 − 38 = 8. CLV = ((44−38)−(46−44))/(46−38) = (6−2)/8 = 0.5.
2. Day X ranges $70–$78 and closes at $77; Day Y ranges $70–$82 and closes at $72. Day Y's close is a lower price — but which day's CLV is actually higher, and what does that reveal?
Day X: CLV = ((77−70)−(78−77))/(78−70) = 6/8 = 0.75. Day Y: CLV = ((72−70)−(82−72))/(82−70) = −8/12 ≈ −0.67. Day X has the higher CLV even though its closing price is lower — it closed in the upper three-quarters of a narrower range, while Day Y surged to $82 and gave almost all of it back, closing in the lower third of a much wider range.
3. Why can a candlestick chart reveal something a line chart cannot, even over a sequence of days that end at the exact same closing prices?
A line chart only plots the close, discarding the open/high/low path for each day — and therefore the body and CLV information that shows how the price got there and who looked more in control by the close.

Takeaways

  • Line charts simplify to one number per period; bars and candlesticks keep all four (open, high, low, close).
  • A candle's body (close − open) and CLV (where the close sits in the day's range) encode information a line chart discards entirely.
  • Two days can close at an identical price yet tell opposite stories once the full range is examined.

Moving averages

The questionPrices over 5 days: 10, 12, 11, 13, 14. The simple 5-day average is 12 — but a trader instead computes a 3-day exponential moving average, which deliberately weights the most recent prices more heavily than older ones. Does that give the same answer, or does it tell a noticeably different, more current story?

Intuition

A moving average replaces day-to-day noise with the average of the last N periods, making the underlying trend easier to see:

  • A simple moving average (SMA) gives every one of the last N prices equal weight — today's close counts exactly as much as the close from N days ago.
  • A crossover (a shorter average crossing above or below a longer one) is a commonly watched, debated trend signal — short above long is often read as a strengthening uptrend, and vice versa.
  • Shorter averages track price more closely (and react faster to a new move); longer averages are smoother but slower to turn.
Worked example 1 — the simple average

(10+12+11+13+14)/5 = 60/5 = 12.

Build it up: weighting recent prices more — the exponential moving average

An exponential moving average (EMA) answers a natural complaint about the SMA: why should a price from N days ago count exactly as much as today's? An EMA instead blends today's price with yesterday's EMA, using a smoothing factor α = 2/(N+1) that puts more weight on recent data without discarding older data entirely — each past price's influence just fades geometrically rather than being dropped all at once.

Worked example 2 — a 3-day EMA, step by step

For N=3, α = 2/(3+1) = 0.5. Seed the EMA with the simple average of the first 3 prices (10, 12, 11): (10+12+11)/3 = 11. Day 4's price is 13: EMA = 0.5×13 + 0.5×11 = 12. Day 5's price is 14: EMA = 0.5×14 + 0.5×12 = 13. The final 3-day EMA is 13 — noticeably higher than the 3-day SMA of the same last three prices (11+13+14)/3 ≈ 12.67, because the EMA leans harder on day 5's high price of 14.

MA_N=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}P_i\qquad \text{EMA}_t=\alpha P_t+(1-\alpha)\,\text{EMA}_{t-1},\quad \alpha=\frac{2}{N+1}
MA_Nsimple moving average over the last N periods
P_i, P_tprice in period i (or at time t)
EMA_t, EMA_{t-1}the exponential moving average at the current and previous period (first seeded with a simple average)
αsmoothing factor; larger α (shorter N) weights recent prices more heavily
Try this
  1. Confirm the SMA is 12, then raise one price a lot — notice how much a single outlier can move a short average.
  2. In the crossover chart below, watch the fast (short) average cross back and forth over the slow (long) one — each crossing is a classic, debated "signal" point.
Common traps
  • A short moving average reacts to noise more than a long one — neither is simply "better", it's a trade-off.
  • An EMA's "seed" (the value you start the recursion from) matters less and less over time, but seeding it from too few days can distort early readings — real platforms typically seed from far more history than this small example uses.
  • Treating any single crossover as a standalone buy/sell signal — crossovers also fire false signals in sideways, choppy markets, and are commonly combined with other evidence (like volume, Lesson 0.4) rather than traded alone.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • 50-day/200-day moving-average crossovers discussed in market commentary.
  • MACD (moving average convergence/divergence), a widely used indicator, is built directly on top of the exponential moving average introduced here.
  • Most charting platforms let you switch the exact same price series between SMA and EMA (and change the lookback period) — the same data, a different averaging method, can flash a "signal" at a different moment.

Check yourself

1. What does a moving average do to day-to-day noise?
Smooths it out by averaging over several periods — trading noise for a clearer (but lagged) view of trend.
2. Using α = 2/(N+1) for a 3-day EMA, the previous EMA is 12 and the new price is 16. What's the new EMA?
α = 0.5. New EMA = 0.5×16 + 0.5×12 = 8 + 6 = 14.
3. Why can a 3-day EMA and a 3-day SMA, computed on the exact same five prices, end up with different final values?
The SMA weights the last three prices equally, while the EMA weights more recent prices more heavily through its recursive α-blending — so when the latest price is unusually high or low, the EMA reacts more strongly than the SMA does.

Takeaways

  • Moving averages trade noise for smoothness.
  • Shorter vs. longer averages trade off responsiveness and stability.
  • Exponential moving averages weight recent prices more heavily than simple moving averages, reacting faster to new data at the cost of being "jumpier".

Momentum indicators

The questionA stock's average recent gain is 1.5%, its average recent loss is 1.0%. What does the Relative Strength Index (RSI) say — and where do "average gain" and "average loss" actually come from in the first place, given that each day is either a gain or a loss, never both?

Intuition

RSI compares the size of recent gains to recent losses, scaled to a 0–100 range:

  • High readings (commonly around 70+) are called "overbought"; low readings (commonly around 30−) "oversold" — descriptive labels for how large recent gains have been relative to recent losses, not guarantees of a reversal.
  • RSI is typically computed over a fixed lookback window (commonly 14 periods), so it updates as the window rolls forward one period at a time.
  • A reading of exactly 50 means average gains and average losses over the window have been the same size — a "neutral" reading, neither strongly up nor down.
Worked example 1 — from given averages

RS = 1.5/1.0 = 1.5. RSI = 100 − 100/(1+1.5) = 100 − 40 = 60.

Build it up: where "average gain" and "average loss" come from

Over a lookback window, each day's price change is sorted into exactly one of two buckets: if the price rose, that day's move counts as a gain and contributes 0 to that day's loss bucket; if it fell, the (positive) size of the drop counts as a loss and that day contributes 0 to the gain bucket. Average gain and average loss are then just the plain average of each bucket across the whole window — including the zeros from the "other" direction's days.

Worked example 2 — computing RSI from 6 raw closes

Closes: 44.00, 44.25, 44.50, 43.75, 44.65, 45.10 → five daily changes: +0.25, +0.25, −0.75, +0.90, +0.45. Gains bucket: 0.25, 0.25, 0, 0.90, 0.45 → average = 1.85/5 = 0.37. Losses bucket: 0, 0, 0.75, 0, 0 → average = 0.75/5 = 0.15. RS = 0.37/0.15 ≈ 2.467. RSI = 100 − 100/(1+2.467) ≈ 100 − 28.85 = 71.15 — just into "overbought" territory, driven by the one large down day being outweighed by three up days.

RS=\frac{\text{avg gain}}{\text{avg loss}}\qquad RSI=100-\frac{100}{1+RS}
avg gainaverage of the positive price changes over the lookback window (0 counted on down days)
avg lossaverage of the (positive) size of negative price changes over the same window (0 counted on up days)
RS, RSIrelative strength, and RSI scaled to 0–100
Try this
  1. Raise average loss toward average gain: RS falls toward 1 and RSI moves toward 50 — neutral.
  2. In the curve below, notice how steeply RSI rises as RS moves away from 1 on the low side, versus how it flattens out for large RS — the formula itself isn't a straight line.
Common traps
  • "Overbought" doesn't mean "must fall now" — a strong trend can stay overbought for a long time.
  • A day with zero price change still has to be counted in the window (as a 0 in both buckets) — skipping it quietly shortens the lookback and skews the result.
  • This lesson uses a plain average for teaching clarity; the original Wilder RSI uses a smoothed moving average of gains/losses, so a live charting platform's default RSI on the same prices will read very slightly differently.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • RSI commonly plotted under a price chart as an overbought/oversold gauge.
  • RSI "divergences" (price makes a new high or low but RSI doesn't) are widely discussed among technical traders as a possible early-warning sign — contested, not proof.
  • Momentum indicators like RSI are frequently plotted alongside moving averages (Lesson 0.2) rather than read alone.

Check yourself

1. What does RSI compare?
The size of recent average gains to recent average losses, scaled to 0–100.
2. Avg gain 1.0, avg loss 2.0. RS and RSI?
RS = 0.5; RSI = 100 − 100/1.5 ≈ 33.3 (oversold territory).
3. A 5-day window has daily changes (in %) of +0.5, −0.3, +0.2, −0.3, +0.1. What are avg gain, avg loss, RS and RSI?
Gains: 0.5, 0, 0.2, 0, 0.1 → avg = 0.8/5 = 0.16. Losses: 0, 0.3, 0, 0.3, 0 → avg = 0.6/5 = 0.12. RS = 0.16/0.12 ≈ 1.333. RSI = 100 − 100/2.333 ≈ 57.1.

Takeaways

  • RSI scales recent gain/loss strength to 0–100.
  • Extremes are descriptive labels, not certainties.
  • Average gain and average loss come from sorting each day's change into one bucket and 0 into the other, then averaging across the whole lookback window.

Volume analysis

The questionA stock breaks above a resistance level on very high volume. Does that mean something different from the same breakout on thin volume — and could tracking volume over many days, not just one, reveal something a single day's ratio misses entirely?

Intuition

Volume — how many shares traded — is often used to confirm or question a price move:

  • A move on heavy volume is commonly viewed as more "convincing" (more participants agreeing with it) than the same move on thin volume, though this is a heuristic, not a law.
  • A single day's volume is easiest to read relative to its own recent average — hence a "volume ratio" rather than a raw share count, which varies hugely across different stocks.
  • One day's ratio, on its own, says nothing about whether conviction has been building or fading over the days (or weeks) leading up to it.
Worked example 1 — one day's ratio

Average daily volume 1,000,000 shares; breakout day volume 2,500,000 shares: volume ratio = 2,500,000/1,000,000 = 2.5× average — a notably heavier-than-usual day.

Build it up: turning many days of volume into one running trend — On-Balance Volume

On-Balance Volume (OBV) answers the question above directly: it's a running cumulative total that adds a day's volume when the price closes up, and subtracts it when the price closes down. The result is a single line that can reveal steady accumulation (OBV trending up) or distribution (OBV trending down) even on days where the price itself looks flat or choppy.

Worked example 2 — four days of OBV

Signed volumes (positive on an up-close day, negative on a down-close day): Day 1 +1,200,000; Day 2 −900,000; Day 3 +1,500,000; Day 4 −600,000. Running OBV: 0+1,200,000 = 1,200,000 → 1,200,000−900,000 = 300,000 → 300,000+1,500,000 = 1,800,000 → 1,800,000−600,000 = 1,200,000 (final). Even though the series ends on a down day, the final OBV of 1,200,000 is still well above 0 — across the four days, more volume showed up on up days than down days, a hint of net accumulation that a single day's ratio alone wouldn't show.

\text{volume ratio}=\frac{\text{day's volume}}{\text{average volume}}\qquad \text{OBV}_t=\text{OBV}_{t-1}+\text{sign}(\Delta P_t)\times V_t
volume ratioa day's volume divided by its recent average volume
OBV_t, OBV_{t-1}running on-balance-volume total, this period and the previous one (starts at 0)
sign(ΔP_t)+1 if the period closed up, −1 if it closed down, 0 if unchanged
V_tthat period's volume
Try this
  1. Bring the day's volume down toward the average: the ratio approaches 1 — an unremarkable volume day.
  2. In the OBV calculator, flip the sign on one day's volume (turn an up day into a down day of the same size): watch how much that single day swings the running total.
Common traps
  • Volume confirms conviction, not direction by itself — a heavy-volume move can still reverse.
  • OBV's absolute level means little on its own — what matters is its direction over time (rising, falling, or diverging from price), not the raw cumulative number, which depends on an arbitrary starting point.
  • A volume spike around a scheduled event (earnings, an index rebalance) can be a one-off mechanical effect rather than a genuine shift in investor conviction.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • "Breakout on high volume" is a commonly cited (and debated) pattern.
  • On-Balance Volume and related accumulation/distribution indicators are standard add-ons on most charting platforms, plotted directly under the price.
  • Unusually heavy volume around corporate actions (an index addition or removal, a large block trade) is routinely flagged in market commentary as "mechanical" rather than information-driven.

Check yourself

1. What does volume measure?
How many shares (or contracts) traded over a period.
2. Day volume 3,000,000, average 1,500,000. Ratio?
2×.
3. Three days of signed volume: +800,000 (up), −500,000 (down), +300,000 (up). What's the running OBV after each day, starting from 0?
After day 1: 800,000. After day 2: 800,000 − 500,000 = 300,000. After day 3: 300,000 + 300,000 = 600,000 (final).

Takeaways

  • Volume is used to gauge conviction behind a price move.
  • A single day's ratio is a heuristic, not a certainty.
  • On-Balance Volume turns many days of volume into one running trend line — its direction matters more than its absolute level.

The debate around technical analysis

The questionIf markets are even roughly efficient (Financial Markets, Lesson "market-efficiency"), should chart patterns work at all? And even if a pattern looks real in historical data, how would you tell a genuine edge apart from a run of lucky coin flips?

Intuition

Technical analysis has enthusiastic practitioners and serious academic skeptics:

  • Weak-form market efficiency specifically questions whether past-price patterns offer a reliable, repeatable edge — if enough participants already act on a visible pattern, the argument goes, the edge should get traded away.
  • Practitioners counter that markets are efficient only approximately, and that behavioral patterns (Behavioral Finance path) can create exploitable, if small and inconsistent, tendencies.
  • Neither side has fully "won" this debate — it remains genuinely contested, actively researched evidence, not a settled question.
Worked example 1 — a small edge vs. costs

If a pattern "works" only 52% of the time versus a 50% coin flip, that's a real but very small edge — easily erased by trading costs (Investment Fundamentals Lessons 1.1–1.2) unless the edge is large and consistent.

Build it up: is a measured edge even statistically real?

A measured win rate is only an estimate, built from a limited sample of past trades — and a small sample's estimate can easily be noise rather than a genuine edge. The standard error of a win rate measures how much that estimate could plausibly bounce around purely from sample size, even if the "true" long-run rate were exactly 50%. A common rule of thumb: an edge within roughly 1–2 standard errors of zero is hard to distinguish from random chance.

Worked example 2 — the same 52% edge, two sample sizes

At n = 100 trades: SE = √(0.52×0.48/100) ≈ 5.0%. The measured edge (2 percentage points above 50%) is only 2/5.0 ≈ 0.40 standard errors from zero — statistically indistinguishable from a coin flip. At n = 10,000 trades: SE = √(0.52×0.48/10,000) ≈ 0.50%. The same 2-point edge is now 2/0.50 = 4.0 standard errors from zero — much harder to dismiss as luck, though it still needs out-of-sample confirmation and a hard look at trading costs before it means anything in practice.

\text{edge}=\text{win rate}-50\%\qquad SE=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}\qquad z=\frac{p-0.5}{SE}
pmeasured win rate, as a decimal (e.g. 0.52)
nnumber of trades/observations in the sample
SEstandard error of the win-rate estimate
zthe edge measured in standard errors away from a 50% coin flip
Try this
  1. Raise the win rate: the edge grows. Push trading costs up alongside: a small edge can be erased entirely.
  2. In the standard-error calculator, hold the win rate fixed at 52% and raise the sample size n from 100 toward 10,000 — watch the standard error shrink and the same small edge turn from statistically meaningless into much harder to dismiss.
Common traps
  • A small statistical edge can be fully erased by trading costs and taxes.
  • Testing many different patterns or rules against the same historical data until one looks good (overfitting/data-mining) will eventually turn up an apparent "pattern" by chance alone, even in purely random data.
  • A statistically significant edge found in past data is still not a guarantee it continues to work going forward — markets can change as more participants learn and trade on a known pattern.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Academic market-efficiency research is routinely cited both for and against technical trading approaches.
  • Backtesting software, used by both retail and professional traders, tests chart-based rules against years of historical data before risking real money.
  • The Quant Investing path on this site formalizes exactly this kind of statistical-significance testing for trading signals.

Check yourself

1. What does weak-form efficiency specifically question?
Whether past price patterns offer a reliable, repeatable trading edge.
2. Win rate 55%, two round-trip costs of 0.3pp each. Net edge?
55 − 50 − 0.6 = 4.4 percentage points.
3. A 52% win rate is measured over 400 trades (SE ≈ 2.5%) versus the same 52% over just 100 trades (SE ≈ 5.0%). Which sample makes the 2-point edge more convincing, and why?
The 400-trade sample — the edge is about 2/2.5 ≈ 0.8 standard errors from zero there, versus only about 2/5.0 ≈ 0.4 at n=100. Neither is strongly past typical significance thresholds (often ~2 standard errors), which is exactly the point: sample size changes how much a given win rate can be trusted.

Takeaways

  • Technical analysis is debated, not settled, evidence.
  • A small edge can be erased by trading costs.
  • A measured edge needs a large enough sample — and should hold up out-of-sample — before it can be trusted as more than noise.
