Equity styles

The questionTwo stocks, same industry: one trades at a P/E of 35, the other at 12. The cheaper one also pays a much bigger dividend. Are they playing the same game — and could the higher-multiple one still end up the better total-return bet?

Intuition

Growth companies are expected to expand earnings quickly and typically trade at higher multiples (recall P/E, Investment Fundamentals Lesson 3.3) because the market is paying up for earnings that don't exist yet. Value companies trade cheaper relative to current earnings or assets, usually because the market expects their earnings to grow more slowly, or because they're temporarily out of favor. A few things to keep straight:

  • Growth vs. value is a split about valuation and growth expectations — nothing to do with company size.
  • Large-cap vs. small-cap is a separate split, by total market value of shares outstanding. The two dimensions combine into a "style box" — a large-cap value stock and a small-cap growth stock sit in different corners of the same grid.
  • Style isn't permanent: a fast grower that matures can drift from "growth" into "value" over years as its earnings growth slows and its multiple compresses.
Worked example 1 — the multiple gap

35/12 ≈ 2.9× — the growth stock is priced at nearly three times the value stock's earnings multiple, for the same current year of earnings.

Build it up: where does the return actually come from?

A P/E multiple alone doesn't say which stock will perform better — it only says how much the market is paying today per dollar of current earnings. A simple (and deliberately rough) way to compare the two styles' return drivers is to split expected total return into two pieces: the dividend you're paid now, plus the rate earnings are expected to grow. Growth companies typically reinvest most profit instead of paying it out, so they offer a lower dividend yield but a higher expected growth rate; value companies typically do the reverse.

Worked example 2 — dividend yield + growth

Growth stock: 0.5% dividend yield + 14% expected earnings growth ≈ 14.5% expected total return. Value stock: 4% dividend yield + 3% expected earnings growth ≈ 7% expected total return. The multiples tell only half the story — the growth stock's higher price today is a bet that its growth rate holds up; if it doesn't, that 14.5% can fall a long way.

\text{multiple ratio}=\frac{P/E_{\text{growth}}}{P/E_{\text{value}}}\qquad \text{expected return}\approx\text{dividend yield}+\text{earnings growth rate}
P/Eprice divided by earnings per share — higher means more is paid per dollar of current earnings
dividend yieldannual dividend per share ÷ share price
earnings growth ratethe expected annual % growth in a company's earnings — never guaranteed
Try this
  1. Bring the two P/Es together: the ratio approaches 1 — the "style gap" narrows.
  2. In the expected-return calculator, lower the growth stock's growth rate toward the value stock's: its expected-return advantage shrinks or disappears even though its multiple stays high — multiple and expected return aren't the same thing.
Common traps
  • A high multiple isn't automatically "overpriced" if growth is genuinely fast; a low multiple isn't automatically "cheap" (Lesson 3.3's trap again).
  • Assuming growth stocks always beat value stocks, or vice versa — both styles have had long stretches of outperforming and underperforming each other.
  • Treating the dividend-yield-plus-growth split as a precise forecast rather than a rough, illustrative decomposition — actual returns depend on whether the expected growth, and the multiple the market is willing to pay for it, both hold up.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Growth-heavy vs. value-heavy index funds track this exact split, often by sorting on metrics like P/E and P/B.
  • "Large-cap growth", "small-cap value" and similar labels are standard categories used across factor investing (Quant Investing path) and fund classification systems.
  • A sector tilt — for example, technology indices skewing growth, utility indices skewing value — is partly just this style split showing up industry by industry.

Check yourself

1. Which style typically trades at a higher earnings multiple, and why?
Growth — the market is paying up today for earnings it expects to arrive in the future, not only for what the company earns right now.
2. P/E 40 vs. P/E 10 — ratio? And is large-cap vs. small-cap the same split as this one?
40/10 = 4×. No — cap size is a separate dimension (total market value of shares), independent of growth vs. value.
3. A stock has a 1% dividend yield and 10% expected earnings growth; another has a 5% yield and 2% expected growth. Using the rough decomposition, which has the higher expected total return, and what's the catch?
1%+10%=11% vs. 5%+2%=7% — the first looks higher, but that 10% growth rate is a forecast, not a fact; if it disappoints, the "catch-up" value stock's steadier 7% may end up more reliable.

Takeaways

  • Growth vs. value is about valuation and growth expectations; large vs. small cap is a separate, size-based split.
  • A multiple alone doesn't reveal expected return — splitting return into dividend yield plus growth rate makes the trade-off concrete.
  • Neither style wins permanently; both the multiple gap and the return advantage can narrow, widen, or flip over time.

Fixed-income types

The questionA government bond yields 4%. A similar-maturity corporate bond yields 5.5%. Why the gap — and is the extra 1.5 percentage points pure profit, or is it priced to cover something specific?

Intuition

Government, corporate, municipal and high-yield bonds mainly differ in who owes the money and how likely they are to pay it back in full and on time:

  • Government bonds (from a fiscally stable issuer) are usually treated as the lowest-risk benchmark in their own currency.
  • Corporate bonds carry business risk — the issuing company could underperform, over-borrow, or fail outright.
  • Municipal bonds are issued by local or regional governments and, in some countries, carry particular tax treatment that affects their after-tax comparison to other bonds.
  • High-yield ("junk") bonds are issued by borrowers with meaningfully weaker credit — the name describes the yield on offer, not a free source of extra return.

A riskier borrower must offer a higher yield to attract lenders — the extra yield over a comparable government bond is the credit spread.

Worked example 1 — the spread itself

5.5% − 4% = 1.5 percentage points (150 basis points) of credit spread, compensating for the corporate issuer's higher default risk relative to the government.

Build it up: what is that spread actually paying for?

A useful (simplified) way to think about a credit spread is that it should roughly cover the expected loss from default, plus a risk premium lenders demand for bearing uncertainty they can't diversify away entirely. Expected loss is the probability the borrower defaults times how much is actually lost if it does (since bondholders typically recover something in a default, not zero):

Worked example 2 — expected loss vs. the spread charged

Suppose this corporate bond has a 2% annual probability of default, and lenders expect to recover 40% of their money if it does (a 60% loss given default). Expected loss = 2% × 60% = 1.2%. The market is charging a 1.5% spread, so only 0.3 percentage points of that 1.5% is a risk premium beyond the "fair" compensation for expected loss — the rest is just pricing in the arithmetic of default risk.

\text{Credit spread}=\text{Corporate yield}-\text{Government yield}\qquad \text{Expected loss}\approx PD\times LGD
PDprobability of default over the period
LGDloss given default — the fraction of the investment lost if default happens (1 − recovery rate)
Try this
  1. Raise the corporate yield: the spread widens — the market is pricing in more perceived risk.
  2. In the expected-loss calculator, raise the default probability or lower the recovery rate: expected loss rises, shrinking the "pure risk premium" left over from a fixed spread — and can even exceed it, implying the bond may be priced too cheaply for its risk.
Common traps
  • "High-yield" is a polite name for higher default risk, not a free source of extra return.
  • Comparing headline yields across bonds with very different maturities or tax treatments without adjusting — a municipal bond's tax-exempt status (where applicable) can make its after-tax yield competitive with a higher pre-tax corporate yield.
  • Credit ratings summarize risk but are opinions updated on a lag, not guarantees — spreads can move before, or instead of, a rating change.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Credit spreads widening sharply during stress periods (illustrative: spreads on lower-rated corporate debt have historically widened well beyond government-bond yields in downturns) as perceived default risk rises.
  • Corporate bond funds and ETFs often grouped by credit tier (investment-grade vs. high-yield) precisely along this spectrum.
  • Municipal bond funds marketed partly on their tax treatment relative to taxable corporate or government bonds.

Check yourself

1. What does a credit spread compensate a lender for?
The extra risk of the borrower not paying as promised — roughly, expected loss from default plus a risk premium for bearing that uncertainty.
2. Government yield 3%, corporate 4.8%. Spread? If default probability is 3% and loss given default is 50%, how much of that spread is "pure risk premium"?
Spread = 1.8pp. Expected loss = 3%×50% = 1.5%. Risk premium = 1.8−1.5 = 0.3 percentage points.
3. Is "high-yield" bond return extra free money, and does a high credit rating guarantee no losses?
No to both — high-yield compensates for materially higher default risk, and even investment-grade-rated bonds can default or be downgraded; a rating is an opinion, not a guarantee.

Takeaways

  • Bond types mainly differ by borrower and default risk; municipal bonds add a tax dimension in many markets.
  • Credit spread is the market's price for that extra risk, roughly decomposable into expected loss (PD × LGD) plus a risk premium.
  • A bond's spread can be compared to its own expected loss to judge whether it looks cheap or rich relative to its estimated default risk.

Alternative assets

The questionA private fund charges "2 and 20". On $1,000,000 committed with $200,000 of profit above the hurdle, what does that actually cost — and after paying it, how much of the fund's "20% gross return" does an investor actually keep?

Intuition

Private equity, hedge funds and collectibles trade liquidity and transparency (you often can't sell quickly, and there's no daily market price) for potentially different return drivers than public markets. "2 and 20" means a 2% annual management fee plus 20% of profits above a hurdle (a minimum return the fund must clear before it earns a performance cut).

Worked example 1 — the fee bill

Management fee: 2% × $1,000,000 = $20,000. Performance fee: 20% × $200,000 = $40,000. Total: $60,000 for the year.

Build it up: from gross return to what the investor actually keeps

The fee itself is only half the picture — what matters to an investor is the net return after fees are subtracted from the gross return the fund actually earned. The $200,000 of "profit above hurdle" in the question is itself part of the fund's gross gain; the investor's net result is that gross gain minus whatever the fund charges to generate it.

Worked example 2 — gross to net

On $1,000,000 committed, suppose the fund's gross profit for the year is $200,000 (a 20% gross return). Fees from Worked example 1 total $60,000. Net profit = $200,000 − $60,000 = $140,000, so the net return is $140,000 / $1,000,000 = 14% — fees quietly consumed 6 of the 20 percentage points, almost a third of the headline gain.

\text{Total fee}=\text{mgmt \%}\times\text{committed}+\text{carry \%}\times\text{profit above hurdle}\qquad \text{Net return}=\frac{\text{Gross profit}-\text{Total fee}}{\text{Committed}}
mgmt %the annual management fee rate, charged on committed capital regardless of performance
carry %the performance fee rate, charged only on profit above the hurdle
Try this
  1. Raise the profit above hurdle: the performance fee grows proportionally; the management fee doesn't.
  2. In the allocation lab below, raise the weight on Alternatives: see how it changes a blended portfolio's expected return and risk before any fee is even subtracted — fees then still need to be paid out of whatever gross return actually shows up.
Common traps
  • Illiquidity isn't automatically compensated by better returns — it's a real cost of flexibility given up, not a guaranteed premium.
  • Quoting only the gross return a fund earned, without netting out fees, overstates what an investor actually keeps.
  • Infrequent pricing of illiquid holdings (private equity, collectibles, some real estate) can make reported volatility look artificially smooth — a lack of daily prices isn't the same as a lack of risk.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • "2 and 20" is a widely-referenced (though not universal) private-fund fee convention, varying by fund type and negotiating power.
  • Hedge fund "high-water marks" — a rule that a fund must recover any prior losses before charging a new performance fee — directly address the gross-vs-net gap this lesson covers.
  • Collectibles (fine art, wine, classic cars) as an illustrative alternative-asset category, valued infrequently and traded in thin, specialized markets.

Check yourself

1. What does "2 and 20" mean?
A 2% annual management fee plus 20% of profits above a hurdle.
2. $500,000 committed, 2% mgmt fee, $100,000 profit above hurdle, 20% carry, and the fund's gross profit for the year is $100,000. Total fee, and net return?
Total fee = 0.02×500,000 + 0.20×100,000 = 10,000 + 20,000 = $30,000. Net profit = 100,000 − 30,000 = $70,000; net return = 70,000/500,000 = 14%.
3. Does illiquidity guarantee a higher return, and does a smooth-looking return history on an illiquid asset mean it actually carried little risk?
No to both — illiquidity is a cost (reduced flexibility) with no guaranteed payoff, and infrequent pricing can hide volatility rather than eliminate it.

Takeaways

  • Alternatives trade liquidity/transparency for potentially different return drivers.
  • "2 and 20" is a common fee structure; always ask what the net, not gross, return was after fees.
  • Illiquid assets' smooth-looking prices can understate true risk, since there's no daily market forcing a fresh valuation.

International assets

The questionA foreign stock returns 10% in its local currency, which then falls 4% against your home currency. What's your actual return — and is there a way to avoid that currency swing entirely?

Intuition

International investing (across developed markets — established economies and institutions — and emerging markets — earlier-stage, often faster-growing but less liquid and more volatile) adds a currency-movement component on top of the local-market return. A weaker foreign currency subtracts from your home-currency return; a stronger one adds to it. Two returns are being multiplied together here, not added, which matters once both are large.

Worked example 1 — the currency drag

(1.10 × 0.96) − 1 ≈ 5.6% — noticeably below the 10% local return, purely from currency. The stock itself did exactly what the headline said; the exchange rate quietly took back almost half the gain.

Build it up: hedging the currency leg

Investors who want the foreign stock's local return without the currency's ups and downs can use currency hedging — typically a forward or futures contract that locks in today's exchange rate for a future date. Hedging isn't free: it has an explicit cost (driven mainly by the interest-rate gap between the two currencies), so a hedged investment's return is approximately the local return minus that hedging cost, regardless of which way the currency actually moves.

Worked example 2 — hedged vs. unhedged

Same foreign stock, a different year: local return 12%, currency falls 4% again, and hedging this exposure costs 1.5%/year. Unhedged: (1.12 × 0.96) − 1 ≈ 7.52%. Hedged: 12% − 1.5% = 10.5% — hedging looks better here because the currency happened to move against the investor, but the 1.5% cost is paid either way, even in a year the currency moves in the investor's favor.

R_{\text{home, unhedged}}=(1+R_{\text{local}})\times(1+R_{\text{fx}})-1\qquad R_{\text{home, hedged}}\approx R_{\text{local}}-\text{hedging cost}
R_fxthe foreign currency's return against your home currency (negative if it weakens)
hedging costthe ongoing price of locking in today's exchange rate, paid whether the currency helps or hurts
Try this
  1. Make the currency move positive (strengthen): home-currency return exceeds the local return.
  2. In the hedging calculator, flip the currency move to positive: the unhedged return now beats the hedged one — hedging removes both the downside and the upside from currency, it doesn't just protect against bad outcomes.
Common traps
  • Forgetting the currency leg entirely when comparing a foreign return to a domestic one.
  • Assuming currency movements always average out to roughly zero over any horizon — over shorter periods they can be a large, persistent part of total return, in either direction.
  • Treating a hedge as a one-sided insurance policy — it removes currency's effect both when it would have helped and when it would have hurt, and it costs money every year regardless.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • A US investor's return on a foreign stock depends on both the stock and the exchange rate.
  • Currency-hedged international index funds exist specifically to strip out this effect, at the cost of an ongoing hedging fee.
  • Emerging-market currencies have historically shown larger swings than major developed-market currencies, adding to (or subtracting from) local equity returns more dramatically.

Check yourself

1. Local return 8%, currency +2%. Home-currency return?
(1.08×1.02)−1 ≈ 10.2%.
2. Local return 9%, currency −3%, hedging costs 1%/year. Unhedged and hedged home returns — which is higher this time?
Unhedged: (1.09×0.97)−1 ≈ 5.73%. Hedged: 9%−1% = 8%. Hedged wins here because the currency moved against the investor.
3. Does hedging only protect against currency moving the wrong way?
No — it removes the currency effect in both directions and always costs its fee, even in years the currency would have helped.

Takeaways

  • International returns multiply the local return and a currency return, not add them.
  • Currency moves can meaningfully help or hurt the final result, and can be hedged away at a cost.
  • A currency hedge is symmetric — it gives up upside as well as downside, for a fee paid every year.

Inflation-protected assets

The questionA regular bond's coupon is fixed in dollars. What's different about a bond whose principal adjusts with inflation — and if one bond is inflation-linked and another isn't, can comparing their two yields tell you what the market expects inflation to be?

Intuition

An inflation-linked bond adjusts its principal with a price index (like CPI, Lesson 0.2 of Economics), so the coupon payment (a fixed rate applied to that adjusted principal) rises with inflation too — directly addressing the purchasing-power erosion from that same CPI/inflation lesson. A regular ("nominal") bond makes no such adjustment: its coupon and principal are fixed in dollar terms no matter what happens to prices.

Worked example 1 — the principal adjustment

$1,000 principal, 3% inflation adjustment: new principal = 1,000 × 1.03 = $1,030. A 2% coupon then applies to $1,030, not the original $1,000, paying $1,030 × 0.02 = $20.60 instead of $20.00.

Build it up: what the market is implying about future inflation

Because an inflation-linked bond's yield is already a real yield (it doesn't need to compensate for inflation — the principal does that automatically), comparing it to a nominal bond's yield of similar maturity backs out the market's implied inflation forecast — the breakeven inflation rate. It's the same real/nominal relationship as the Fisher equation (Economics Lesson 0.2), just read in reverse: instead of computing a real return from a known nominal return and inflation rate, here the market gives both yields and the "unknown" being solved for is inflation itself.

Worked example 2 — breakeven inflation

A nominal government bond yields 4.5%; a similar-maturity inflation-linked bond yields 1.5% (real). Quick approximation: 4.5% − 1.5% = 3.0% breakeven inflation. Exact Fisher relationship: (1.045/1.015) − 1 ≈ 2.96% — close to, but a touch below, the quick approximation, the same small gap seen in Economics Lesson 0.2.

\text{Adjusted principal}=\text{Principal}\times(1+\text{inflation})\qquad \text{Breakeven inflation}\approx y_{\text{nominal}}-y_{\text{real}}\ \ \left(\text{exact: }\frac{1+y_{\text{nominal}}}{1+y_{\text{real}}}-1\right)
y_nominalyield on a regular (non-inflation-linked) bond of similar maturity
y_realyield on the inflation-linked bond — already net of inflation
breakeven inflationthe inflation rate at which both bonds would deliver the same return — the market's implied forecast, not a promise
Try this
  1. Raise inflation: the adjusted principal (and thus the coupon in dollar terms) rises with it.
  2. In the breakeven calculator, bring the nominal and real yields close together: breakeven inflation falls toward zero — the market is pricing in very little future inflation.
Common traps
  • Inflation-linked bonds still carry interest-rate risk — inflation protection isn't the same as price stability (Lesson 2.2's bonds lesson again: real yields can still rise or fall and move the bond's price).
  • Breakeven inflation is the market's implied expectation, priced into today's yields — not a guarantee of what inflation will actually turn out to be.
  • A real yield can be negative (the inflation-linked bond's stated real return, before inflation adjustment, is below zero) — inflation protection doesn't guarantee a positive real return, only that purchasing power erosion itself is offset.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Inflation-linked government bonds (e.g. TIPS-style securities) issued by multiple national governments.
  • Breakeven inflation rates are widely quoted by central banks and market commentators as a read on inflation expectations.
  • Pension funds and insurers sometimes hold inflation-linked bonds specifically to match long-dated, inflation-sensitive future obligations.

Check yourself

1. What adjusts on an inflation-linked bond?
The principal, based on a price index like CPI — and the coupon payment, since it's a fixed rate applied to that adjusted principal.
2. Principal $2,000, inflation 4%. Adjusted principal and coupon payment at a 3% coupon rate?
Adjusted principal = 2,000 × 1.04 = $2,080. Coupon = 2,080 × 0.03 = $62.40.
3. Nominal bond yields 5%, inflation-linked bond yields 2% real. Breakeven inflation (quick approx and exact), and what does that number actually represent?
Approx: 5%−2%=3%. Exact: (1.05/1.02)−1 ≈ 2.94%. It represents the market's implied inflation forecast over that bond's maturity — not a guarantee of future inflation.

Takeaways

  • Inflation-linked bonds adjust principal (and thus coupon payments) with a price index.
  • They still carry interest-rate risk, just not inflation-erosion risk.
  • Comparing a nominal bond's yield to a similar-maturity inflation-linked bond's yield reveals the market's implied breakeven inflation rate.
