3.1

Reading a balance sheet

The questionA company reports $500,000 in assets and $300,000 in liabilities. Is that good or bad? And what happens to that verdict if most of the company's "current assets" turn out to be slow-moving inventory sitting in a warehouse rather than cash in the bank?

Intuition

A balance sheet is a snapshot at one single moment in time — unlike the income statement (Lesson 3.2), which covers a whole period. It has three buckets:

  • Assets — everything the company owns of value: cash, inventory, equipment, buildings, amounts customers owe it.
  • Liabilities — everything the company owes: loans, bonds issued, unpaid bills, wages owed.
  • Equity — what's left over for owners once liabilities are subtracted from assets. It isn't measured independently; it's defined as the residual.

Solvency asks whether a company can meet its long-term obligations, typically checked with debt-to-equity (liabilities ÷ equity). Liquidity asks whether it can meet its near-term obligations, usually checked with the current ratio (current assets ÷ current liabilities — both restricted to things due or convertible to cash within about a year).

Worked example 1 — the basics

Assets $500,000, liabilities $300,000 → equity = $200,000. Debt-to-equity = 300,000/200,000 = 1.5. Separately, current assets of $120,000 against current liabilities of $80,000 gives a current ratio of 120,000/80,000 = 1.5 — comfortably above 1, meaning short-term obligations look well covered at first glance.

Build it up: the identity that always holds, and what "current assets" can hide

Assets = Liabilities + Equity isn't a coincidence or a rule of thumb — it's true by construction, because equity is defined as assets minus liabilities. Any real balance sheet you're handed should satisfy this identity exactly; if it doesn't, something's been mis-booked. That gives a quick sanity check before trusting any other number on the page.

The current ratio has a blind spot, though: it treats all current assets as equally liquid, but inventory sitting unsold is far less liquid than cash or money customers already owe you. The quick ratio (or "acid-test") strips inventory out, giving a stricter liquidity check.

Worked example 2 — identity check and the quick-ratio gap

Company B: assets $2,000,000, liabilities $1,400,000 → equity = $600,000. Identity check: 1,400,000 + 600,000 = 2,000,000 ✓. Debt-to-equity = 1,400,000/600,000 ≈ 2.33 — noticeably more leveraged than the first company. Its current assets are $300,000 (including $150,000 of inventory) against current liabilities of $250,000: current ratio = 300,000/250,000 = 1.2 — looks fine. But quick ratio = (300,000 − 150,000)/250,000 = 0.6 — below 1, meaning liquid assets alone don't cover near-term liabilities. The current ratio alone would have missed this.

The formal definition

\text{Equity}=\text{Assets}-\text{Liabilities}\qquad \text{D/E}=\frac{\text{Liabilities}}{\text{Equity}}
\text{Current ratio}=\frac{\text{Current assets}}{\text{Current liabilities}}\qquad \text{Quick ratio}=\frac{\text{Current assets}-\text{Inventory}}{\text{Current liabilities}}
Assetseverything the company owns of value
Liabilitieseverything the company owes
Equitythe residual belonging to owners
Current assets/liabilitiesthose due, or convertible to cash, within roughly a year
Inventoryunsold goods — a current asset, but the least liquid one
Try this
  1. Raise liabilities while equity stays fixed: debt-to-equity climbs.
  2. In the second calculator, raise inventory toward the full current-assets figure: the quick ratio falls toward 0 even if the current ratio doesn't move at all.
Common traps
  • More debt isn't automatically bad — it can fund productive growth; context (industry, interest rates) matters.
  • A very high current ratio can also signal cash sitting idle rather than financial strength.
  • A healthy-looking current ratio can hide a real liquidity problem when much of "current assets" is slow-moving inventory — the quick ratio strips that out and can tell a very different story.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Debt-to-equity is commonly compared across companies within the same industry.
  • "Working capital" discussions reference the current ratio.
  • The quick ratio (acid-test) is a standard screen for businesses that carry large inventories, such as retailers or manufacturers, where the current ratio alone can be misleading.

Check yourself

1. Why does Assets = Liabilities + Equity always hold, rather than just usually hold?
Equity isn't measured independently — it's defined as assets minus liabilities, so the identity is true by construction. If it doesn't balance, a number has been recorded wrong.
2. Assets $2,000,000, liabilities $1,400,000, current assets $300,000 (of which $150,000 is inventory), current liabilities $250,000. Current ratio and quick ratio — and what does the gap between them tell you?
Current ratio = 300,000/250,000 = 1.2. Quick ratio = (300,000 − 150,000)/250,000 = 0.6. The gap shows that a current ratio above 1 was being propped up by inventory — once that's excluded, liquid assets don't cover near-term liabilities.
3. Why might comparing debt-to-equity across different industries be misleading?
Typical, healthy debt levels vary a lot by industry (e.g. capital-intensive utilities vs. asset-light software firms), so an industry-agnostic comparison can mislead.

Takeaways

  • A balance sheet is a snapshot of assets, liabilities and equity, and Assets = Liabilities + Equity always holds by definition.
  • Debt-to-equity and the current ratio summarise solvency and liquidity respectively — but the current ratio can be misleadingly flattered by inventory.
  • The quick ratio strips out inventory for a stricter liquidity check, and ratios generally should be judged in industry context, not in isolation.
3.2

Reading an income statement

The questionTwo companies both report $1,000,000 in revenue. How do you tell which one is actually running a tighter, more profitable business — especially if one has the thinner gross margin, but still ends up more profitable once every cost is counted?

Intuition

An income statement runs from revenue (the top line) through a sequence of costs to net income (the bottom line). It's a waterfall, not a single number:

  • Revenue minus COGS (cost of goods sold — the direct cost of making what was sold) = gross profit.
  • Gross profit minus operating expenses (salaries, marketing, R&D, rent — running the business day to day) = operating income.
  • Operating income minus everything else (interest on debt, taxes, one-off items) = net income.

Each stage has its own margin — the dollar figure divided by revenue, expressed as a percentage. Margins, not raw dollars, are what let you compare companies of very different sizes fairly: a company with $10 million net income on $1 billion revenue is thinner, not fatter, than one with $100,000 net income on $1 million revenue.

Worked example 1 — the full waterfall

Revenue $1,000,000, COGS $600,000 → gross profit $400,000 (gross margin 40%). Operating expenses $250,000 → operating income $150,000 (operating margin 15%). Interest and taxes together take a further $70,000 → net income $80,000 (net margin 8%). Each stage shows exactly where the money went.

Build it up: operating margin as the waypoint, and why it can flip the ranking

Operating margin sits between gross and net margin and measures profitability from the core business, before financing and tax decisions get involved. It matters because two companies can have the same revenue and completely opposite gross-margin stories, yet end up ranked the other way around once operating expenses are factored in.

Worked example 2 — same revenue, opposite story

Company A: revenue $1,000,000, COGS $600,000 → gross profit $400,000 (40% gross margin). Operating expenses $300,000 → operating income $100,000 (10% operating margin). After $20,000 of interest/tax → net income $80,000 (8% net margin).

Company B: revenue $1,000,000, COGS $750,000 → gross profit $250,000 (25% gross margin — looks much thinner than A). Operating expenses just $70,000 → operating income $180,000 (18% operating margin). After $40,000 of interest/tax → net income $140,000 (14% net margin). Company B looked worse at the gross-margin line but ends up considerably more profitable at the net-margin line, purely because it runs a much leaner operating-expense base.

The formal definition

\text{Gross margin}=\frac{\text{Revenue}-\text{COGS}}{\text{Revenue}}\qquad \text{Operating margin}=\frac{\text{Gross profit}-\text{Opex}}{\text{Revenue}}\qquad \text{Net margin}=\frac{\text{Net income}}{\text{Revenue}}
COGScost of goods sold — the direct cost of producing what was sold
Opexoperating expenses — running the business day to day (not including interest or tax)
Operating incomegross profit minus operating expenses — profit from the core business alone
Net incomewhat's left after all expenses, interest and taxes
Try this
  1. Raise COGS toward revenue: gross margin shrinks toward 0%.
  2. In the two-company calculator, keep each company's gross margin fixed but swap their operating-expense figures: watch the net-margin ranking flip even though gross margin didn't change at all.
Common traps
  • Comparing raw net income dollars across very differently-sized companies says little — compare margins instead.
  • A high gross margin can still end in a low net margin if operating expenses, interest or taxes are large.
  • A strong operating margin doesn't guarantee a strong net margin — heavy debt-interest costs below the operating line can still erode it, so it's worth checking more than one margin line, not just one.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Earnings reports regularly discuss "margin expansion" or "margin compression" from one period to the next, often separately for gross, operating and net margin.
  • Software and services businesses are often cited as having structurally high gross margins (illustratively, 70–80%), while retailers and distributors are cited as structurally low-gross-margin businesses (illustratively, 25–35%) that compete instead on volume and lean operating costs.
  • Analysts frequently flag a company whose operating margin is improving even while gross margin is flat or falling, as a sign of cost discipline elsewhere in the business.

Check yourself

1. Why compare margins instead of raw profit dollars?
Margins are percentages of revenue, which makes profitability comparable across companies of very different sizes.
2. Revenue $1,200,000, COGS $800,000, operating expenses $300,000. Gross margin and operating margin?
Gross profit = 400,000, gross margin = 400,000/1,200,000 ≈ 33.33%. Operating income = 400,000 − 300,000 = 100,000, operating margin = 100,000/1,200,000 ≈ 8.33%.
3. Can a company have a lower gross margin than a rival but still end up with a higher net margin?
Yes — if it runs much leaner operating expenses (or lower interest/tax costs), the gap can more than make up for a thinner gross margin by the time you reach net income, as in Worked example 2.

Takeaways

  • Income statements run from revenue down to net income through a waterfall of gross, operating and net profit.
  • Gross, operating and net margin are percentage measures that allow fair comparison across company sizes.
  • A thinner gross margin doesn't settle the question — leaner operating expenses can flip the final net-margin ranking entirely.
3.3

P/E and other multiples

The questionA stock trades at $60 with $4 of earnings per share — "P/E of 15". What does that number actually mean, is 15 good, and how could a fast-growing company and a shrinking company both show the exact same P/E of 15 for completely opposite reasons?

Intuition

A multiple expresses price relative to some fundamental figure, so prices of wildly different companies become comparable. Two common ones:

  • Price-to-earnings (P/E) — how much investors are paying today per dollar of annual earnings.
  • Price-to-book (P/B) — how price compares to the company's accounting net worth per share (equity, from Lesson 3.1, divided by shares outstanding).

A multiple is only meaningful in comparison — to the company's own history, to peers in the same industry, or to its expected growth. A P/E of 15 sitting alone tells you almost nothing.

Worked example 1 — the basics

Price $60, EPS $4 → P/E = 60/4 = 15, meaning investors pay $15 for every $1 of current annual earnings. If book value per share is $30, P/B = 60/30 = 2.0.

Build it up: trailing vs. forward, and the PEG ratio

"EPS" in a P/E calculation can mean two different things, and mixing them up is a common source of confusion:

  • Trailing P/E — uses the last 12 months of actual, already-reported earnings. Backward-looking, but based on real numbers.
  • Forward P/E — uses analysts' projected earnings for the next 12 months. Forward-looking, but based on an estimate that may turn out wrong.

This directly answers the opening question: a company whose earnings are expected to grow quickly can show a high trailing P/E (expensive relative to last year's smaller earnings) but a much lower forward P/E (reasonable relative to next year's larger, expected earnings) — while a company whose earnings are expected to shrink shows the opposite pattern. One more step ties the multiple directly to that growth expectation: the PEG ratio divides the P/E by the expected annual earnings growth rate (as a plain number, not a percentage), as a rough cross-check of whether a given multiple looks reasonable relative to how fast earnings are expected to grow — a PEG noticeably below 1 is sometimes read as a sign the multiple isn't demanding much for the growth on offer, though it is only a rule of thumb, not a verdict.

Worked example 2 — trailing vs. forward

Price $90, trailing EPS (last 12 months, actual) $3.00 → trailing P/E = 90/3 = 30 — looks expensive. Forward EPS (next 12 months, projected) $4.50, implying 50% expected earnings growth → forward P/E = 90/4.5 = 20 — the same price looks much more reasonable once the expected growth is priced into the denominator.

Worked example 3 — PEG

Using the trailing P/E of 30 and the 50% expected growth rate from Worked example 2: PEG = 30/50 = 0.6 — by the common rule of thumb (PEG around or below 1 read as "not demanding much for the growth"), this multiple doesn't look stretched once growth is accounted for, even though the trailing P/E of 30 looked high in isolation.

The formal definition

P/E=\frac{\text{Price}}{\text{EPS}}\qquad\qquad P/B=\frac{\text{Price}}{\text{Book value per share}}\qquad\qquad PEG=\frac{P/E}{\text{Expected annual EPS growth \%}}
EPSearnings per share — net income divided by shares outstanding
Trailing EPSactual EPS over the last 12 reported months
Forward EPSanalysts' projected EPS for the next 12 months
Book value per shareequity (Lesson 3.1) divided by shares outstanding
PEGP/E divided by the expected annual earnings growth rate, as a plain number
Try this
  1. Raise EPS with price fixed: P/E falls — the same price now buys more earnings.
  2. In the trailing/forward calculator, raise forward EPS further above trailing EPS: forward P/E and PEG both fall, even though the price and trailing P/E never changed.
Common traps
  • A "low" P/E can mean cheap, or it can mean the market expects earnings to fall — context matters.
  • Comparing P/E across very different industries without adjusting for typical growth and risk is misleading.
  • PEG is a rough rule of thumb, not a precise valuation tool — it assumes the growth estimate is accurate and ignores differences in risk between companies, which real decisions can't.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • P/E is commonly compared across companies in the same sector.
  • P/B is especially common for valuing financial companies like banks.
  • Forward P/E is widely quoted for whole market indices alongside trailing P/E, and PEG ratios are commonly referenced when discussing growth-oriented stock categories.

Check yourself

1. What does a P/E of 20 mean, in plain words, and why is the number alone not enough to judge whether it's "good"?
Investors are paying $20 today for every $1 of the company's current annual earnings; whether that's "good" depends on comparing it to history, peers or expected growth — not the raw number in isolation.
2. Price $40, trailing EPS $2, forward EPS $2.50. Trailing P/E, forward P/E, implied growth %, and PEG?
Trailing P/E = 40/2 = 20. Forward P/E = 40/2.5 = 16. Growth = (2.50−2)/2 = 25%. PEG = 20/25 = 0.8.
3. Why might a company's trailing P/E look high even though its forward P/E looks much lower?
Strong expected earnings growth makes next year's projected earnings much larger than last year's actual earnings, which lowers the multiple computed on those larger, forward-looking earnings — even though the price and trailing P/E alone looked expensive.

Takeaways

  • A multiple expresses price relative to a fundamental like earnings or book value.
  • Trailing P/E looks backward at actual earnings; forward P/E looks forward at projected earnings — the same stock can look very different on each.
  • PEG cross-checks a P/E against expected growth, but it's a rule of thumb, not a guarantee, and a low multiple is never automatically "cheap" in a good way.

Intro to discounted cash flow

The questionA project is expected to hand you $100,000 in 3 years. Worth $75,131 or $100,000 today — and why does the rate you pick change the answer so much? And once you're projecting cash flows for a company that (hopefully) keeps operating forever, where does the projecting actually stop?

Intuition

Discounted cash flow (DCF) extends Lesson 0.3's present-value idea from one future payment to a whole stream of projected cash flows: discount each one back to today and add them up. The arithmetic is simple; what actually drives the answer is the assumed discount rate and growth of future cash flows — small changes there can swing the result enormously.

Worked example 1 — a single future payment

A single $100,000 cash flow in 3 years, discounted at 10%: PV = 100,000/1.1³ ≈ $75,131. A full DCF just repeats this for each year's projected cash flow and sums the results.

Build it up: summing a stream, then the problem of "forever"

A business doesn't hand you one payment — it (hopefully) keeps generating cash every year indefinitely. A DCF handles this in two pieces: an explicit projection period — a few years of individually estimated cash flows, discounted and summed just like Worked example 1 — followed by a terminal value, a single number standing in for every cash flow after that, because nobody can credibly forecast year-by-year detail decades out. The simplest way to estimate it treats the cash flow as growing at a steady long-term rate forever (a "growing perpetuity"):

Worked example 2 — summing three explicit years

Projected cash flows of $100,000, $110,000 and $121,000 in years 1–3 (10% annual growth), discounted at 10%: PV₁ = 100,000/1.1 ≈ $90,909.09; PV₂ = 110,000/1.1² ≈ $90,909.09; PV₃ = 121,000/1.1³ ≈ $90,909.09. Sum ≈ $272,727.27. (Notice all three years contribute an identical present value — that's a special case: when the cash-flow growth rate exactly equals the discount rate, every year's discounted contribution is the same dollar amount.)

Worked example 3 — adding the terminal value

Beyond year 3, assume cash flows keep growing at a slower, sustainable long-term rate g = 3% forever. Year-4 cash flow = 121,000 × 1.03 = $124,630. Terminal value at year 3 = 124,630/(0.10 − 0.03) ≈ $1,780,428.57. Discounted back 3 years: 1,780,428.57/1.1³ ≈ $1,337,662.34. Total value = explicit sum $272,727.27 + discounted terminal value $1,337,662.34 ≈ $1,610,389.61 — and the terminal value alone makes up over 80% of the total.

Now raise the long-term growth rate g by just 2 percentage points, from 3% to 5%, holding everything else fixed: year-4 cash flow = 121,000 × 1.05 = $127,050, terminal value = 127,050/(0.10 − 0.05) = $2,541,000, discounted back ≈ $1,909,090.91, total value ≈ $2,181,818.18 — a 35.5% jump in the entire valuation from a 2-point change in one long-run assumption.

The formal definition

PV=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^{t}}\qquad\qquad TV_n=\frac{CF_n(1+g)}{r-g}\qquad\qquad PV(TV_n)=\frac{TV_n}{(1+r)^{n}}
CFₜthe projected cash flow in year t
rthe discount rate
nnumber of years explicitly projected
gthe assumed long-term growth rate after year n, forever
TVₙterminal value — the value, as of year n, of every cash flow after that
Try this
  1. Raise the discount rate in the first plot: present value falls — often sharply for cash flows further out.
  2. In the terminal-value calculator, nudge the long-term growth rate g up and down by just a point or two and watch how much more the total value swings compared to nudging the discount rate by the same amount — this is the "small assumption, huge output" effect from Worked example 3.
Common traps
  • Small changes in the assumed discount or growth rate can swing a DCF valuation enormously — "garbage in, garbage out".
  • Projecting cash flows many years into the future is inherently uncertain, even with careful work.
  • The terminal value often makes up the large majority of a DCF's total value — so a model built from carefully detailed near-term projections can still be dominated by one long-run growth guess buried at the end.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • DCF is a standard tool in equity research and company valuation.
  • The same present-value math underlies bond pricing (Lesson 2.2) and real-estate valuation.
  • Equity research reports routinely disclose their assumed terminal growth rate, since it's often the single most-debated number in the entire model.

Check yourself

1. What does a DCF do that single-period present value (Lesson 0.3) doesn't?
It discounts and sums a whole stream of projected future cash flows, plus a terminal value for everything beyond the explicit projection — not just one single payment.
2. A final projected cash flow is $200,000, discount rate 9%, long-term growth rate 2%. Terminal value at that year?
Next-year cash flow = 200,000 × 1.02 = $204,000. Terminal value = 204,000/(0.09 − 0.02) = 204,000/0.07 ≈ $2,914,285.71.
3. Why can a 2-percentage-point change in the terminal growth rate swing total value by over 30%, when the discount rate changing by the same amount typically swings it less?
Terminal value divides by (r − g); when g is close to r, that denominator is small, so small moves in g produce proportionally much larger moves in the result — and because the terminal value is usually the majority of total value, that swing dominates the whole DCF (as in Worked example 3, where total value jumped 35.5% from a 2-point rise in g).

Takeaways

  • DCF sums the present value of a projected stream of cash flows, plus a discounted terminal value for everything afterward.
  • The terminal value, driven by the long-term growth assumption, typically dominates the total — a small change there swings the whole valuation far more than the near-term numbers do.
  • The same discounting idea underlies bonds and real estate too.

Intro to chart reading

The questionA stock moves from $40 to $55 over 10 weeks, but it wasn't a straight line — some weeks it dipped before climbing again. Is that still an "uptrend", and how would you confirm it rather than just eyeballing the wiggles?

Intuition

A few building blocks show up on almost every price chart:

  • A candlestick shows one period's open, high, low and close at a glance — the thick "body" spans open to close, thin "wicks" mark the high and low reached along the way.
  • A trend is a series of higher highs and higher lows (up) or lower highs and lower lows (down); a simple trend line connects them.
  • Support is a price level buyers have repeatedly stepped in at; resistance is a level sellers have repeatedly capped the price at.

(The dedicated Technical Analysis path goes much deeper into indicators and the evidence for and against this kind of analysis.)

Worked example 1 — a trend line's slope

From $40 to $55 over 10 weeks: slope = (55 − 40)/10 = $1.50/week, a positive slope — a simple, quantified way to describe an uptrend, even though the path in between wasn't perfectly straight.

Build it up: smoothing the wiggles with a moving average

A raw trend line only looks at the start and end points — it says nothing about the noisy path in between, and a single bad week can make an otherwise real trend look shaky by eye. A simple moving average (SMA) smooths this out by averaging the last n closes at each point, rolling forward one period at a time. Comparing the current SMA reading to the one from a period earlier gives a cleaner read on direction than eyeballing the raw, jagged closes.

Worked example 2 — a 3-week SMA

Five weekly closes: $40, $42, $41, $44, $47 — notice week 3 dipped before climbing again, exactly the kind of wiggle the opening question asked about. The 3-week SMA ending at week 5 = (41+44+47)/3 = $44.00. The 3-week SMA one week earlier (ending at week 4) = (42+41+44)/3 ≈ $42.33. The SMA itself rose by about $1.67 over that week — confirming the uptrend despite the dip, because the smoothing absorbed the single down week into the average rather than treating it as a reversal.

The formal definition

\text{slope}=\frac{P_1-P_0}{\text{weeks}}\qquad\qquad SMA_n(t)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}P_{t-i}
P₀, P₁price at the start and end of the window
slopepositive = uptrend, negative = downtrend, by this simple measure
SMAₙ(t)the average of the last n closing prices as of period t
Pₜ₋ᵢthe close i periods before period t
Try this
  1. Make the slope (parameter a) negative: the line becomes a downtrend. Set a to 0: a flat, sideways trend.
  2. In the SMA calculator, edit one of the five closes to make the series bounce around more — notice the SMA readings move far less than the raw closes do, since the smoothing absorbs single-week noise.
Common traps
  • A trend line fit to a short window can look convincing and reverse immediately afterward.
  • Support and resistance are observed patterns from past behaviour, not guaranteed floors or ceilings.
  • A moving average lags real price moves — by design, it confirms a trend after it's already underway, not before.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Candlestick charts appear on virtually every trading platform.
  • Support and resistance are referenced constantly in technical-analysis discussions.
  • Moving averages (commonly a 50-period or 200-period SMA) are among the most widely referenced indicators on charting platforms — a shorter average crossing above a longer one is often called a "golden cross," and the reverse a "death cross," in financial media; these are observed patterns, not guarantees.

Check yourself

1. What does a candlestick show for one period, and which part is the "body" vs. the "wicks"?
The open, high, low and close prices for that period; the body spans open to close, and the thin wicks mark the high and low reached along the way.
2. Weekly closes 50, 48, 47, 45, 43. What's the 3-week SMA for the most recent 3 weeks vs. the previous 3, and what trend does that confirm?
Most recent (weeks 3–5): (47+45+43)/3 = 45. Previous (weeks 2–4): (48+47+45)/3 ≈ 46.67. The SMA is falling, confirming a downtrend.
3. Is support a guaranteed floor for the price?
No — it's a level where buyers have stepped in before; it can still be broken, especially if the conditions that created it change.

Takeaways

  • Candlesticks summarise a period's price action at a glance.
  • A trend can be quantified as a slope, and a moving average smooths out short-term noise to confirm the direction more reliably than eyeballing raw closes.
  • Support, resistance and moving averages are all observed patterns from past behaviour, not guarantees about the future.
