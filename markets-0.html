Market types

The questionEquities, bonds, currencies and derivatives all trade in "markets" — are they really the same kind of thing?

Intuition

Each market type has its own participants and purpose: equity markets trade ownership stakes; bond markets trade loans; the FX market trades one currency for another; derivatives markets trade contracts whose value derives from something else. They differ enormously in size and structure.

Worked example

The FX market's daily turnover has been commonly cited (illustrative, order-of-magnitude figures that change over time) at roughly $7.5 trillion/day, versus roughly $0.5 trillion/day for global equity trading — about 15× larger.

\text{ratio}=\frac{\text{Market A volume}}{\text{Market B volume}}
volumevalue of trading activity over a period (here, one day)
Try this
  1. Confirm the ratio ≈ 15, then try equal volumes — ratio becomes 1.
Common traps
  • Treating "the market" as one thing — equity, bond, FX and derivatives markets behave quite differently.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Stock exchanges, the bond market, the FX market and futures exchanges.

Check yourself

1. What does the bond market trade?
Loans — claims on fixed interest and principal repayment.
2. Which market type is commonly cited as the largest by daily turnover?
The FX (currency) market.
3. What does a derivative's value depend on?
Something else — an underlying asset, rate or index.

Takeaways

  • Different market types trade fundamentally different claims.
  • They vary hugely in size and structure.

Exchange vs. OTC

The questionYou want to sell a thinly-traded corporate bond. There's no single "exchange price" — why not?

Intuition

A centralized exchange matches orders publicly, so everyone sees the same best bid/ask. Over-the-counter (OTC) trading is negotiated directly between two parties, so prices can genuinely differ dealer to dealer at the same moment.

Worked example

Three dealer quotes for the same bond: 99.0, 99.2, 99.5 (per $100 face). The spread between the best and worst is 99.5 − 99.0 = $0.50 — real price dispersion that simply doesn't exist on a single, centralized exchange quote.

\text{dispersion}=\text{max quote}-\text{min quote}
Try this
  1. Bring all three quotes closer together: dispersion shrinks toward 0.
Common traps
  • Assuming an OTC instrument has one "true" price at a given moment the way an exchange-listed stock appears to.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • NYSE/Nasdaq (exchange) vs. most bonds and many derivatives (OTC).

Check yourself

1. What's the key structural difference between an exchange and OTC trading?
An exchange matches orders publicly with one visible best price; OTC is negotiated bilaterally and can show different prices from different dealers.
2. Quotes of 50.1, 50.4, 49.9 — what's the dispersion?
50.4 − 49.9 = 0.5.
3. Which is more likely to have OTC-style price dispersion: a mega-cap stock or a thinly-traded bond?
The thinly-traded bond.

Takeaways

  • Exchanges centralize price discovery; OTC trading is negotiated and can disperse.

Market efficiency

The questionIf a stock's next move were truly a coin flip, what would its expected value be — and what does that have to do with "beating the market"?

Intuition

The efficient market hypothesis says prices already reflect available information, in three commonly-discussed forms: weak (reflects past prices — technical patterns shouldn't give an edge), semi-strong (reflects public information — public fundamental analysis shouldn't either), and strong (reflects even private information — generally the least supported form). None of this is fully settled; it's a spectrum of evidence, not a proven law.

Worked example

A coin-flip-like move of +1% or −1% with equal probability: expected value = 0.5×(+1%) + 0.5×(−1%) = 0% — a stylized "fair game" with no predictable edge.

E[\text{gain}]=p\times\text{gain}+(1-p)\times\text{loss}
Try this
  1. Make the probability unequal (e.g. 60/40): expected value moves away from 0 — no longer a "fair game".
Common traps
  • "Efficient" doesn't mean "always correctly priced" — it means hard to exploit consistently, not infallible.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • The case for low-cost index investing (Investment Fundamentals Lesson 2.7) partly rests on an efficiency-based argument.

Check yourself

1. Name the three commonly-discussed forms of market efficiency.
Weak, semi-strong, strong.
2. Does "efficient" mean prices are always correct?
No — it means hard to consistently exploit, not infallible.
3. p=0.5, gain=+2%, loss=−2%. Expected value?
0.5×2 + 0.5×(−2) = 0%.

Takeaways

  • Efficient market hypothesis has weak/semi-strong/strong forms.
  • It's debated evidence, not a settled fact.

Regulators & oversight

The questionWhy would a regulator fine a company for late or inaccurate disclosure, rather than only for actually losing investors money?

Intuition

Securities regulators exist mainly to ensure fair, accurate disclosure and to deter fraud — not to guarantee investment outcomes. Markets work better when everyone has access to the same timely, truthful information.

Worked example

An illustrative penalty structure: a $50,000 base fine plus $5,000 per day a required disclosure is late. Ten days late: 50,000 + 10×5,000 = $100,000.

\text{fine}=\text{base}+\text{per-day}\times\text{days late}
Try this
  1. Raise "days late": the fine grows linearly.
Common traps
  • Regulation reduces certain risks (fraud, unequal information) — it does not eliminate the risk of a bad investment outcome.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • National securities regulators overseeing exchanges and listed companies' disclosures.

Check yourself

1. What is a securities regulator's main job?
Ensuring fair, accurate disclosure and deterring fraud — not guaranteeing investment outcomes.
2. Base fine $20,000, $2,000/day, 5 days late. Total?
20,000 + 5×2,000 = $30,000.
3. Does regulation protect against a company's investments simply doing poorly?
No — it addresses fairness and disclosure, not business or market outcomes.

Takeaways

  • Regulation targets fairness and disclosure, not investment success.

Globalization of markets

The questionCan "the market" really be traded at 2am your local time?

Intuition

As one region's trading session closes, another's opens, so some market somewhere is trading nearly around the clock. Sessions also overlap for a few hours, which tends to be when activity is heaviest.

Worked example

If one region's session runs 03:00–11:30 and another's runs 09:30–16:00 (same time reference), they overlap from 09:30–11:30 — a 2-hour overlap window.

\text{overlap}=\min(\text{end}_1,\text{end}_2)-\max(\text{start}_1,\text{start}_2)
Try this
  1. Move the two sessions apart until they no longer overlap: overlap hours fall to 0.
Common traps
  • "Always open somewhere" doesn't mean every asset is equally liquid at every hour — thin off-peak-hours trading can widen spreads (Investment Fundamentals Lesson 1.6).
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Asian markets closing as European markets open, overlapping with US trading hours.

Check yourself

1. Why is some market "always open"?
Different regions' trading sessions are staggered across time zones, so as one closes another opens.
2. Sessions of 2:00–10:00 and 8:00–16:00. Overlap hours?
min(10,16) − max(2,8) = 10 − 8 = 2 hours.
3. Is liquidity the same at every hour just because some market is open?
No — off-peak hours can be much thinner and less liquid.

Takeaways

  • Global sessions overlap, creating near-continuous trading.
  • "Open" doesn't guarantee equally deep liquidity at every hour.
