Reading a cash flow statement

The questionA company reports $100,000 of net income but $150,000 of operating cash flow. How can cash be higher than profit?

Intuition

The cash flow statement splits cash movements into operating, investing and financing activities. Profit includes non-cash items like depreciation (an expense with no actual cash outflow that period), so operating cash flow adds it back.

Worked example

Net income $100,000 + depreciation add-back $60,000 − working-capital increase $10,000 = $150,000 operating cash flow.

\text{Operating CF}=\text{Net income}+\text{Depreciation}-\Delta\text{Working capital}
Try this
  1. Raise depreciation: operating cash flow rises above net income further.
Common traps
  • A profitable company can still run short of actual cash if profit isn't converting to cash.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Analysts comparing net income to operating cash flow as a quality-of-earnings check.

Check yourself

1. Name the three sections of a cash flow statement.
Operating, investing, financing.
2. Net income $50,000, depreciation $20,000, no working-capital change. Operating CF?
$70,000.
3. Why is depreciation added back to net income?
It's a non-cash expense — it reduces reported profit without an actual cash outflow that period.

Takeaways

  • Operating cash flow can differ from net income due to non-cash items.
  • A gap between the two is worth investigating.

Ratio analysis

The questionA company earns $80,000 on $400,000 of equity and $1,000,000 of total assets. Which figure tells you more about management's efficiency?

Intuition

Return on equity (ROE) measures profit relative to owners' capital; return on assets (ROA) measures it relative to everything the company controls, debt included. ROE can look inflated by heavy borrowing even when the underlying business isn't especially efficient.

Worked example

ROE = 80,000/400,000 = 20%. ROA = 80,000/1,000,000 = 8% — the gap hints at significant debt financing.

ROE=\frac{\text{Net income}}{\text{Equity}}\qquad ROA=\frac{\text{Net income}}{\text{Total assets}}
Try this
  1. Raise total assets with equity fixed: ROA falls, but ROE stays the same — more debt-financed assets, same equity profitability.
Common traps
  • A high ROE driven mainly by heavy debt is a different (riskier) story than one driven by genuine operating efficiency.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • ROE is commonly compared across companies in the same industry.

Check yourself

1. Net income $60,000, equity $300,000. ROE?
20%.
2. Why might ROE be high while ROA is only modest?
Heavy use of debt can inflate ROE relative to ROA, which reflects all assets regardless of financing.
3. Does a high ROE always mean an efficient business?
Not necessarily — check whether it's driven by leverage rather than genuine operating efficiency.

Takeaways

  • ROE and ROA answer related but different questions.
  • A gap between them can signal heavy leverage.

Economic moats

The questionTwo companies both earn high profits today. One can raise prices 10% with little customer loss; the other loses customers at a 2% price rise. Which has the wider moat?

Intuition

An economic moat is a durable advantage — a brand, network effect, high switching costs, or scale — that protects long-run profits from competition. The company that can raise prices without losing much business has pricing power, a classic sign of a moat.

Worked example

If a 10% price rise costs almost no customers, the implied "customer loss per 1% price rise" is near 0; at the other company it's roughly 2% lost per 1% price rise — a rough, illustrative way to compare pricing power.

\text{price sensitivity}\approx\frac{\%\ \text{customers lost}}{\%\ \text{price increase}}
Try this
  1. Raise customer loss for the same price increase: sensitivity rises — weaker pricing power, a narrower moat.
Common traps
  • A moat can narrow over time — past pricing power doesn't guarantee future pricing power.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Companies widely cited for strong brand, network-effect, or switching-cost moats.

Check yourself

1. Name a type of economic moat.
Any of: brand, network effect, switching costs, scale.
2. Which is a sign of pricing power: losing many customers at a small price rise, or losing few?
Losing few customers at a price rise signals stronger pricing power.
3. Can a moat narrow over time?
Yes — competitive advantages can erode; a past moat doesn't guarantee a future one.

Takeaways

  • A moat is a durable competitive advantage.
  • Pricing power is one observable clue to a moat's strength.

Red flags in financials

The questionA company's revenue grows 10% this year, but its accounts receivable (money owed by customers) grows 40%. Should that raise an eyebrow?

Intuition

Receivables growing much faster than revenue can mean the company is booking sales it hasn't actually collected cash for yet — a potential red flag. Other common ones: recurring "one-time" charges, and profit and cash flow trending in opposite directions (Lesson 0.1 in this module).

Worked example

Receivables growth (40%) ÷ revenue growth (10%) = 4× — receivables are growing four times faster than sales, worth investigating.

\text{red-flag ratio}=\frac{\%\ \text{growth in receivables}}{\%\ \text{growth in revenue}}
Try this
  1. Bring receivables growth down toward revenue growth: the ratio approaches 1 — a less worrying pattern.
Common traps
  • One red flag isn't proof of a problem — it's a prompt to look closer, not an automatic conclusion.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Analysts flagging aggressive revenue recognition during due diligence.

Check yourself

1. What might fast-growing receivables relative to revenue suggest?
Sales being booked before cash is actually collected.
2. Revenue +8%, receivables +32%. Ratio?
32/8 = 4×.
3. Does one red flag prove fraud or trouble?
No — it's a prompt for closer investigation, not automatic proof.

Takeaways

  • Red flags are prompts to investigate, not verdicts.
  • Comparing growth rates across statement lines can reveal mismatches.

Qualitative analysis

The questionTwo companies have near-identical financial ratios. Can the numbers alone tell you which is the better investment?

Intuition

Numbers describe the past; qualitative analysis — management's track record and incentives, industry position, governance — helps judge whether the numbers are likely to hold up or improve. The best fundamental analysis combines both.

Worked example

A simple illustrative "confidence score": starting from a 50% baseline, add 10 points for a strong management track record, add 10 for a leading industry position, subtract 15 for weak governance — net score moves with the qualitative inputs, not with any new financial ratio.

\text{score}=\text{base}+\sum(\text{qualitative adjustments})
Try this
  1. Push the governance adjustment very negative: the overall score falls even if nothing in the financials changed.
Common traps
  • Qualitative judgement is inherently more subjective than a ratio — it should supplement, not replace, the numbers.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Management track record and strategy routinely discussed on earnings calls.

Check yourself

1. Name a qualitative factor in fundamental analysis.
Any of: management quality, industry position, governance.
2. Can two companies with identical ratios be different investments?
Yes — qualitative factors can differ even when the numbers look alike.
3. Should qualitative judgement replace ratio analysis?
No — it should supplement it, since it's inherently more subjective.

Takeaways

  • Numbers and qualitative judgement both matter.
  • Identical ratios can still hide very different businesses.
