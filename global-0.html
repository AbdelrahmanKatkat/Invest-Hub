Reading macro news

The questionInflation comes in at 3.5%. Markets jump sharply. Economists had expected 3.2% all along — barely different from the actual number. Three weeks later, a far "worse-looking" 4.0% reading barely moves anything at all. Why?

Intuition

Markets react to the surprise relative to expectations, not the raw number alone. Before almost any scheduled economic release — inflation (CPI), jobs, GDP, central-bank decisions — professional forecasters publish a consensus estimate, and prices already reflect that consensus in advance (Financial Markets Lesson "market-efficiency"). The release itself only moves prices to the extent it differs from what was already priced in.

This is why a "high" inflation reading that was fully expected can barely move markets, while a much smaller miss against expectations can cause a sharp reaction — and why the same headline number can mean opposite things on two different days, depending entirely on what the market had already assumed.

Worked example 1 — the basic surprise

Actual 3.5%, expected 3.2%: surprise = 3.5 − 3.2 = +0.3 percentage points — the part that actually moves prices, not the 3.5% headline by itself.

Build it up: from one release to many — a composite surprise index

A single release's surprise only tells part of the story, and different indicators have very different typical volatility: a 0.3 percentage-point CPI surprise is unusually large, while a 50,000-job surprise on a jobs report with a typical swing of ±70,000 is comparatively minor. To compare surprises of different sizes and units on equal footing, standardize each one into a z-score — the surprise divided by its own typical (historical) standard deviation — then average several z-scores together into one composite reading. This is the same idea behind real, commonly cited gauges like the Citigroup Economic Surprise Index, which aggregates many indicators' standardized surprises into a single number tracking whether data has, on net, been beating or missing expectations lately.

Worked example 2 — building a composite surprise index

Three releases in one week: CPI surprise +0.3pp (typical std dev 0.15pp) → z = 0.3/0.15 = 2.00. Jobs report surprise +50,000 (typical std dev 70,000) → z = 50/70 ≈ 0.71. Retail sales surprise −0.4pp (typical std dev 0.3pp) → z = −0.4/0.3 ≈ −1.33. Composite index = (2.00 + 0.71 − 1.33)/3 ≈ +0.46 — on net, a mild upside surprise across the week's data, even though one of the three releases missed.

\text{Surprise}=\text{Actual}-\text{Expected}\qquad z_i=\frac{\text{Surprise}_i}{\sigma_i}\qquad \text{Composite}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n z_i
Actual, Expectedthe reported figure and the consensus forecast for it
σᵢthe typical (historical) standard deviation of that indicator's surprises — its own unit of "normal" variability
zᵢa standardized surprise, comparable across indicators with different units and typical sizes
Try this
  1. Set actual equal to expected in the first calculator: surprise goes to 0 — in theory, little market reaction from this release alone.
  2. In the composite index calculator, flip retail sales to a positive surprise too: the composite index rises further, reflecting data broadly beating expectations rather than one release standing out.
Common traps
  • Reacting to the headline number without checking what was already expected — the raw figure alone says little.
  • Comparing surprises across different indicators in raw units (percentage points vs. thousands of jobs) instead of standardizing them first.
  • Treating "good news" for the economy as automatically "good news" for markets — a hot number can raise the odds of a rate hike (Economics Lesson "interest-rates"), which can pressure stock and bond prices even though the underlying economy looks strong.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • CPI and jobs reports routinely moving markets based on the surprise versus consensus forecasts, not the absolute reading.
  • Composite data-surprise gauges like the Citigroup Economic Surprise Index, widely referenced in macro commentary as a single read on whether data has been beating or missing expectations lately.
  • Central bank rate-decision odds (implied by futures markets) shifting immediately after a surprising inflation or jobs report.

Check yourself

1. What actually moves markets about a scheduled data release?
The surprise relative to what was already expected (consensus), not just the raw reported number.
2. A jobs report surprise of +20,000 with a typical std dev of 40,000, and a CPI surprise of −0.1pp with a typical std dev of 0.2pp. Z-scores, and which indicator surprised more (in standardized terms)?
Jobs: 20/40 = 0.5. CPI: −0.1/0.2 = −0.5. Same standardized magnitude (0.5), opposite direction — despite very different raw units.
3. Why could a "hot" (strong) economic report sometimes be followed by falling stock prices?
A strong report can raise the odds the central bank hikes (or delays cutting) interest rates, and higher rates can pressure both bond and stock valuations even though the underlying economy looks healthy.

Takeaways

  • Surprise relative to expectations drives the market reaction, not the raw figure.
  • Standardizing surprises into z-scores lets you compare and combine indicators with very different units into one composite reading.
  • "Good" economic news can still pressure markets if it shifts interest-rate expectations.

Earnings season

The questionA company reports EPS of $1.35 when analysts expected $1.20 — a clear "beat." Yet the stock falls 6% the next day. How can beating expectations and falling in price both be true at once?

Intuition

An "earnings beat" is relative to consensus estimates — the average forecast across analysts covering the stock — not some absolute bar. The same actual EPS can be a big beat against one estimate and a miss against a higher one. Four times a year (once per fiscal quarter), companies report actual results against whatever the market had assumed going in; "earnings season" is simply the few concentrated weeks when most companies report, so a large share of the market's expectations get tested against reality all at once.

Worked example 1 — the basic beat

(1.35 − 1.20)/1.20 = +12.5% EPS beat.

Build it up: why a "beat" can still send the stock down

Stock prices are forward-looking — they reflect expectations of future, not just past, earnings (Investment Fundamentals Lesson 0.3's present-value logic, applied to a whole stream of future profits). A quarter's actual result is already in the past by the time it's reported; what the company says about the next quarter — its guidance — often matters more to the stock price than the quarter that just ended. A company can beat last quarter's estimate convincingly and still guide the next quarter's expected earnings below what analysts had been assuming, and the stock reacts to the disappointing future, not the pleasing past.

Worked example 2 — guidance surprise

Analysts had assumed next quarter's EPS would be around $1.50 (the prior consensus). The company's own new guidance instead points to $1.35: guidance surprise = (1.35 − 1.50)/1.50 = −10% — a clear disappointment about the future, even on the same day as a strong past-quarter beat. This "beat but cut guidance" combination can easily send a stock down despite the headline beat. The mirror case also happens: a company that merely meets last quarter's estimate exactly (0% surprise) but raises next quarter's guidance from a $1.00 consensus to $1.08 — a +8% guidance surprise — can see its stock rise, with no "beat" in the backward-looking number at all.

\text{EPS surprise \%}=\frac{\text{Actual}-\text{Estimate}}{\text{Estimate}}\qquad \text{Guidance surprise \%}=\frac{\text{New guidance}-\text{Prior consensus}}{\text{Prior consensus}}
Actual, Estimatereported EPS and the prior consensus forecast for the quarter just ended
New guidance, Prior consensusthe company's own forecast for the next period, vs. what the market had already assumed for it
Try this
  1. Raise the estimate above the actual in the first calculator: the surprise turns negative — a miss, even with the same actual result.
  2. In the guidance calculator, push new guidance above the prior consensus: the surprise turns positive, the "beat and raise" combination markets tend to reward most.
Common traps
  • A single quarter's "beat" or "miss" says little without the context of guidance and the full-year outlook.
  • Assuming the stock reaction always points the same direction as the headline EPS surprise — guidance, revenue, and margins often matter just as much or more.
  • Ignoring how a beat was achieved — one driven by cost-cutting alone reads very differently from one driven by strong revenue growth (Fundamental Analysis path).
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Quarterly earnings reports moving individual stocks and sometimes whole sectors when several related companies report similar surprises in the same week.
  • "Beat and raise" vs. "beat and cut" as commonly discussed shorthand for how a reported quarter and forward guidance combine to move a stock.
  • Aggregate, season-wide beat rates (the share of S&P 500 companies beating estimates in a given quarter) reported in financial media as a read on corporate health broadly.

Check yourself

1. What is an "earnings beat" relative to?
Consensus analyst estimates for that specific quarter, not an absolute number.
2. Actual EPS $2.00, estimate $1.80. Surprise %?
(2.00-1.80)/1.80 ≈ 11.1%.
3. A company beats this quarter's EPS estimate but guides next quarter's EPS to $0.95 against a $1.00 prior consensus. Guidance surprise, and why might the stock still fall?
(0.95-1.00)/1.00 = −5%. Because prices are forward-looking, a disappointing outlook for the next period can outweigh a backward-looking beat on the quarter that already happened.

Takeaways

  • A beat or miss is always relative to an estimate, never an absolute bar.
  • Guidance (the forward-looking forecast) often moves a stock more than the backward-looking quarter that just reported.
  • How a beat was achieved — revenue growth vs. cost-cutting — adds context a single surprise number can't capture.

Geopolitics & markets

The questionA country's bonds normally yield 4%. Rising political instability pushes buyers to demand more. Separately, an energy stock falls 3.5% on the day a conflict breaks out in an oil-producing region — even though the broad market that same day only fell 1%. How would you tell how much of that move was really "about" the event, rather than just the ordinary market-wide decline?

Intuition

Political risk can add a required-return premium — investors demand extra compensation for added uncertainty, the same spread logic as Asset Classes Lesson "fixed-income-types," just driven by politics instead of credit quality. It rarely moves every asset by the same amount: a currency, a sovereign bond, and a handful of sensitive sectors (commonly energy and defense) tend to react far more than the broad market as a whole, because the specific risk is concentrated in them.

Worked example 1 — the risk premium

Baseline yield 4% + political risk premium 1.5% = required yield 5.5%.

Build it up: separating the event's effect from an ordinary market-wide move

The opening question's second half is a real, widely used technique called an event study. On any ordinary day, a stock is expected to move roughly in line with the broad market, scaled by its own sensitivity (beta): a stock with beta 1.2 is expected to move about 1.2% for every 1% the market moves, in either direction. The abnormal return is simply what's left over after subtracting that "expected, market-driven" move from what actually happened — the part plausibly caused by the specific event, not general market conditions that day.

Worked example 2 — abnormal return around an event

An energy stock has beta 1.2. The broad market falls 1% on the day a conflict breaks out: expected move = 1.2 × (−1%) = −1.2%. The stock's actual return that day is −3.5%. Abnormal return = −3.5% − (−1.2%) = −2.3% — the part of the move plausibly attributable to the event itself, roughly twice as large as what the stock's ordinary market sensitivity alone would predict.

\text{Required yield}=\text{Baseline yield}+\text{Risk premium}\qquad \text{Abnormal return}=R_{\text{actual}}-\beta\times R_{\text{market}}
β (beta)a stock's typical sensitivity to broad market moves
R_market, R_actualthe market's return and the stock's own actual return over the same period
Try this
  1. Raise the political risk premium in the first calculator: required yield rises, and (per Economics Lesson "interest-rates") bond prices would fall to deliver it.
  2. In the abnormal-return calculator, set the actual return equal to beta × market return exactly: abnormal return goes to 0 — the stock just moved with the market, with no event-specific effect detected.
Common traps
  • Geopolitical effects are often sector- or country-specific, not a blanket move across every asset.
  • Attributing a stock's entire daily move to a headline event without first subtracting what ordinary market-wide movement (scaled by beta) would already explain.
  • Assuming an elevated risk premium is permanent — premiums embedded in yields and prices can fade as quickly as they appeared once uncertainty resolves.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Energy and defense sectors reacting more sharply than the broad market to specific geopolitical events.
  • Sovereign bond spreads (the gap over a benchmark, safer government's yield) widening for countries seen as politically riskier.
  • Event-study methodology used by researchers (and some analysts) to isolate a specific announcement's effect from everyday market noise.

Check yourself

1. How does political risk typically show up in bond yields?
As an added risk premium on top of the baseline yield.
2. Baseline 3%, premium 2%. Required yield?
5%.
3. A stock with beta 0.8 moves +9% on a day the market rises 2%. Expected return, abnormal return, and what does the abnormal return suggest?
Expected = 0.8×2% = 1.6%. Abnormal = 9% − 1.6% = 7.4% — a large positive move well beyond what the market alone would predict, suggesting a stock-specific (or sector-specific) event drove most of the gain.

Takeaways

  • Political risk adds a required-return premium, like a credit spread.
  • Effects are usually specific to a sector, country or currency, not universal.
  • An event study (actual return minus beta-scaled market return) isolates the event-specific move from ordinary market-wide noise.

Currency & FX basics

The question$1 buys €0.92. You convert $1,000. How many euros do you get — and if a trader could borrow at 1% in one currency and invest at 5% in another, why wouldn't that free 4-percentage-point gain be a sure thing?

Intuition

An exchange rate converts one currency into another, directly feeding Asset Classes Lesson "international-assets"'s currency-adjusted return. Interest-rate differentials between countries relate to carry — the incentive to borrow in a low-rate currency and hold a higher-rate one — but carry trades add currency risk on top of the rate differential, and that currency risk can easily overwhelm it.

Worked example 1 — a simple conversion

$1,000 × 0.92 = €920.

Build it up: why the "free" rate gap usually isn't free

Borrowing at 1% to invest at 5% looks like a guaranteed 4-percentage-point gain — until the currency you've invested in moves against you before you convert back. A currency's value can swing by several percentage points over a year for reasons that have nothing to do with the interest-rate gap itself, and that move applies to the entire position, not just the rate differential.

Worked example 2 — a carry trade that loses money

Borrow at 1%, invest at 5% — a 4-point rate gap. But the invested currency depreciates 6% against the borrowed currency over the year: net result ≈ (1.05 × (1 − 0.06)) − 1.01 = 0.987 − 1.01 = −2.3% — a real loss, despite the seemingly attractive 4-point rate advantage, because the currency move (−6%) was larger than the rate gap it was meant to capture.

Over long horizons, exchange rates also tend to track something more fundamental than any single trade's rate gap: relative inflation. Purchasing power parity (PPP) is the idea that, over the long run, a currency with persistently higher inflation should depreciate against one with lower inflation — roughly by the gap between the two inflation rates (Economics Lesson "inflation") — so that goods don't become permanently cheaper or more expensive in one country relative to the other purely because of currency alone.

Worked example 3 — PPP-implied currency move

Home inflation 2%, foreign inflation 5%: PPP-implied move = (1.05/1.02) − 1 ≈ +2.94% — the foreign currency would be expected to depreciate against the home currency by roughly this much over the period, to offset its higher inflation. (PPP is a long-run tendency seen in the data, not a precise short-term predictor — actual currency moves can and do diverge from it for extended stretches.)

\text{Converted amount}=\text{Amount}\times\text{Exchange rate}\qquad \text{PPP-implied move}=\frac{1+\text{inflation}_{\text{foreign}}}{1+\text{inflation}_{\text{home}}}-1
inflation_home, inflation_foreignthe two countries' inflation rates over the same period (Economics Lesson "inflation")
Try this
  1. Raise the exchange rate in the first calculator: the same dollar amount converts to more of the other currency.
  2. In the carry-trade calculator, shrink the depreciation toward 0%: the net result rises back toward the full 4-point rate gap — currency risk is what was eating into it.
  3. In the PPP calculator, set both inflation rates equal: the implied currency move goes to exactly 0% — no inflation gap, no PPP-implied drift.
Common traps
  • A carry trade's extra yield can be wiped out (or worse) by an adverse currency move — the rate gap alone says nothing about the risk taken to earn it.
  • Treating purchasing power parity as a precise short-term forecasting tool rather than a long-run tendency that can diverge from actual rates for years at a time.
  • Forgetting that currency risk cuts both ways — it can just as easily boost a foreign investment's return as erode it.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Converting a foreign investment's return back to a home currency (Asset Classes Lesson "international-assets").
  • Currency carry trades between low-rate and high-rate currencies, a well-known (and well-studied) strategy with real currency risk attached.
  • Long-run currency valuation models and indices (such as "Big Mac"-style PPP comparisons) referenced in macro commentary as a loose gauge of whether a currency looks over- or under-valued.

Check yourself

1. $500 converted at a rate of 0.85. Result?
€425.
2. Borrow at 2%, invest at 6% (a 4-point gap), but the invested currency depreciates 3%. Approximate net return?
(1.06 × 0.97) − 1.02 ≈ 1.0282 − 1.02 = 0.82% — still a (small) gain here, because the 3% depreciation was smaller than the 4-point rate gap, unlike the lesson's −6% example.
3. Home inflation 3%, foreign inflation 8%. PPP-implied currency move?
(1.08/1.03) − 1 ≈ 4.85% — the foreign currency would be expected to depreciate by roughly this much against the home currency over the long run.

Takeaways

  • Exchange rates directly convert returns between currencies.
  • Carry trades add currency risk on top of the rate differential, and that risk can exceed the gap it was meant to capture.
  • Purchasing power parity links currency moves to inflation differentials over the long run — a tendency, not a precise forecast.

Signal vs. noise

The questionIf you reacted to every market headline, how often would you actually be changing your long-term plan for a good reason — and is there a concrete way to separate the noisy day-to-day wiggle from an actual change in direction?

Intuition

Most daily headlines are noise relative to a long-term plan; a smaller number carry genuine signal. A disciplined, scheduled review process (Investment Fundamentals Module 4) filters this, instead of reacting to every story as it breaks. The challenge is that noise and signal arrive mixed together in the same stream of daily numbers — there's no label on a headline saying which one it is.

Worked example 1 — an illustrative signal rate

If roughly 1 in 20 headlines genuinely changes a reasonable long-term outlook, that's a 5% signal rate — a rough, illustrative way to frame "most of it is noise," not a precise measured statistic.

Build it up: smoothing a noisy series to see the underlying trend

One concrete, widely used technique for separating signal from noise in a numerical series (rather than a stream of headlines) is a moving average — replacing each day's raw, jumpy value with the average of it and the last few days, so short-lived wiggles partly cancel out while a genuine, sustained move still shows through (Technical Analysis Lesson "moving-averages" explores this further for price charts specifically). The raw day-to-day changes can look sharp and erratic even when the underlying trend barely moved.

Worked example 2 — a 3-day moving average

Five days of a noisy index-like series: 100, 104, 97, 102, 99. Day-to-day changes are jumpy: +4, −7, +5, −3. The 3-day moving average ending each of the last three days: (100+104+97)/3 ≈ 100.33, (104+97+102)/3 = 101.00, (97+102+99)/3 ≈ 99.33. The smoothed series' changes (+0.67, then −1.67) are far gentler than the raw day-to-day swings — most of the sharp daily movement was noise that mostly cancels out, not a real change in trend.

\text{signal rate}=\frac{\text{headlines that genuinely matter}}{\text{total headlines}}\qquad \text{MA}_t=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}x_{t-i}
MAₜthe moving average ending at day t
kthe window length (here, 3 days)
x_{t-i}the raw value i days before day t
Try this
  1. Raise the number of headlines with the "genuinely matters" count fixed: the signal rate falls — more noise diluting the same signal.
  2. In the moving-average calculator, make all five values closer together: the three moving averages converge toward each other too — less noise to smooth away in the first place.
Common traps
  • Treating every headline as equally actionable is a recipe for the behavioural mistakes covered in Behavioral Finance.
  • Confusing a smoothed series' stability for certainty — a moving average reduces noise, it doesn't eliminate the real possibility the trend itself later changes.
  • Over-smoothing: a very long moving-average window can hide a genuine, meaningful change in trend for too long, trading one problem (noise) for another (slow reaction).
Where it shows up in real investing
  • A written, scheduled review process vs. reacting to every news cycle (Investment Fundamentals Module 4).
  • 50-day and 200-day moving averages widely discussed in market commentary as smoothed trend lines (Technical Analysis Lesson "moving-averages").
  • Central bank commentary that explicitly looks through short-term "noisy" data releases to focus on underlying, multi-month trends.

Check yourself

1. Is most daily financial news signal or noise, relative to a long-term plan?
Mostly noise.
2. What defends against over-reacting to noise, both in headlines and in smoothing a numeric series?
A disciplined, scheduled review process instead of reacting to every headline, and — for numeric data — tools like a moving average that reduce the influence of any single noisy data point.
3. Four days of values 50, 56, 49, 53 — what's the 3-day moving average ending on the last two days, and what does comparing them to the raw day-to-day changes show?
MA ending day 3: (50+56+49)/3 ≈ 51.67. MA ending day 4: (56+49+53)/3 ≈ 52.67. The raw changes (+6, −7, +4) are much choppier than the smoothed change (+1.0), illustrating how much of the daily movement was noise rather than a real shift in level.

Takeaways

  • Most headlines are noise relative to a long-term plan.
  • A scheduled process filters signal from noise better than constant reaction.
  • A moving average is a concrete tool for smoothing noisy numeric data so an underlying trend is easier to see.
