Order types in depth

The questionA stock is trading around $50. Should you always just click "buy now"?

Intuition

A market order says "fill me immediately at the best available price" — fast, but the exact price isn't guaranteed. A limit order says "only fill me at my price or better" — the price is guaranteed, the fill is not. A stop order sits dormant until a trigger price is touched, then becomes a market order — commonly used to cap a loss or lock in a gain, but once triggered it inherits a market order's "price not guaranteed" behaviour.

Worked example

You place a market order on a stock quoted near $50; it fills at $50.10 after a brief price move. A limit order capped at $50.00 would have either filled at $50.00 or not filled at all. On 100 shares, the market order cost 100 × ($50.10 − $50.00) = $10 more.

The formal definition

\text{extra cost}=\text{shares}\times(\text{fill price}-\text{limit price})
fill pricethe price a market order actually executes at
limit pricethe price a limit order was capped at
Try this
  1. Raise the fill price while keeping the limit price fixed: the extra cost of using a market order grows.
Common traps
  • A stop order's trigger price is not a guaranteed fill price — in a fast-moving market it can execute far from where it was set.
  • A limit order can simply never fill if the market never reaches it.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Stop-loss orders used to cap downside during a sharp sell-off.
  • Limit orders used around earnings announcements, when prices often gap sharply.

Check yourself

1. Which order type guarantees a fill, and which guarantees a price?
A market order is very likely to fill but not at a guaranteed price; a limit order guarantees a price (or better) but may not fill.
2. Once a stop order triggers, what does it become?
A market order — so it inherits "fills immediately, price not guaranteed".
3. Why might a stop-loss not protect you from a 15% overnight gap down?
It only triggers when the market trades at that price; an overnight gap can skip straight past the trigger, so the order fills well below it.

Takeaways

  • Market orders prioritise speed; limit orders prioritise price.
  • Stop orders become market orders once triggered.
  • Neither order type guarantees both price and fill at once.

Bid-ask spread & liquidity

The questionDoes it matter whether you trade a stock with a $0.02 spread or one with a $0.50 spread?

Intuition

The bid is the best price a buyer currently offers; the ask is the best price a seller currently offers. The gap between them — the spread — is paid simply by trading: buy at the ask, sell at the bid. Liquidity describes how easily a reasonably large order trades without moving the price; thin, illiquid markets tend to have wider spreads and more price impact ("slippage") per trade.

Worked example

Bid $49.95, ask $50.05 → spread $0.10. Buying then selling 200 shares costs roughly 200 × $0.10 = $20, about 0.2% of a $10,000 trade — a real, if small, cost before any price movement at all.

The formal definition

\text{round-trip cost}\approx \text{shares}\times(\text{ask}-\text{bid})
bid / askbest current buy / sell price
round-tripbuying then later selling the same position
Try this
  1. Widen the spread and watch the round-trip cost and its percentage of trade value both grow.
Common traps
  • A "cheap-looking" thinly-traded stock can be expensive to actually trade once the spread is counted.
  • Spread costs compound with trading frequency — frequent in-and-out trading pays the spread every time.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Large, heavily-traded stocks typically have spreads of a few cents; thinly-traded micro-cap stocks can have spreads of several percent of the price.

Check yourself

1. What is the bid-ask spread, in one sentence?
The gap between the best available buy price (bid) and sell price (ask).
2. A stock has bid $19.90, ask $20.10. What's the round-trip cost on 50 shares?
50 × ($20.10 − $19.90) = 50 × $0.20 = $10.
3. Why do illiquid stocks tend to have wider spreads?
Fewer participants are offering to trade, so market makers widen the spread to compensate for the extra risk and difficulty of finding the other side of a trade.

Takeaways

  • The spread is a real, built-in cost of trading, separate from any price movement.
  • Liquidity and spread width tend to move together.
  • Frequent trading pays the spread repeatedly.
1.3

Exchanges & indices

The questionA company sells new shares to the public for the first time at $20 each, raising money for itself. Later, investors trade those shares at $35. Does the company get that extra $15 per share?

Intuition

The primary market is where a company sells new securities directly and receives the proceeds (an IPO, for example). The secondary market — what people usually mean by "the stock market" or "an exchange" — is investors trading existing shares among themselves; the company isn't a party to those trades and doesn't receive the money. A company's market capitalization is its price times total shares, but the shares actually available to trade (excluding large insider or founder stakes) are the float, and major indices typically weight by float-adjusted market cap (recall the price-weighted vs. cap-weighted comparison from Lesson 0.8).

Worked example

A company trades at $40 with 100 million total shares (market cap $40 × 100M = $4.0B), but insiders hold 40 million of those shares. Float = 60 million shares, so the float-adjusted cap used by many indices is $40 × 60M = $2.4B — 40% smaller than the headline figure.

The formal definition

\text{Market cap}=P\times S \qquad\qquad \text{Float-adjusted cap}=P\times(S-S_{\text{insider}})
Pshare price
Stotal shares outstanding
S_insidershares held by insiders/founders, not freely tradable
Try this
  1. Raise insider-held shares toward the total share count: float-adjusted cap shrinks toward zero even though the headline market cap is unchanged.
Common traps
  • A "market cap" headline figure usually uses total shares, not the float that indices actually weight by.
  • Secondary-market trading does not send money to the company, no matter how high the price goes.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • NYSE and Nasdaq host both IPOs (primary market) and ongoing trading (secondary market).
  • Major cap-weighted indices commonly use float-adjusted weighting, which matters most for companies with large insider stakes.

Check yourself

1. In which market does a company actually receive money from selling shares?
The primary market (e.g. an IPO) — not the secondary market, where investors trade existing shares among themselves.
2. A company has price $25, 200M total shares, 150M insider-held. What is its float-adjusted cap?
25 × (200M − 150M) = 25 × 50M = $1.25B.
3. Why do float-adjusted and headline market cap diverge most for founder-heavy companies?
A large insider stake removes a big chunk of shares from the float, so float-adjusted cap can be much smaller than price × total shares.

Takeaways

  • Primary market = company raises money; secondary market = investors trade among themselves.
  • Market cap = price × shares; float-adjusted cap excludes insider-held shares.
  • Indices commonly weight by float-adjusted cap, not headline market cap.
1.4

Brokers & account types

The questionTwo accounts each hold $10,000 of the same stock. One paid for it entirely in cash; the other borrowed half the purchase price. Do they behave the same way?

Intuition

A cash account only lets you buy what you've actually paid for. A margin account lets you borrow part of the purchase price from the broker, using your securities as collateral — this amplifies both gains and losses, and the broker charges interest on the borrowed amount regardless of how the position performs. Separately, a taxable brokerage account is flexible but offers no special tax treatment, while a tax-advantaged retirement account trades some flexibility (contribution limits, withdrawal rules) for better tax treatment of the gains inside it.

Worked example

Borrowing $5,000 on margin at an 8% annual rate for 3 months costs 5000 × 0.08 × (3/12) = $100 in interest — due whether the stock went up, down, or sideways.

The formal definition

\text{Interest}=\text{Principal}\times r\times\frac{\text{months}}{12}
Principalthe amount borrowed on margin
rthe annual margin interest rate
Try this
  1. Double the borrowed amount: the interest cost doubles, independent of how the stock performs.
Common traps
  • Margin amplifies losses exactly as much as gains — a given percentage drop in the stock produces a larger percentage drop in your own equity.
  • Margin interest accrues even on a losing position.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Margin calls, where a broker demands more collateral or force-sells a position, tend to cluster during sharp downturns.
  • Tax-advantaged retirement accounts are a common vehicle for the compounding benefit discussed in Lesson 0.1.

Check yourself

1. What does a margin account let you do that a cash account doesn't?
Borrow part of the purchase price from the broker, using your securities as collateral.
2. Interest cost on $8,000 borrowed at 6% for 6 months?
8000 × 0.06 × (6/12) = $240.
3. Does margin interest depend on whether the position is profitable?
No — it accrues on the borrowed amount regardless of the position's performance.

Takeaways

  • Margin borrowing amplifies both gains and losses.
  • Margin interest is owed regardless of performance.
  • Account type (cash/margin, taxable/tax-advantaged) is a separate choice from what you actually buy.
1.5

Clearing & settlement

The questionYou sell a stock today. Can you spend the cash tomorrow?

Intuition

A trade has a trade date (when you and the other side agree on price and quantity) and a later settlement date (when ownership and cash actually change hands). In between, a clearinghouse guarantees both sides will perform, even if one party were to default. Many equity markets today commonly settle one business day after the trade (often written "T+1").

Worked example

You sell on a Monday with T+1 settlement: the trade settles Tuesday (assuming no holiday), and that's when the cash is formally yours to withdraw or reinvest without timing restriction.

The formal definition

\text{Settlement date}=\text{Trade date}+N\text{ business days}
Nthe settlement cycle length (e.g. 1 for "T+1")
Try this
  1. Change N and see how many business days pass before funds are considered settled.
Common traps
  • A sale isn't instantly "final" the moment you click — the clearinghouse is still completing the exchange until settlement.
  • Some account types restrict rapidly reusing unsettled proceeds for new trades.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • US equities moved from a T+2 to a T+1 settlement cycle, shortening the gap between trade and settlement.

Check yourself

1. What is the difference between a trade date and a settlement date?
The trade date is when the deal is agreed; the settlement date is when ownership and cash actually change hands.
2. What does a clearinghouse do?
Guarantees both sides of a trade will perform, reducing the risk that one party defaults before settlement.
3. Under T+1, if you sell on a Wednesday, when does it settle (no holidays)?
Thursday — one business day later.

Takeaways

  • Trade date and settlement date are different moments.
  • A clearinghouse guarantees both sides of a trade.
  • Shorter settlement cycles reduce the time counterparty risk exists.
1.6

Market hours & circuit breakers

The questionA company reports bad earnings right after the regular trading session ends, with its stock at $50. What happens to the price overnight?

Intuition

Regular trading hours are when the bulk of volume and the tightest spreads occur; extended hours (pre-market and after-hours) exist but typically have much thinner liquidity and wider spreads. Because news doesn't wait for the market to open, prices can gap between one session's close and the next session's open. Circuit breakers are temporary, market-wide trading halts triggered by very large, fast index declines, meant to give the market time to find an orderly price rather than free-fall.

Worked example

The stock closes regular hours at $50; after-hours news pushes it to $47 — a gap of (47 − 50)/50 = −6% before the next regular session even opens. A stop order set at $49 for the regular session would not have triggered during the after-hours move and would not have protected against this gap.

The formal definition

\text{gap \%}=\frac{P_{\text{new}}-P_{\text{old}}}{P_{\text{old}}}
P_oldthe prior session's closing price
P_newthe price at or near the next session's open
Try this
  1. Set the new price below the old price and watch the gap go negative; above it, positive.
Common traps
  • A stop order does not protect against a gap that jumps straight past its trigger price.
  • Extended-hours trading usually has far less liquidity than the regular session, so the same order can move the price much more.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Earnings reported after the close routinely cause a gapped opening the next session.
  • Market-wide circuit breakers have historically triggered during unusually fast, sharp index declines.

Check yourself

1. Why can a stock "gap" between sessions?
News or events can occur while the regular session is closed, and the next session's price reflects that information all at once rather than gradually.
2. A stock gaps from $80 to $86. What's the gap percentage?
(86 − 80)/80 = 7.5%.
3. What is the purpose of a circuit breaker?
To pause trading temporarily after a very large, fast decline, giving the market time to reassess rather than trading in a disorderly free-fall.

Takeaways

  • Prices can gap between sessions when news arrives while markets are closed.
  • Extended-hours trading has thinner liquidity than the regular session.
  • Circuit breakers pause trading after extreme, fast moves.
