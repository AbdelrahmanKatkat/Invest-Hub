Equity styles

The questionTwo stocks, same industry: one trades at a P/E of 35, the other at 12. Are they playing the same game?

Intuition

Growth companies are expected to expand earnings quickly and typically trade at higher multiples (recall P/E, Investment Fundamentals Lesson 3.3); value companies trade cheaper relative to current earnings or assets, often because growth is expected to be slower. Large-cap vs. small-cap is a separate split, by company size.

Worked example

35/12 ≈ 2.9× — the growth stock is priced at nearly three times the value stock's earnings multiple.

\text{multiple ratio}=\frac{P/E_{\text{growth}}}{P/E_{\text{value}}}
Try this
  1. Bring the two P/Es together: the ratio approaches 1 — the "style gap" narrows.
Common traps
  • A high multiple isn't automatically "overpriced" if growth is genuinely fast; a low multiple isn't automatically "cheap" (Lesson 3.3's trap again).
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Growth-heavy vs. value-heavy index funds track this exact split.

Check yourself

1. Which style typically trades at a higher earnings multiple?
Growth.
2. P/E 40 vs. P/E 10 — ratio?
4×.
3. Is large-cap vs. small-cap the same split as growth vs. value?
No — it's a separate dimension, by company size rather than valuation style.

Takeaways

  • Growth vs. value is about valuation and growth expectations.
  • Large vs. small cap is a separate, size-based split.

Fixed-income types

The questionA government bond yields 4%. A similar-maturity corporate bond yields 5.5%. Why the gap?

Intuition

Government, corporate, municipal and high-yield bonds mainly differ in who owes the money and how likely they are to pay it back. A riskier borrower must offer a higher yield to attract lenders — the extra yield is the credit spread.

Worked example

5.5% − 4% = 1.5 percentage points (150 basis points) of credit spread, compensating for the corporate issuer's higher default risk relative to the government.

\text{Credit spread}=\text{Corporate yield}-\text{Government yield}
Try this
  1. Raise the corporate yield: the spread widens — the market is pricing in more perceived risk.
Common traps
  • "High-yield" is a polite name for higher default risk, not a free source of extra return.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Credit spreads widening during stress periods as perceived default risk rises.

Check yourself

1. What does a credit spread compensate a lender for?
The extra risk of the borrower not paying as promised.
2. Government yield 3%, corporate 4.8%. Spread?
1.8 percentage points.
3. Is "high-yield" bond return extra free money?
No — it compensates for materially higher default risk.

Takeaways

  • Bond types mainly differ by borrower and default risk.
  • Credit spread is the market's price for that extra risk.

Alternative assets

The questionA private fund charges "2 and 20". On $1,000,000 committed with $200,000 of profit above the hurdle, what does that actually cost?

Intuition

Private equity, hedge funds and collectibles trade liquidity and transparency (you often can't sell quickly or see a daily price) for potentially different return drivers than public markets. "2 and 20" means a 2% annual management fee plus 20% of profits above a hurdle.

Worked example

Management fee: 2% × $1,000,000 = $20,000. Performance fee: 20% × $200,000 = $40,000. Total: $60,000 for the year.

\text{Total fee}=\text{mgmt \%}\times\text{committed}+\text{carry \%}\times\text{profit above hurdle}
Try this
  1. Raise the profit above hurdle: the performance fee grows proportionally; the management fee doesn't.
Common traps
  • Illiquidity isn't automatically compensated by better returns — it's a real cost of flexibility given up.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • "2 and 20" is a widely-referenced (though not universal) private-fund fee convention.

Check yourself

1. What does "2 and 20" mean?
A 2% annual management fee plus 20% of profits above a hurdle.
2. $500,000 committed, 2% mgmt fee, $100,000 profit above hurdle, 20% carry. Total fee?
0.02×500,000 + 0.20×100,000 = 10,000 + 20,000 = $30,000.
3. Does illiquidity guarantee a higher return?
No — it's a cost (reduced flexibility), not a guaranteed benefit.

Takeaways

  • Alternatives trade liquidity/transparency for potentially different return drivers.
  • "2 and 20" is a common fee structure, not free money for the manager-less investor.

International assets

The questionA foreign stock returns 10% in its local currency, which then falls 4% against your home currency. What's your actual return?

Intuition

International investing (across developed and emerging markets) adds a currency-movement component on top of the local-market return. A weaker foreign currency subtracts from your home-currency return; a stronger one adds to it.

Worked example

(1.10 × 0.96) − 1 ≈ 5.6% — noticeably below the 10% local return, purely from currency.

R_{\text{home}}=(1+R_{\text{local}})\times(1+R_{\text{fx}})-1
R_fxthe foreign currency's return against your home currency (negative if it weakens)
Try this
  1. Make the currency move positive (strengthen): home-currency return exceeds the local return.
Common traps
  • Forgetting the currency leg entirely when comparing a foreign return to a domestic one.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • A US investor's return on a foreign stock depends on both the stock and the exchange rate.

Check yourself

1. Local return 8%, currency +2%. Home-currency return?
(1.08×1.02)−1 ≈ 10.2%.
2. Does a weakening foreign currency help or hurt a foreign investment's home-currency return?
Hurts it, all else equal.
3. Developed vs. emerging markets — which split is this?
A split by how established and liquid a country's markets and institutions are.

Takeaways

  • International returns combine the local return and a currency return.
  • Currency moves can meaningfully help or hurt the final result.

Inflation-protected assets

The questionA regular bond's coupon is fixed in dollars. What's different about a bond whose principal adjusts with inflation?

Intuition

An inflation-linked bond adjusts its principal with a price index (like CPI), so the coupon payment (a fixed rate applied to that adjusted principal) rises with inflation too — directly addressing the purchasing-power erosion from Lesson 0.2.

Worked example

$1,000 principal, 3% inflation adjustment: new principal = 1,000 × 1.03 = $1,030. A 2% coupon then applies to $1,030, not the original $1,000.

\text{Adjusted principal}=\text{Principal}\times(1+\text{inflation})
Try this
  1. Raise inflation: the adjusted principal (and thus the coupon in dollar terms) rises with it.
Common traps
  • Inflation-linked bonds still carry interest-rate risk — inflation protection isn't the same as price stability.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Inflation-linked government bonds (e.g. TIPS-style securities).

Check yourself

1. What adjusts on an inflation-linked bond?
The principal, based on a price index like CPI.
2. Principal $2,000, inflation 4%. Adjusted principal?
2,000 × 1.04 = $2,080.
3. Does inflation protection eliminate interest-rate risk?
No — the bond's price can still move with interest rates, same as Lesson 2.2's bonds lesson.

Takeaways

  • Inflation-linked bonds adjust principal with a price index.
  • They still carry interest-rate risk, just not inflation-erosion risk.
