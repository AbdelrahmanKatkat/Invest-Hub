Value investing

The questionYou estimate a company is worth $80 a share based on its future cash flows. It trades at $55. A second analyst, using a completely different method — comparing price to the company's accounting net worth — says the stock also looks cheap. How much of a buffer does the first estimate give you if you're wrong, and why does a second, independent check matter?

Intuition

Value investing means buying below an estimated intrinsic value — what a business is really worth based on the cash it can generate — with a margin of safety: the gap between that estimate and the price you actually pay, acting as a cushion against being wrong.

  • No intrinsic-value estimate is exact — it depends on assumptions about future cash flows, growth and the discount rate (Investment Fundamentals Lesson 0.3's present value). A margin of safety doesn't make the estimate more accurate; it makes being somewhat wrong survivable.
  • The bigger the uncertainty in the estimate, the larger a margin of safety a careful investor typically wants before acting on it.
  • A cheap price alone isn't the same as cheap relative to value — a stock can fall 50% and still be expensive if the business's prospects have deteriorated even more (a "value trap").
Worked example 1 — margin of safety

(80 − 55)/80 = 31.25% margin of safety: the estimate could be wrong by roughly that much before the "value exceeds price" conclusion flips.

\text{Margin of safety}=\frac{\text{Intrinsic value}-\text{Price}}{\text{Intrinsic value}}

Build it up: a second, independent anchor

Relying on one valuation estimate means relying on one set of assumptions. Investment Fundamentals Lesson 3.3 introduced the price-to-book (P/B) ratio — price divided by a company's accounting net worth per share — as a second anchor that doesn't depend on forecasting future cash flows at all. When a cash-flow estimate and a cheap P/B agree, that's a stronger (though still not certain) signal than either alone.

Worked example 2 — a second anchor

Book value per share is $44; price is $55: P/B = 55/44 = 1.25. If comparable companies in the same industry typically trade near 2.5× book, this stock looks cheap on both the cash-flow estimate and the book-value anchor — two independent checks pointing the same way.

Intrinsic valuean estimate of what the business is really worth, usually from discounted future cash flows
Pricewhat the market currently charges for one share
P/Bprice ÷ book value per share — a cash-flow-free cross-check
Try this
  1. Bring the price close to the estimated value: margin of safety shrinks toward 0.
  2. In the P/B calculator, raise the price while holding book value fixed: P/B climbs — at some point the "cheap on book value" signal disappears even if the cash-flow estimate still looks attractive.
Common traps
  • An intrinsic-value estimate is still an estimate — the margin of safety cushions estimation error, it doesn't eliminate it.
  • A "value trap": a stock that looks statistically cheap (low P/E or P/B) because the underlying business is genuinely deteriorating, not because the market is mispricing it.
  • Agreement between two valuation anchors reduces the chance both are wrong for the same reason, but it doesn't guarantee either one is right.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Classic value-investing commentary about buying "a dollar for fifty cents."
  • Value-oriented mutual funds and ETFs that screen for low P/E or low P/B stocks relative to their sector.
  • Bank and insurance-company analysis, where P/B is especially commonly used alongside (or instead of) cash-flow models (Investment Fundamentals Lesson 3.3).

Check yourself

1. What is margin of safety, and what does it protect against?
The gap between an estimated intrinsic value and the price paid — it cushions against the estimate itself being wrong, it doesn't make the estimate more accurate.
2. Intrinsic value $100, price $70. Margin of safety? If book value per share is $50, what's the P/B?
Margin of safety = (100-70)/100 = 30%. P/B = 70/50 = 1.4.
3. A stock's price has fallen 60% and its P/E looks very low. Is it automatically a good value investment?
Not necessarily — if the business's earnings power has fallen even more than the price, it can be a "value trap": statistically cheap but not actually cheap relative to its (worsened) true value.

Takeaways

  • Value investing buys below an estimated intrinsic worth, using margin of safety as a cushion against estimation error.
  • A second, independent anchor like P/B reduces (but never removes) reliance on one set of assumptions.
  • A statistically cheap price is not the same as a cheap price relative to a deteriorating business — that's a value trap.

Growth investing

The questionA stock trades at a P/E of 30 (Investment Fundamentals Lesson 3.3) and is expected to grow earnings 20% a year. Is that multiple "expensive" — and does the answer change if you're willing to hold for several years rather than judge the price today?

Intuition

Growth investing accepts a higher P/E in exchange for faster expected earnings growth — the bet is that today's "expensive-looking" price will look reasonable once earnings catch up. The PEG ratio relates the multiple to the growth rate as a rough cross-check:

  • A stock with a high P/E and very high expected growth can have the same PEG as a low-P/E, low-growth stock — PEG tries to put "expensive" and "cheap" on a common footing.
  • PEG is only as reliable as the growth forecast feeding it — a popular, widely expected growth rate is also a widely known one, and may already be reflected in the price.
  • Growth investing differs from value investing (Lesson 0.1) in what it's betting on: value bets the price is wrong today; growth bets the future earnings will be large enough to justify today's price.
Worked example 1 — PEG

30/20 = 1.5 PEG. A PEG near or below 1 is sometimes used as an (imprecise) rule-of-thumb reference point, though it's not a hard rule.

PEG=\frac{P/E}{\text{Expected growth rate }(\%)}

Build it up: growing into the multiple

PEG is a single-year snapshot. A more direct way to see the growth bet is to compound earnings forward several years (the same compounding math as Investment Fundamentals Lesson 0.1) and ask what the current price implies about the P/E an investor would effectively be paying against future earnings, if the growth forecast comes true.

Worked example 2 — the forward multiple

Current EPS $2, price $60 (P/E = 60/2 = 30). If earnings grow 20%/year for 3 years: EPS₃ = 2×(1.20)³ ≈ $3.456. Against that future earnings figure, today's $60 price implies a forward P/E of 60/3.456 ≈ 17.4 — noticeably less "expensive" three years out, if the growth materializes.

\text{EPS}_n=\text{EPS}_0\times(1+g)^n\qquad\text{Forward P/E}=\frac{\text{Price}}{\text{EPS}_n}
PEGP/E divided by the expected annual earnings growth rate (%)
EPS₀, EPSₙearnings per share today, and projected n years ahead
gthe assumed annual earnings growth rate
Try this
  1. Raise expected growth with P/E fixed: PEG falls — the same multiple looks more reasonable against faster growth.
  2. In the earnings-compounding plot, raise the growth rate slider: the curve bends upward much faster — small differences in assumed growth compound into large differences in forward earnings over several years.
Common traps
  • Growth forecasts are uncertain — PEG and forward P/E are only as good as the growth estimate feeding them.
  • A widely expected growth rate may already be "priced in" — the stock doesn't necessarily re-rate just because the expected growth shows up, if everyone expected it.
  • Compounding cuts both ways: a growth rate that disappoints doesn't just fall short, it falls short compounded over every year assumed.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Growth-oriented funds concentrated in fast-expanding industries, such as technology-sector growth funds.
  • Analyst "forward P/E" figures quoted in equity research, built exactly this way from projected future earnings.
  • High-growth small-cap sectors, where expectations (and the room to disappoint them) both tend to run largest.

Check yourself

1. What does PEG relate, and what's its main limitation?
It relates the P/E ratio to the expected earnings growth rate; it's only as reliable as the growth forecast used, which is uncertain.
2. P/E 40, growth 25%. PEG? EPS $1.50 growing at 25%/year — what is it after 2 years?
PEG = 40/25 = 1.6. EPS₂ = 1.50×(1.25)² = $2.34375 ≈ $2.34.
3. Why might a stock with "obviously" high expected growth not necessarily be a good buy?
If the growth is widely expected, it may already be reflected in (priced into) today's share price — the stock only outperforms if growth comes in even better than what's already expected.

Takeaways

  • Growth investing pays more per dollar of current earnings for expected future growth.
  • PEG is a rough one-year cross-check; compounding earnings forward shows the same bet playing out over several years.
  • Widely expected growth may already be priced in, and disappointment compounds just like growth does.

Momentum investing

The questionA stock has risen steadily for 6 months. Does that trend, by itself, say anything useful about the next 6 months? And does it matter whether you measure the trend through yesterday, or stop one month short of today?

Intuition

Momentum investing buys what has recently trended up, on evidence that trends have historically tended to persist for some period before reversing — directly related to the trend-slope idea from Investment Fundamentals Lesson 3.5, now used as a selection rule across many stocks rather than read off one chart.

  • Momentum is a relative ranking tool: a portfolio typically buys the stocks with the strongest trailing returns across a large group, not just any single stock that happens to be rising.
  • The lookback window matters — too short, and the "signal" is mostly noise; too long, and it stops reflecting anything recent.
  • Momentum and value (Lesson 0.1) often point in opposite directions: a stock with strong recent momentum is, almost by definition, not the stock trading furthest below its estimated value.
Worked example 1 — trailing return

A stock rising from $40 to $52 over 6 months has a trailing 6-month return of (52−40)/40 = 30% — a momentum strategy would rank this favorably versus a flat or declining peer.

\text{trailing return}=\frac{P_{\text{now}}-P_{\text{6mo ago}}}{P_{\text{6mo ago}}}

Build it up: the "12-minus-1" construction

Academic and practitioner momentum research commonly measures a 12-month return that skips the most recent month (often called "12-1" momentum), rather than a plain 12-month return through today. The most recent month is dropped because very short-term price moves have historically tended to show the opposite pattern — short-term reversal rather than momentum — and mixing the two can blur the signal the strategy is trying to capture.

Worked example 2 — 12-1 vs. plain 12-month

Price 12 months ago: $45. Price 1 month ago: $66. Price now: $70. 12-1 momentum (12mo-ago to 1mo-ago) = (66−45)/45 ≈ 46.7%. Plain 12-month return (12mo-ago to now) = (70−45)/45 ≈ 55.6% — the two measures tell a noticeably different story about the same stock, because the final month's extra move is excluded from one and not the other.

\text{momentum}_{12\text{-}1}=\frac{P_{\text{1mo ago}}-P_{\text{12mo ago}}}{P_{\text{12mo ago}}}
P_now, P_6mo agoprice today and 6 months ago
P_1mo ago, P_12mo agoprice 1 month ago and 12 months ago — the standard "12-1" window
Try this
  1. Make the two prices equal: trailing return goes to 0% — no momentum signal either way.
  2. In the 12-1 calculator, push the price 1 month ago up toward the price now: the gap between the two measures shrinks — most of the difference comes from what happened in that final month.
Common traps
  • Momentum can reverse sharply and without warning — past persistence is not a guarantee.
  • Mixing up a plain 12-month return with the 12-1 construction — they can disagree meaningfully, as the worked example shows.
  • Momentum strategies can suffer unusually sharp losses exactly when many momentum-driven positions unwind at once (a "momentum crash") — a known risk in the academic literature, not a hypothetical one.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Momentum factor funds and trend-following strategies offered by several large asset managers.
  • Managed-futures and trend-following funds that apply a similar idea across commodities, currencies and bonds, not just stocks.
  • Technical analysis (its own path in this site) uses closely related trend concepts at a shorter, chart-reading timescale.

Check yourself

1. What does momentum investing buy, and over roughly what kind of horizon does the "signal" typically get measured?
What has recently trended upward, typically measured over a lookback window of several months to about a year, expecting the trend to persist for a while longer.
2. Price $30 twelve months ago, $33 one month ago, $36 now. What's the 12-1 momentum, and the plain 12-month return?
12-1 = (33-30)/30 = 10%. Plain 12-month = (36-30)/30 = 20%.
3. Why do many momentum strategies skip the most recent month rather than using a plain trailing return?
Very short-term price moves have historically tended to show reversal rather than momentum, so including the most recent month can blur the longer trend the strategy is trying to capture.

Takeaways

  • Momentum investing bets on the persistence of recent trends, usually ranked across many stocks at once.
  • The standard "12-1" construction skips the most recent month to avoid blending in short-term reversal.
  • It carries real reversal risk, including the possibility of sharp "momentum crash" episodes.

Income investing

The questionYou bought a stock at $50. Its dividend has since grown to $3/year. What's your yield relative to what you actually paid — and if that dividend keeps growing, how different does the same stock look to someone buying fresh today?

Intuition

Income investing prioritises current cash income over price appreciation. Yield on cost measures the current dividend against your original purchase price, which can look very different from the yield a new buyer sees today (the current-price dividend yield from Investment Fundamentals Lesson 2.1).

  • Yield on cost only rises over time if the dividend itself grows — it isn't automatic, and some companies freeze or cut dividends instead of growing them.
  • A long holding period combined with steady dividend growth can push yield on cost well above what any new buyer currently receives.
  • Income investing differs from growth investing (Lesson 0.2) in what's being compounded — growth investors compound earnings and hope the price follows; income investors compound the cash paid out directly.
Worked example 1 — yield on cost today

3/50 = 6% yield on cost — potentially much higher than the stock's current-price dividend yield if the price has also risen since purchase.

\text{Yield on cost}=\frac{\text{Current dividend}}{\text{Original purchase price}}

Build it up: a growing dividend, projected forward

A dividend that grows at a steady rate compounds exactly like any other growing quantity (Investment Fundamentals Lesson 0.1) — the lab below reuses that same compounding engine, now tracking a dividend climbing year after year rather than an account balance.

Worked example 2 — projecting the dividend forward

Starting dividend $3, growing 7%/year for 10 years: 3×(1.07)¹⁰ ≈ $5.90. Against the original $50 purchase price, yield on cost would then be 5.90/50 ≈ 11.80% — nearly double the 6% starting point, purely from a decade of dividend growth.

Worked example 3 — the same dividend, two buyers

Suppose, 10 years on, the price has also risen to $70 and a new buyer purchases at that price. Their fresh yield is 5.90/70 ≈ 8.43% — respectable, but well below the original holder's 11.80% yield on cost on the same $5.90 dividend. The gap is entirely explained by the different purchase price, not by anything special about either buyer's shares.

\text{Dividend}_n=\text{Dividend}_0\times(1+g)^n
Dividend₀, Dividendₙthe dividend today, and projected n years ahead
gthe assumed annual dividend growth rate

Common traps
  • A high yield on cost reflects a long, successful holding period — it says nothing about buying the stock fresh today at its current price.
  • An unusually high current-price dividend yield can signal distress (the price has fallen because the market expects a dividend cut), not a bargain.
  • Dividend growth isn't guaranteed — a company can freeze or cut its dividend, which also freezes or reverses yield-on-cost growth.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Dividend-growth portfolios commonly cited as a retirement-income approach.
  • "Dividend aristocrat" style indices and funds that specifically screen for a long history of consecutive dividend increases.
  • Dividend reinvestment plans (DRIPs), which use each payout to buy more shares, compounding both the share count and the dividend together.

Check yourself

1. What does yield on cost use as its denominator, and why can two investors in the exact same stock have very different yields on cost?
The original purchase price, not the current price — investors who bought at different times paid different prices, so the same current dividend produces a different yield on cost for each.
2. Dividend $4, purchase price $40. Yield on cost? If that dividend grows 5%/year for 5 years, what is it then?
Yield on cost today = 4/40 = 10%. Dividend after 5 years = 4×(1.05)^5 ≈ $5.105.
3. Does a high yield on cost mean the stock is a good buy today, for a new investor?
No — it reflects the original holder's purchase price, not today's; a new buyer's yield depends entirely on today's price, which may be far higher.

Takeaways

  • Yield on cost measures income against your original price, not today's.
  • A growing dividend compounds yield on cost upward over a long holding period, using the same math as any other compounding quantity.
  • The same dividend can mean very different yields for the original holder versus a fresh buyer today.

Passive indexing

The questionIf markets are reasonably efficient (Financial Markets Lesson "market-efficiency") and active management costs more (Investment Fundamentals Lesson 2.7), what's the simplest alternative — and is the stated expense ratio the whole story, or just part of the cost gap?

Intuition

Passive indexing gives up trying to beat a benchmark and instead tracks it as cheaply as possible. It directly combines two earlier lessons: the market-efficiency argument that beating the market consistently is hard, and the fee-drag math showing how much costs matter over decades.

  • A passive fund's goal isn't to outperform its benchmark — it's to match it as closely as possible, net of a very small fee ("tracking error" measures how well it succeeds).
  • The headline expense ratio is the most visible cost, but it isn't the only one — how a fund trades also matters.
  • Passive indexing and the other four strategies in this module aren't mutually exclusive in practice — many real portfolios blend a low-cost indexed "core" with smaller active or factor-tilted positions (sometimes called "core-satellite").
Worked example 1 — fee drag over decades

Reusing Investment Fundamentals Lesson 2.7's numbers: $10,000 at 7% gross for 30 years grows to about $75,485 at a 0.03% fee versus about $57,435 at a 1% fee — the same math, now framed as the core argument for passive indexing.

Build it up: turnover and taxable trading add a second, quieter drag

A fund's stated expense ratio captures its management fee, but actively managed funds also tend to trade far more often ("turnover") than index funds, which mostly just hold their benchmark's constituents. In a taxable account, each profitable sale can trigger a taxable gain years before the investor would otherwise have realized one by simply holding — an extra, less visible drag on compounding, on top of the headline fee.

Worked example 2 — adding turnover-driven tax drag

Suppose, on top of Worked example 1's fees, light turnover costs the passive fund an extra illustrative 0.02%/year while heavy turnover costs the active fund an extra illustrative 0.50%/year. Net annual rates become 7% − 0.03% − 0.02% = 6.95% (passive) and 7% − 1% − 0.50% = 5.5% (active). Over the same 30 years: $10,000 grows to about $75,063 (passive) versus about $49,840 (active) — an even wider gap than the fee-only comparison, entirely from a cost that doesn't show up in either fund's advertised expense ratio.

\text{Ending value}=\text{Principal}\times(1+\text{gross}-\text{fee}-\text{turnover drag})^{\text{years}}
grossthe market's return before any costs
feethe fund's stated annual expense ratio
turnover dragan illustrative extra annual cost from taxable trading activity, beyond the stated fee

Common traps
  • "Passive" doesn't mean risk-free — an index fund still carries the full risk of the market it tracks.
  • Comparing only the headline expense ratio between an active and a passive fund can understate the true cost gap, since turnover-driven taxable trading isn't included in that number.
  • Assuming every "index fund" is automatically ultra-low-cost — expense ratios vary across index products, and tracking error can also differ between funds following the same benchmark.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Broad-market index funds and ETFs are the standard passive vehicle.
  • Fund fact sheets that disclose both an expense ratio and a separate "turnover ratio," reflecting the two different cost sources discussed here.
  • Core-satellite portfolio construction, blending a passive core with targeted active or factor positions (Portfolio Management path).

Check yourself

1. What two earlier ideas does passive indexing combine?
Market efficiency (hard to beat consistently) and the cost of fees compounding over time.
2. Besides the expense ratio, what other cost tends to be higher for actively managed funds, and why does it matter?
Trading turnover — more frequent buying and selling can trigger taxable gains more often in a taxable account, adding a cost beyond the stated fee, which also compounds against the investor over time.
3. Why does even a small, "invisible" extra cost (like 0.02% vs. 0.50% in turnover drag) matter so much over 30 years?
Because it's subtracted from the rate being compounded every single year — compounding applies to the difference as much as to the headline return, so small annual gaps become large dollar gaps over decades.

Takeaways

  • Passive indexing trades the attempt to beat the market for low, predictable costs.
  • Turnover-driven taxable trading is a second, less visible cost gap on top of the headline expense ratio.
  • It still carries full market risk, and often gets blended with smaller active positions rather than used in isolation.
