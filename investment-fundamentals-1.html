Order types in depth

The questionA stock is trading around $50. Should you always just click "buy now" — and if you set a protective order to sell if it falls, can you be sure it will actually get you out at a price you'd find acceptable?

Intuition

Every order type is really a trade-off between two things you can't always have both of: getting filled and getting your price.

  • A market order says "fill me immediately at the best available price" — it is extremely likely to execute, but the exact price isn't guaranteed, especially if the market is moving fast.
  • A limit order says "only fill me at my price or better" — the price is guaranteed if it fills, but it may simply never fill.
  • A stop order sits dormant until a trigger price is touched, then instantly becomes a market order — commonly used to cap a loss or lock in a gain, but once triggered it fully inherits a market order's "fills fast, price not guaranteed" behaviour.
Worked example 1 — market vs. limit

You place a market order on a stock quoted near $50; it fills at $50.10 after a brief price move. A limit order capped at $50.00 would have either filled at $50.00 or not filled at all. On 100 shares, the market order cost 100 × ($50.10 − $50.00) = $10 more.

Build it up: the stop-limit order — guaranteeing a price can mean guaranteeing no fill

A fourth type combines the other two: a stop-limit order sits dormant like a stop order, but once triggered it becomes a limit order rather than a market order. That fixes the "price not guaranteed" problem of a plain stop order — but reintroduces the limit order's own problem: in a fast decline that gaps straight through the limit price, the order can simply never fill, leaving the position open and still falling.

Worked example 2 — stop vs. stop-limit in a fast drop

You bought a stock at $50/share (1,000 shares) and set a protective order at a $45 trigger. Bad news hits and the price gaps straight down to $43 with no trades in between.

  • Plain stop order (becomes a market order at $45): fills around $43. Realized loss = 1,000 × ($50 − $43) = $7,000 — painful, but locked in and done.
  • Stop-limit order (triggers at $45, capped at a $44.50 limit): the market never trades back up to $44.50 after gapping to $43, so the order does not fill. The shortfall is max(0, $44.50 − $43) = $1.50/share below the limit. You're still holding 1,000 shares with an unrealized loss of $7,000 at that moment — and no guarantee the price won't keep falling before it ever recovers to $44.50.

The formal definition

\text{extra cost}=\text{shares}\times(\text{fill price}-\text{limit price})\qquad \text{shortfall}=\max(0,\ \text{limit price}-\text{market price})
fill pricethe price a market order actually executes at
limit pricethe price a limit (or stop-limit) order is capped at
shortfallhow far the market has moved past the limit; positive means the order does not fill
Try this
  1. Raise the fill price while keeping the limit price fixed: the extra cost of using a market order grows.
  2. In the second calculator, drag the gap price below the limit price: the shortfall becomes positive — exactly the condition where the stop-limit order would be left unfilled.
Common traps
  • A stop order's trigger price is not a guaranteed fill price — in a fast-moving market it can execute far from where it was set.
  • A limit order (plain or stop-limit) can simply never fill if the market never reaches it, leaving you with the exposure you were trying to exit.
  • Assuming a stop-limit order is strictly "safer" than a plain stop order — it protects the price, but at the cost of a real chance of no fill at all during the sharpest moves.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Stop-loss orders used to cap downside during a sharp sell-off in an individual stock.
  • Limit orders used around earnings announcements, when prices often gap sharply overnight.
  • Stop-limit orders used by investors who want a price floor on the exit and are willing to accept the risk of not getting out at all.

Check yourself

1. Which order type guarantees a fill, and which guarantees a price?
A market order is very likely to fill but not at a guaranteed price; a limit order guarantees a price (or better) but may not fill.
2. Once a plain stop order triggers, what does it become — and how is a stop-limit order different?
A plain stop order becomes a market order (fills fast, price not guaranteed). A stop-limit order instead becomes a limit order once triggered — the price is capped, but it may not fill at all if the market gaps past the limit.
3. A stock gaps from $50 straight to $43 with a stop-limit order triggered at a $44.50 limit. Does the order fill, and why?
No — the market never traded at or above $44.50 after the gap, so there was no price at which the limit condition could be satisfied; the shortfall is $1.50/share and the position stays open.

Takeaways

  • Market orders prioritise speed; limit orders prioritise price; neither guarantees both at once.
  • Stop orders become market orders once triggered; stop-limit orders become limit orders once triggered.
  • A stop-limit order trades "no price guarantee" for "no fill guarantee" — it can leave a losing position open during the fastest, worst moves.

Bid-ask spread & liquidity

The questionDoes it matter whether you trade a stock with a $0.02 spread or one with a $0.50 spread — and does the quoted spread tell you the whole story if you want to trade a large number of shares at once?

Intuition

The bid is the best price a buyer currently offers; the ask is the best price a seller currently offers. The gap between them — the spread — is paid simply by trading: buy at the ask, sell at the bid. Liquidity describes how easily a reasonably large order trades without moving the price; thin, illiquid markets tend to have wider spreads and more price impact ("slippage") per trade. Liquidity and traded volume are related but not identical: a stock can trade a decent volume per day while still having a thin order book at any single instant, which is what actually determines how much a large order moves the price.

Worked example 1 — the quoted spread

Bid $49.95, ask $50.05 → spread $0.10. Buying then selling 200 shares costs roughly 200 × $0.10 = $20, about 0.2% of a $10,000 trade — a real, if small, cost before any price movement at all.

Build it up: beyond the quoted spread — walking the order book

The quoted bid and ask are only the best prices, each good for a limited number of shares. An order book actually has several price levels stacked behind the best one; a large order "walks the book," filling the cheapest shares first and then progressively worse ones. The quoted spread alone understates the true cost of a large order — this additional cost is what's usually meant by slippage or price impact.

Worked example 2 — walking a simplified two-level book

You want to buy 2,000 shares. The ask side shows 500 shares available at $50.02 (the top of the book) and the next 1,500 shares available at $50.08 (simplified to two levels for clarity). Your order fills: 500 × $50.02 + 1,500 × $50.08 = $25,010 + $75,120 = $100,130, for an average price of $100,130 / 2,000 = $50.065 — about 0.090% worse than the $50.02 top-of-book price you might have expected from the quote alone.

The formal definition

\text{round-trip cost}\approx \text{shares}\times(\text{ask}-\text{bid}) \qquad \text{avg.\ fill price}=\frac{\sum(\text{tier size}\times\text{tier price})}{\text{total shares}}
bid / askbest current buy / sell price
round-tripbuying then later selling the same position
tierone price level of the order book, each with its own available size
Try this
  1. Widen the spread and watch the round-trip cost and its percentage of trade value both grow.
  2. In the order-book calculator, shrink the top tier's size well below your order size: more of the order spills into the worse second tier, and the price impact grows.
Common traps
  • A "cheap-looking" thinly-traded stock can be expensive to actually trade once the spread is counted.
  • Spread costs compound with trading frequency — frequent in-and-out trading pays the spread every time.
  • Reading the quoted spread as the full cost of any order size — a large order can walk through several price tiers and pay meaningfully more than the top-of-book quote suggests.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Large, heavily-traded stocks (deep order books) typically have spreads of a few cents; thinly-traded micro-cap stocks can have spreads of several percent of the price.
  • Institutional investors splitting large orders over time specifically to reduce price impact from walking the book all at once.
  • Daily "volume" figures reported for a stock are a different concept from the depth available at any single instant, which is what actually sets price impact.

Check yourself

1. What is the bid-ask spread, in one sentence?
The gap between the best available buy price (bid) and sell price (ask).
2. A stock has bid $19.90, ask $20.10. What's the round-trip cost on 50 shares?
50 × ($20.10 − $19.90) = 50 × $0.20 = $10.
3. You need 2,000 shares; only 500 are available at the top-of-book ask, with the rest filling at a worse price. Will your average fill price be better or worse than the quoted ask, and why?
Worse — once the top tier (500 shares) is used up, the remaining 1,500 shares fill at a higher ask price, pulling the average fill price above the quoted top-of-book price.

Takeaways

  • The spread is a real, built-in cost of trading, separate from any price movement.
  • Liquidity and spread width tend to move together, and liquidity is about order-book depth, not just daily volume.
  • Large orders can walk through multiple price tiers, so the quoted spread alone understates their true cost.

Exchanges & indices

The questionA company sells new shares to the public for the first time at $20 each, raising money for itself. Later, investors trade those shares at $35. Does the company get that extra $15 per share — and if that company joins a stock index, does a 10% move in its share price always move the index by the same amount as a 10% move in any other member?

Intuition

The primary market is where a company sells new securities directly and receives the proceeds (an IPO, for example). The secondary market — what people usually mean by "the stock market" or "an exchange" — is investors trading existing shares among themselves; the company isn't a party to those trades and doesn't receive the money. A company's market capitalization is its price times total shares, but the shares actually available to trade (excluding large insider or founder stakes) are the float, and major indices typically weight by float-adjusted market cap.

Worked example 1 — float-adjusted market cap

A company trades at $40 with 100 million total shares (market cap $40 × 100M = $4.0B), but insiders hold 40 million of those shares. Float = 60 million shares, so the float-adjusted cap used by many indices is $40 × 60M = $2.4B — 40% smaller than the headline figure.

Build it up: how an index actually adds up its members — price-weighted vs. cap-weighted

An index isn't a single traded thing — it's a formula applied to a basket of securities, and how each member counts can matter enormously. A price-weighted index (the classic example is the Dow Jones Industrial Average) simply sums member prices and divides by a divisor — so a $200 stock moves the index ten times as much per percentage point as a $20 stock, regardless of which company is actually bigger. A cap-weighted index (the classic example is the S&P 500) instead weights each member by its (float-adjusted) market capitalization, so a company's influence on the index tracks its actual economic size, not its arbitrary per-share price.

Worked example 2 — the same move, two very different index impacts

Two companies: A trades at $20/share with 1,000M shares outstanding (market cap $20B); B trades at $200/share with only 50M shares outstanding (market cap $10B) — so A is actually twice as large a company as B. Now B's price rises 10%, to $220, while A is unchanged.

  • Price-weighted (simple two-stock average, divisor 2): old index = (20+200)/2 = 110; new index = (20+220)/2 = 120 — a move of (120−110)/110 = +9.09%, driven almost entirely by B, the smaller company, purely because its per-share price is higher.
  • Cap-weighted: B is 10/(20+10) = 33.3% of the combined cap; the index return is 0.333 × 10% = +3.33% — proportional to B's actual 33.3% share of total company size, a far smaller and arguably more representative move.

The formal definition

\text{Market cap}=P\times S \qquad \text{Float-adjusted cap}=P\times(S-S_{\text{insider}}) \qquad \text{Index}_{\text{price-wt}}=\frac{\sum P_i}{\text{divisor}} \qquad r_{\text{cap-wt}}=\sum w_i\,r_i,\ \ w_i=\frac{\text{cap}_i}{\sum \text{cap}}
Pshare price
Stotal shares outstanding
S_insidershares held by insiders/founders, not freely tradable
w_imember i's weight in a cap-weighted index (its share of total market cap)
r_imember i's return over the period
Try this
  1. Raise insider-held shares toward the total share count: float-adjusted cap shrinks toward zero even though the headline market cap is unchanged.
  2. In the weighting calculator, raise company B's price further while keeping both share counts fixed: the price-weighted change keeps growing even though B's actual weight (and influence) in the cap-weighted index barely moves.
Common traps
  • A "market cap" headline figure usually uses total shares, not the float that indices actually weight by.
  • Secondary-market trading does not send money to the company, no matter how high the price goes.
  • Assuming every index weights members the same way — a price-weighted index can let a smaller, higher-priced company swing the index more than a much larger, lower-priced one.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • NYSE and Nasdaq host both IPOs (primary market) and ongoing trading (secondary market).
  • The Dow Jones Industrial Average is price-weighted (and uses a divisor adjusted for stock splits), while the S&P 500 and Nasdaq Composite are float-adjusted, cap-weighted.
  • Major cap-weighted indices commonly use float-adjusted weighting, which matters most for companies with large insider stakes.

Check yourself

1. In which market does a company actually receive money from selling shares?
The primary market (e.g. an IPO) — not the secondary market, where investors trade existing shares among themselves.
2. A company has price $25, 200M total shares, 150M insider-held. What is its float-adjusted cap?
25 × (200M − 150M) = 25 × 50M = $1.25B.
3. In a two-stock price-weighted index, a $200 stock and a $20 stock, the $200 stock rises 10% and the $20 stock is unchanged. Why does the index move by nearly the full weight of the higher-priced stock's return even if the $20 stock is the larger company?
A price-weighted index sums raw prices, so a higher per-share price carries more index weight regardless of the company's actual market capitalization — unlike a cap-weighted index, where weight tracks economic size.

Takeaways

  • Primary market = company raises money; secondary market = investors trade among themselves.
  • Market cap = price × shares; float-adjusted cap excludes insider-held shares, and indices commonly weight by the float-adjusted figure.
  • Price-weighted indices let a high-priced stock dominate regardless of company size; cap-weighted indices tie influence to actual market value.

Brokers & account types

The questionTwo accounts each hold $10,000 of the same stock. One paid for it entirely in cash; the other borrowed half the purchase price. Do they behave the same way — and if the borrowed account's stock starts falling, exactly how far does it have to fall before the broker steps in?

Intuition

A cash account only lets you buy what you've actually paid for. A margin account lets you borrow part of the purchase price from the broker, using your securities as collateral — this amplifies both gains and losses, and the broker charges interest on the borrowed amount regardless of how the position performs. Separately, a taxable brokerage account is flexible but offers no special tax treatment, while a tax-advantaged retirement account trades some flexibility (contribution limits, withdrawal rules) for better tax treatment of the gains inside it. A further, easy-to-overlook distinction is custody: shares bought through a broker are typically held in "street name" (registered in the broker's name on your behalf, not issued to you as a paper certificate) — you still beneficially own them, but the broker is the custodian handling the paperwork.

Worked example 1 — the cost of borrowing

Borrowing $5,000 on margin at an 8% annual rate for 3 months costs 5000 × 0.08 × (3/12) = $100 in interest — due whether the stock went up, down, or sideways.

Build it up: the margin call — how far can the price fall before the broker acts?

A margin account has an initial margin requirement (how much of your own money you must put in up front) and an ongoing maintenance margin requirement (the minimum fraction of current position value that must remain your own equity). Because the dollar amount you borrowed stays fixed while the position's value can fall, your equity's share of that shrinking value drops faster than the price itself does — which is why a margin call can arrive well before the stock has "lost half its value."

Worked example 2 — finding the margin call price

You buy 100 shares at $100 ($10,000 total), borrowing $5,000 (50% initial margin) and contributing $5,000 of your own equity. Your broker requires 25% maintenance margin. The margin call price solves (shares × P − loan) / (shares × P) = 0.25, i.e. P = loan / (shares × (1 − 0.25)) = 5,000 / (100 × 0.75) = $66.67. That's only a (100 − 66.67)/100 = 33.3% decline from your $100 entry — far less than the roughly 50% drop it might intuitively seem to take, precisely because the $5,000 loan stays fixed while the equity cushion shrinks as a share of the falling position value.

The formal definition

\text{Interest}=\text{Principal}\times r\times\frac{\text{months}}{12} \qquad P_{\text{call}}=\frac{\text{Loan}}{\text{Shares}\times(1-\text{MM})}
Principalthe amount borrowed on margin
rthe annual margin interest rate
MMthe maintenance margin requirement (minimum equity share of position value)
P_callthe share price at which a margin call is triggered
Try this
  1. Double the borrowed amount: the interest cost doubles, independent of how the stock performs.
  2. In the margin-call calculator, raise the maintenance margin requirement: the call price rises and the decline needed to trigger it shrinks — a stricter requirement gives you less room to fall.
Common traps
  • Margin amplifies losses exactly as much as gains — a given percentage drop in the stock produces a larger percentage drop in your own equity.
  • Margin interest accrues even on a losing position.
  • Assuming it takes a roughly 50% price drop to wipe out 50%-margin equity — a maintenance margin requirement typically triggers a call at a much smaller decline, as the worked example shows.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Margin calls, where a broker demands more collateral or force-sells a position without necessarily consulting the account holder first, tend to cluster during sharp, broad market downturns.
  • Tax-advantaged retirement accounts are a common vehicle for the compounding benefit discussed in Lesson 0.1.
  • "Street name" custody and broker-dealer safeguards exist specifically to protect your beneficial ownership of shares if the broker itself fails — a separate question from whether the investment itself performs well.

Check yourself

1. What does a margin account let you do that a cash account doesn't?
Borrow part of the purchase price from the broker, using your securities as collateral.
2. Interest cost on $8,000 borrowed at 6% for 6 months?
8000 × 0.06 × (6/12) = $240.
3. You buy 200 shares at $50 ($10,000), borrowing $5,000, with a 25% maintenance margin requirement. At what price does a margin call trigger, and what decline does that represent?
P_call = 5,000 / (200 × 0.75) = $33.33, a (50 − 33.33)/50 ≈ 33.3% decline from the $50 entry price.

Takeaways

  • Margin borrowing amplifies both gains and losses, and margin interest is owed regardless of performance.
  • A maintenance margin requirement means a margin call can arrive at a far smaller price decline than intuition suggests, because the loan stays fixed while equity shrinks.
  • Account type (cash/margin, taxable/tax-advantaged) and custody (who holds the shares) are separate choices from what you actually buy.
1.5

Clearing & settlement

The questionYou sell a stock today. Can you spend the cash tomorrow — and why would an entire industry spend years and real money shortening the gap between "today" and "tomorrow" by just one day?

Intuition

A trade has a trade date (when you and the other side agree on price and quantity) and a later settlement date (when ownership and cash actually change hands). In between, a clearinghouse steps into the middle of the trade and guarantees both sides will perform, even if one party were to default before settlement (the same central-counterparty idea behind exchange trading generally). Many equity markets today commonly settle one business day after the trade (often written "T+1").

Worked example 1 — a simple settlement timeline

You sell on a Monday with T+1 settlement: the trade settles Tuesday (assuming no holiday), and that's when the cash is formally yours to withdraw or reinvest without timing restriction.

Build it up: why the settlement window itself is a source of risk

Between the trade date and settlement, something could still go wrong — a counterparty could default, or the market could move sharply — before the exchange is actually completed. To protect against this, a clearinghouse requires its members to post margin (collateral) sized to the risk open during that window. The longer the window, the more could plausibly go wrong before settlement, so (in a deliberately simplified, illustrative model) required margin can be thought of as roughly proportional to how many days the trade sits unsettled.

Worked example 2 — shortening T+2 to T+1

Using an illustrative, simplified model where margin required ≈ 0.5% of trade value per day of open settlement risk: for a $1,000,000 trade, a 2-day (T+2) window needs about 0.005 × 2 × $1,000,000 = $10,000 of margin, while a 1-day (T+1) window needs about 0.005 × 1 × $1,000,000 = $5,000 — half as much collateral tied up for the same trade. (Real clearinghouses use more sophisticated, statistically-grounded risk models than this simple proportional illustration — but the core intuition, less open time means less that can go wrong before settlement, is the same reasoning behind the industry-wide move to shorter cycles.)

The formal definition

\text{Settlement date}=\text{Trade date}+N\text{ business days} \qquad \text{Illustrative margin}\approx k\times N\times\text{trade value}
Nthe settlement cycle length (e.g. 1 for "T+1")
kan illustrative risk factor (here, 0.5% per day of open settlement risk) — not a real clearinghouse formula
Try this
  1. Change N and see how many business days pass before funds are considered settled.
  2. In the illustrative margin calculator, drop N from 2 to 1: the illustrative required margin halves, showing the shape of the motivation behind shortening settlement cycles.
Common traps
  • A sale isn't instantly "final" the moment you click — the clearinghouse is still completing the exchange until settlement.
  • Some account types restrict rapidly reusing unsettled proceeds for new trades.
  • Assuming a shorter settlement cycle removes counterparty risk entirely — it shrinks the window during which something could go wrong, but the clearinghouse's guarantee is still what actually absorbs a default.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • US equities moved from a T+2 to a T+1 settlement cycle, shortening the gap between trade and settlement.
  • Clearinghouses (central counterparties) underpinning major equity and futures exchanges, collecting margin from their members to cover this open-window risk.
  • Different markets and countries moving toward shorter settlement cycles at different paces, so the exact cycle length isn't universal across every market.

Check yourself

1. What is the difference between a trade date and a settlement date?
The trade date is when the deal is agreed; the settlement date is when ownership and cash actually change hands.
2. What does a clearinghouse do?
Guarantees both sides of a trade will perform, reducing the risk that one party defaults before settlement.
3. Using the simplified model (margin ≈ 0.5% × N × trade value), why would halving the settlement cycle length from T+2 to T+1 roughly halve the illustrative margin requirement?
Because the model makes margin directly proportional to N — fewer days of open settlement risk means proportionally less collateral is deemed necessary to cover what could go wrong before the trade settles.

Takeaways

  • Trade date and settlement date are different moments, with a clearinghouse guaranteeing both sides in between.
  • A longer settlement window is a longer window for something to go wrong, which is one reason clearinghouses size required margin to it.
  • Shorter settlement cycles reduce — but do not eliminate — the time counterparty risk exists.
1.6

Market hours & circuit breakers

The questionA company reports bad earnings right after the regular trading session ends, with its stock at $50. What happens to the price overnight — and separately, if the whole market suddenly plunges in minutes during the regular session, is there anything that stops the fall besides sellers simply running out of shares to sell?

Intuition

Regular trading hours are when the bulk of volume and the tightest spreads occur; extended hours (pre-market and after-hours) exist but typically have much thinner liquidity and wider spreads. Because news doesn't wait for the market to open, prices can gap between one session's close and the next session's open. Circuit breakers are temporary, market-wide trading halts triggered by very large, fast index declines during the regular session, meant to give the market time to find an orderly price rather than free-fall.

Worked example 1 — an overnight gap

The stock closes regular hours at $50; after-hours news pushes it to $47 — a gap of (47 − 50)/50 = −6% before the next regular session even opens. A stop order set at $49 for the regular session would not have triggered during the after-hours move and would not have protected against this gap.

Build it up: the three-level circuit breaker structure

Rather than a single on/off switch, market-wide circuit breakers in US equities are commonly structured as three escalating levels, each triggered by a decline in a broad index (such as the S&P 500) from the prior session's close: a smaller decline triggers a shorter pause (Level 1), a larger decline triggers a longer pause (Level 2), and an extreme decline halts trading for the rest of the session entirely (Level 3). (Exact threshold percentages and halt durations are set by exchange rule and can change over time — the illustrative shape of "bigger decline, bigger response" is the lasting idea.)

Worked example 2 — which level triggers?

Using illustrative thresholds of 7% (Level 1), 13% (Level 2), and 20% (Level 3) decline from the prior close: if the index is down 9% intraday, it is 9 − 7 = 2 percentage points past the Level 1 threshold (triggered) but 9 − 13 = −4 points short of Level 2 (not triggered) and 9 − 20 = −11 points short of Level 3. Only Level 1 has fired at a 9% decline.

\text{gap \%}=\frac{P_{\text{new}}-P_{\text{old}}}{P_{\text{old}}} \qquad \text{margin past level}=\text{decline \%}-\text{threshold \%}
P_oldthe prior session's closing price
P_newthe price at or near the next session's open
margin past levelpositive means that circuit-breaker level has triggered; negative means it hasn't (yet)
Try this
  1. Set the new price below the old price and watch the gap go negative; above it, positive.
  2. In the circuit-breaker calculator, raise the intraday decline past 13%: Level 2's margin turns positive while Level 3's stays negative — only the first two levels have triggered.
Common traps
  • A stop order does not protect against a gap that jumps straight past its trigger price.
  • Extended-hours trading usually has far less liquidity than the regular session, so the same order can move the price much more.
  • Assuming circuit breakers protect against overnight or pre-market gaps — they're generally built around halting the regular intraday session, not preventing a gapped open.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Earnings reported after the close routinely cause a gapped opening the next session.
  • Market-wide circuit breakers have historically triggered during unusually fast, sharp index declines.
  • The three-level (Level 1/2/3) structure used by major US stock exchanges, with thresholds measured against the prior session's closing index level.

Check yourself

1. Why can a stock "gap" between sessions?
News or events can occur while the regular session is closed, and the next session's price reflects that information all at once rather than gradually.
2. A stock gaps from $80 to $86. What's the gap percentage?
(86 − 80)/80 = 7.5%.
3. Using illustrative thresholds of 7%/13%/20%, an index falls 12% intraday. Which circuit-breaker level(s) have triggered, and which hasn't?
12 − 7 = +5 (Level 1 triggered); 12 − 13 = −1 (Level 2 not yet triggered); 12 − 20 = −8 (Level 3 not triggered).

Takeaways

  • Prices can gap between sessions when news arrives while markets are closed.
  • Extended-hours trading has thinner liquidity than the regular session.
  • Circuit breakers are commonly structured as escalating levels, each triggered by a larger decline during the regular session — they don't address overnight gaps.
