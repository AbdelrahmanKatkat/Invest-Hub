4.1

Asset allocation models

The questionHow much of a portfolio "should" be in stocks versus bonds at age 30 versus age 60 — and is there one formula that gives the right answer for everyone at that age?

Intuition

Simple rules of thumb translate time horizon into an allocation: a longer horizon can better absorb the volatility from Lesson 0.5, since there's more time to recover from a downturn before the money is needed. The most widely cited version is stock % = 110 − age: as age rises, the suggested stock share falls in a smooth, predictable slide known as a glide path.

These rules compress a genuinely complicated decision into one number, which is exactly their strength and their weakness. What a single age-based formula leaves out:

  • Risk tolerance (Lesson 0.6) — two 40-year-olds with the same horizon can have very different comfort with volatility.
  • Other resources — a pension or other guaranteed income already behaves like a bond holding, which can justify a higher stock share in the rest of the portfolio than the generic rule suggests.
  • Multiple goals with different horizons — money earmarked for a house down payment next year has a much shorter horizon than retirement savings, even for the same person on the same day.

Build it up: comparing rules, and tilting for risk tolerance

110−age isn't the only variant in circulation — 100−age and 120−age are both common, and none is uniquely "correct"; they simply encode a more conservative or more aggressive starting glide path. The gap between them can be wide enough to matter.

Worked example 1 — the basic rule

At age 30: 110 − 30 = 80% stocks, 20% bonds/cash. At age 60: 110 − 60 = 50% stocks, 50% bonds/cash — a steadily more conservative mix over time.

Worked example 2 — comparing rules, and tilting the result

At age 50, the three common variants disagree by a wide margin: 100 − 50 = 50% stocks, 110 − 50 = 60% stocks, 120 − 50 = 70% stocks — a 20-percentage-point spread from the same age alone. A practitioner might then tilt a base rule for risk tolerance: starting from 110−age at age 40 (110 − 40 = 70%), a more conservative investor might subtract 10 points (60% stocks), while a more aggressive one adds 10 (80% stocks) — the age rule sets a starting point, not a final answer.

The formal definition

\text{stock \%}=\max\!\big(0,\min(100,\,k-\text{age})\big)
agethe investor's current age
ka constant chosen by the rule variant (commonly 100, 110 or 120)
max/minkeep the result sensibly between 0% and 100%
Try this
  1. Slide age from 20 to 80 in the calculator below: the suggested stock percentage steadily declines.
  2. In the glide-path chart, compare all three lines at age 50 — notice the spread is widest in the middle of a working life and narrows at the very old and very young ends.
Common traps
  • These are rough heuristics, not personalized financial advice — they ignore actual risk tolerance and circumstances.
  • Many variants exist (100−age, 110−age, 120−age); none is uniquely "correct", and the spread between them can be 20 percentage points or more at the same age.
  • A single age-based number can't see "other income" like a pension, or that the same person may have several goals with very different horizons at once.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Target-date retirement funds roughly follow an age-based glide path like this, automatically becoming more conservative as the target date approaches.
  • Robo-advisors typically start from a risk-tolerance questionnaire and tilt an age-based starting point up or down based on the answers.
  • Financial firms and media publish competing rule-of-thumb variants (100−age, 110−age, 120−age), which is itself a sign these are starting heuristics rather than a settled formula.

Check yourself

1. Using 110−age, what's the suggested stock % at age 45?
110 − 45 = 65%.
2. At age 50, what do the 100−age and 120−age rules each suggest, and what does the spread between them tell you?
100−50 = 50% stocks; 120−50 = 70% stocks — a 20-percentage-point spread at the identical age, showing these rules are rough starting points that disagree meaningfully with each other, not a single precise answer.
3. Why might someone with a secure pension or other guaranteed income be comfortable holding more stock than a generic age rule suggests?
A guaranteed income stream already behaves like a bond holding (a steady, low-risk cash flow), so the rest of the portfolio can afford to take on more equity risk than the generic rule assumes.

Takeaways

  • Age/horizon-based rules are simple starting points, not laws, and several competing variants exist.
  • Longer horizons can typically absorb more volatility, which is why the suggested stock share falls with age.
  • Personal risk tolerance, other income sources, and multiple goals with different horizons should all adjust any generic rule.
4.2

Rebalancing

The questionYou start at a 60/40 stocks/bonds split. Stocks then rise 20% while bonds stay flat — are you still 60/40? And if you wait three more years with uneven returns, does it matter whether you check on a fixed date or only when the drift gets large?

Intuition

Price moves cause a portfolio to drift away from its target weights automatically, as winners grow to a larger share. Rebalancing — selling some of what grew and buying what lagged — restores the target mix. It also mechanically enforces "sell (relatively) high, buy (relatively) low", the opposite of the instinct described in Lesson 0.9, without requiring anyone to predict which asset will do well next.

Two decisions have to be made alongside the math: how far is too far to drift, and how often to even check?

Build it up: calendar vs. threshold rebalancing

Two common policies answer those questions differently:

  • Calendar rebalancing — check and reset on a fixed schedule (e.g. once a year), regardless of how far the portfolio has drifted. Simple and predictable, but can mean trading on a tiny, barely-there drift, or letting a large drift run between check-in dates.
  • Threshold rebalancing — only trade once a holding's weight strays more than a chosen band (e.g. 5 percentage points) from its target. Trades less often, but requires watching the portfolio continuously rather than on a fixed date.
Worked example 1 — one year's drift

Start: $60,000 stocks, $40,000 bonds. Stocks rise 20% → $72,000; bonds stay at $40,000; total $112,000. New weights: stocks 72,000/112,000 ≈ 64.3%, bonds ≈ 35.7% — drifted from the 60/40 target. To rebalance back to 60%, sell 72,000 − (0.60 × 112,000) = $4,800 of stocks and move it into bonds.

Worked example 2 — three years, calendar vs. a 5-point threshold

Same $60,000/$40,000 start; stocks return +15%, +10%, +8% in years 1–3, bonds +3% each year. Under a 5-percentage-point threshold, drift is only 2.6pp after year 1 and 4.1pp after year 2 — below the band, so nothing trades — but reaches 5.2pp after year 3, triggering one trade of ≈$6,563. Under annual calendar rebalancing, every year gets reset back to 60/40 regardless of size: trades of ≈$2,880, ≈$1,851 and ≈$1,418 in years 1–3, totalling ≈$6,149 across three trades. The threshold policy traded once; the calendar policy traded three times for a similar total dollar amount.

The formal definition

\text{drifted weight}=\frac{\text{component value}}{\text{total value}}\qquad \text{trade}=\text{current value}-(\text{target weight}\times\text{total value})\qquad |\text{drifted weight}-\text{target weight}|>\text{band}\ \Rightarrow\ \text{rebalance}
component valuethe current dollar value of one holding (e.g. stocks)
target weightthe originally chosen allocation percentage
bandthe threshold policy's allowed drift before a trade is triggered (e.g. 0.05 for 5 percentage points)
Try this
  1. Confirm the drifted weights and trade amount in the calculator match worked example 1.
  2. Raise the stock return further: drift and the rebalancing trade both grow.
  3. In the drift chart, raise the gap between the stock and bond growth rates: the "never rebalanced" line pulls away from the 60% target faster.
Common traps
  • Rebalancing isn't free — trading costs and, in taxable accounts, potential taxes are real considerations.
  • Rebalancing on every tiny move can cost more than the drift it corrects; most plans use a schedule or a drift threshold instead.
  • Neither policy is universally "better" — the right choice (and the right band width or schedule) depends on trading costs, tax treatment, and how much drift the investor is willing to tolerate between checks.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Target-date funds rebalance automatically on an internal schedule.
  • "Calendar" (e.g. annual) and "threshold" (e.g. rebalance if drift exceeds 5%) are two commonly used, named rebalancing policies that many robo-advisors publish in their methodology documents.
  • Rebalancing between a broad stock index fund and a broad bond index fund is the simplest real-world version of this idea.

Check yourself

1. Why does a portfolio drift from its target weights over time?
Different holdings grow at different rates, so the faster-growing ones become a larger share of the total.
2. Start $50,000 stocks/$50,000 bonds (50/50); stocks rise 30%, bonds flat. New stock weight?
Stocks = 65,000; total = 115,000; weight = 65,000/115,000 ≈ 56.5%.
3. In the three-year worked example, which policy made more trades, and which one let drift run the longest before acting?
Calendar rebalancing made three trades (one every year); the 5-point threshold policy made only one trade, waiting until year 3 once cumulative drift finally exceeded the 5-percentage-point band.

Takeaways

  • Prices moving at different rates causes portfolio drift.
  • Rebalancing restores the target mix and enforces a disciplined "sell high, buy low" pattern without requiring market timing.
  • Calendar and threshold rebalancing trade off trading frequency against how much drift is allowed to accumulate; the right choice depends on costs and tolerance for drift, not a universal rule.
4.3

Dollar-cost averaging

The questionYou invest a fixed $100 every month for 3 months, at prices of $10, $8 and $12. Does investing the same dollar amount each time, regardless of price, help or hurt — and if you had that same total amount available today as a lump sum instead, would spreading it out actually leave you better off?

Intuition

Dollar-cost averaging (DCA) invests a fixed amount on a fixed schedule. Because a fixed dollar amount buys more shares when the price is low and fewer when it's high, the average cost per share can end up below the simple average of the prices paid. Its appeal is twofold:

  • Mechanical — more shares accumulate during dips, automatically, with no forecasting required.
  • Behavioural — it removes the "when should I invest?" decision that Lesson 0.9's biases tend to answer badly, by replacing a judgment call with a fixed rule.

But DCA has a quieter cost: money not yet invested isn't participating in the market at all, which matters most when prices are generally rising rather than falling.

Worked example 1 — a dip, then a recovery

$100 at $10 → 10 shares; $100 at $8 → 12.5 shares; $100 at $12 → 8.33 shares. Total: 30.83 shares for $300 invested, average cost = 300/30.83 ≈ $9.73/share — below the simple average price of the three, ($10 + $8 + $12)/3 = $10, because more shares were bought during the $8 dip.

Build it up: lump sum vs. DCA in a rising market

Worked example 1's price path dipped before recovering — exactly the shape that favors DCA. Run the comparison the other way, with a market that simply trends upward the whole time, and the result flips.

Worked example 2 — a steadily rising market

$12,000 total available. Lump sum: invest all $12,000 immediately at month 1's price of $100 → 120 shares. DCA: invest $1,000/month for 12 months while the price rises steadily from $100 to $122 in $2 steps → total shares ≈ 108.53 (each month's $1,000 buys fewer shares than the last, since price only climbs). At month 12's $122 price: lump sum is worth 120 × $122 = $14,640; DCA is worth 108.53 × $122 ≈ $13,240.57 — the lump sum ends up ≈$1,399 ahead, because it put all its capital to work at the lowest price in the window, while DCA kept buying in at progressively higher prices.

The formal definition

\text{Average cost}=\frac{\sum_i \text{investment}_i}{\sum_i \left(\text{investment}_i/\text{price}_i\right)}
investmentᵢthe fixed amount invested in period i
priceᵢthe price paid in period i
Try this
  1. Make all three prices equal in the first calculator: average cost equals that price exactly, matching the simple average.
  2. Make one price much lower than the others: average cost drops further below the simple average.
  3. In the second calculator, set the three prices to rise steadily (e.g. 10, 11, 12) instead of dipping: the lump sum's ending value pulls ahead of DCA's, mirroring worked example 2.
Common traps
  • DCA doesn't protect against a sustained decline — it changes the entry price pattern, not whether the underlying investment does well.
  • Holding cash on the sidelines specifically to "DCA in" has often underperformed investing it immediately, in periods when markets trended upward.
  • Lump sum investing isn't free of regret risk either — if a decline happens right after investing a lump sum, DCA would have avoided some of that drop; no single approach removes timing risk entirely, it only changes its shape.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Automatic monthly contributions to a retirement account are a common real-world form of DCA.
  • Payroll-deducted contributions to an employer-sponsored plan are DCA by default, whether or not the employee thinks of it that way.
  • Deciding how to invest a windfall (a bonus or inheritance) — all at once or spread over months — is one of the most common real versions of the lump-sum-vs-DCA question.

Check yourself

1. Does DCA buy more or fewer shares when the price is low?
More — the same fixed dollar amount buys more shares at a lower price.
2. $100/month for 2 months at $5 and $20. Average cost per share?
Shares = 100/5 + 100/20 = 20 + 5 = 25; average cost = 200/25 = $8.
3. In the 12-month rising-market example, which strategy ended with more value, and why?
The lump sum (≈$14,640 vs ≈$13,241) — it deployed all its capital at month 1's lower price, while DCA kept buying at progressively higher prices as the market rose.

Takeaways

  • DCA buys more shares when price is low, fewer when it's high, automatically.
  • Its main behavioural benefit is removing a hard, bias-prone timing decision — not a guarantee of a better outcome.
  • Whether DCA or a lump sum ends up ahead depends on the price path: a dip-then-recover favors DCA, while a steadily rising market favors investing the lump sum immediately.
4.4

Taxes & fees basics

The questionBeyond the expense ratio from Lesson 2.7, what else quietly drags on long-term returns — and how large can that second drag become once it compounds for decades?

Intuition

Taxes are a second, separate drag alongside fees:

  • A fund's expense ratio is charged every year regardless of performance, and is disclosed precisely.
  • Taxes on dividends and realized capital gains in a taxable account depend on the investor's own trading behaviour, account type and jurisdiction — there's no single published number the way there is for a fee.
  • A tax-advantaged retirement account defers or reduces that tax drag, letting more of the return compound (Lesson 0.1) with less friction.

Fees and tax drag stack on top of each other — both matter, and both compound, which is exactly what makes them easy to underestimate year to year.

Worked example 1 — fee drag alone

$50,000 at a 7% gross return for 25 years: at a 0.03% fee (net 6.97%) it grows to ≈ $269,476; at a 1% fee (net 6%) it grows to ≈ $214,594 — a $54,882 gap from fees alone, before even considering tax treatment.

Build it up: stacking an illustrative tax drag on top, over 30 years

Suppose, as a purely illustrative estimate (actual tax drag varies enormously by jurisdiction and account type), that a taxable account loses an additional ≈1.2% a year to taxes on dividends and realized gains, on top of a 0.03% fee — while an otherwise identical tax-advantaged account pays only the 0.03% fee with no annual tax drag.

Worked example 2 — fee and tax drag together, 30 years

$50,000 at 7% gross for 30 years: in the taxable scenario (fee 0.03% + illustrative tax drag 1.2%, net ≈5.77%) it grows to ≈ $269,058; in the tax-advantaged scenario (fee 0.03% only, net ≈6.97%) it grows to ≈ $377,424 — a gap of ≈ $108,367, larger than the fee-only gap in worked example 1, from an annual drag of roughly similar size to that fee difference.

The formal definition

FV=P\,(1+r_{\text{gross}}-\text{fee}-\text{taxDrag})^{t}
r_grossreturn before fees and taxes
feethe annual expense ratio (a stand-in for any recurring drag)
taxDragan illustrative estimate of annual return lost to taxes in a taxable account (0 for a tax-advantaged account in this simplified model)
Try this
  1. Confirm the two ending values and the gap in the first calculator match worked example 1.
  2. Push years further out: the dollar gap keeps growing faster than years do.
  3. In the second calculator, set taxDrag to 0: the two scenarios converge to the same value, isolating exactly how much the illustrative tax drag alone is costing.
Common traps
  • Fees and taxes each "feel small" year to year, which is exactly why their compounded effect is easy to underestimate.
  • Tax treatment varies by account type and is not identical everywhere — this lesson stays general rather than jurisdiction-specific.
  • A fund's expense ratio is a single disclosed number; tax drag is not — it depends on the investor's own trading behaviour, account type and tax bracket, so any tax-drag figure (including the illustrative one used here) is an estimate, not a universal constant.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Tax-advantaged retirement accounts versus ordinary taxable brokerage accounts is a foundational account-type decision.
  • Every fund discloses its expense ratio in its prospectus or fact sheet.
  • Many jurisdictions tax long-term holdings differently from short-term ones, another reason minimizing unnecessary trading (Lesson 4.2) can help an account's after-tax return.

Check yourself

1. Name the two drags on long-term returns discussed here.
Fees (expense ratios) and taxes.
2. Why can a tax-advantaged account meaningfully help long-term compounding?
By deferring or reducing tax drag along the way, more of the return stays invested and compounds.
3. In the 30-year example, roughly how much bigger is the tax-advantaged ending value than the taxable one, and what does that gap illustrate?
≈$377,424 vs ≈$269,058 — a gap of roughly $108,000, illustrating that an annual tax drag of similar size to a fee can compound into an even larger difference over decades.

Takeaways

  • Taxes and fees are two separate, compounding drags on returns.
  • Tax-advantaged accounts can reduce tax drag specifically; the fee still applies either way.
  • Small annual percentages become large dollar amounts over decades, and fee drag and tax drag matter together, not just whichever one is easier to look up.
4.5

Capstone: build and defend a portfolio

The questionAge 30, moderate risk tolerance, a 35-year horizon, $10,000 to start and $300/month ongoing. Using everything in this path, what's a simple, defensible first portfolio and plan — and how do you defend that plan against your own future impulses?

Intuition

This lesson doesn't introduce new math — it combines what came before into one decision, the same way Lesson 0.10 combined Module 0:

  • Start from an age/horizon-based allocation (4.1), tilted for actual risk tolerance (0.6).
  • Diversify across asset classes so it isn't one concentrated bet (0.7).
  • Keep costs low (2.7, 4.4) and be deliberate about account type (4.4).
  • Automate contributions (4.3) and rebalance on a schedule or threshold (4.2).
  • Write the plan down so it survives the biases from 0.9.
Worked example 1 — a sample allocation

Age 30 suggests roughly 80% stocks by the 110−age rule (4.1). A sample plan: 80% stocks, 15% bonds, 5% cash; $300/month auto-invested (4.3); reviewed and rebalanced on a schedule or a drift threshold (4.2); low-cost index funds only, held in a tax-advantaged account where possible (2.7, 4.4). Set the pie lab below to 80/15/5/0 to see this mix's illustrative expected return and risk.

Build it up: stress-testing the plan against a panic

A written allocation is only half the plan — the other half is what happens the first time markets fall sharply. The behavioural cost of abandoning a plan can be larger than any fee or tax drag in this module.

Worked example 2 — the cost of mistimed trades, illustratively

Suppose $10,000 is invested for 20 years. Staying fully invested the whole time at an illustrative 7%/year grows it to ≈ $38,697. Now suppose panic-selling during downturns and re-entering late repeatedly reduces the effective annual return actually captured to an illustrative 4%/year (a hypothetical used here purely to illustrate the mechanism, not a historical statistic) — that path only reaches ≈ $21,911, a gap of ≈ $16,786, or roughly 1.8× less. The allocation didn't change; only the discipline of staying in it did.

The formal definition

E[R_p]=\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i\,E[R_i]\qquad\qquad FV=P(1+r)^{t}
wᵢthe weight placed in asset class i
E[Rᵢ]that class's expected return
rthe annual return actually captured over the holding period — which behaviour, not just the market, helps determine
Try this
  1. Set the sliders to roughly 80% Stocks, 15% Bonds, 5% Cash, 0% Alternatives (matching the age-based suggestion) and read off the expected return and illustrative risk.
  2. Compare that to a far more conservative 30/60/10/0 mix — notice both numbers fall together.
  3. In the growth-curve chart, compare the 7% and 4% lines at year 20 versus year 5 — the gap between staying invested and mistiming is small early on and grows dramatically only with time.
Common traps
  • Treating this one allocation as permanent, instead of a living plan to revisit as circumstances change.
  • Skipping the "write it down" step and relying on memory or mood during the next downturn (Lesson 0.9).
  • Treating the sample allocation here as a personal recommendation — it's illustrative only; a real plan should reflect your own goals, horizon, risk tolerance and circumstances, ideally with a qualified professional.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • A short written investment policy statement — the plan, the allocation, and the rules for changing it — is commonly recommended by financial professionals.
  • Target-date funds are an off-the-shelf product that already combines an age-based glide path (4.1), diversification (0.7) and scheduled rebalancing (4.2) behind one ticker.
  • Robo-advisors automate several of this module's ideas at once — rebalancing, low costs, and sometimes tax-aware trading — behind a single interface.

Check yourself

1. Name three ideas from this path that a finished portfolio plan should combine.
Any three of: an age/horizon-based starting allocation, diversification, low costs, automated contributions, a rebalancing schedule or threshold, and a written plan to defend against biases.
2. Why write the plan down rather than just "knowing" it?
A written plan is harder to abandon impulsively during a stressful market move than an unwritten intention.
3. In the illustrative 20-year, $10,000 example, how much is lost by capturing an effective 4%/year instead of staying invested at 7%/year, and which earlier lesson does that connect to?
≈$38,697 at 7%/year versus ≈$21,911 at an effective 4%/year — a gap of ≈$16,786 — connecting to Lesson 0.9's behavioral biases (panic selling and mistimed re-entry) and Lesson 0.1's compounding.

Takeaways

  • A finished portfolio plan combines allocation, diversification, cost, automation and discipline.
  • Writing the plan down is itself a defence against behavioural mistakes.
  • A plan should be revisited on a schedule, not abandoned in a moment of stress — the discipline to stay invested can matter as much as the allocation itself.
