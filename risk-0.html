Drawdown & VaR

The questionA portfolio peaked at $120,000, then fell to $84,000 before recovering. How bad was that fall, in a single number — and separately, before anything has happened yet, how do you even put a number on "how bad a loss might get" for the next year?

Intuition

Maximum drawdown is a backward-looking, purely historical measure: it just finds the worst peak-to-trough decline that actually occurred in a track record, no modeling required. Value-at-Risk (VaR) is a different, forward-looking idea: an estimated loss threshold that a portfolio shouldn't be expected to exceed more than some stated percentage of the time, based on a statistical model's assumptions about how returns behave.

  • Drawdown answers: "looking back, what's the worst actual decline this portfolio has already lived through?"
  • VaR answers: "looking forward, what loss level should a given confidence level — say 95% of the time — not be exceeded?"
  • Both are single numbers that compress a messy return history or forecast into something comparable across funds — which is exactly why they're reported side by side on fund fact sheets, and exactly why each can be misused if treated as the whole risk picture.
Worked example 1 — drawdown

(84,000 − 120,000)/120,000 = −30% maximum drawdown — the portfolio lost just under a third of its peak value before recovering.

\text{Drawdown}=\frac{\text{Trough}-\text{Peak}}{\text{Peak}}

Build it up: turning "how risky" into a dollar threshold

The simplest way to build a VaR estimate is the parametric (variance–covariance) method: assume returns are roughly normally distributed, then scale the portfolio's volatility (standard deviation, \sigma) by a z-score that corresponds to the confidence level you want. A 95% one-tailed confidence level uses z ≈ 1.645; a stricter 99% level uses z ≈ 2.326 — the higher the confidence, the larger the multiplier, and the larger the estimated loss threshold.

Worked example 2 — parametric VaR

A $500,000 portfolio with an estimated annual return volatility of 20%: 1-year 95% VaR = 1.645 × 0.20 × $500,000 = $164,500. In words: under this model's assumptions, a one-year loss worse than $164,500 is estimated to occur no more than 5% of the time. At the stricter 99% confidence level, the same portfolio's VaR rises to 2.326 × 0.20 × $500,000 = $232,600 — demanding more confidence pushes the threshold further out into the tail.

Worked example 3 — scaling to a shorter horizon

Volatility scales with the square root of time, so a 1-month volatility is the annual figure divided by √12: 0.20/√12 ≈ 5.77%. The 1-month 95% VaR is then 1.645 × 0.0577 × $500,000 ≈ $47,487 — far smaller than the 1-year figure, simply because there's less time for a loss to accumulate.

\text{VaR}=z\times\sigma\times\text{Portfolio value}\qquad \sigma_{\text{period}}=\sigma_{\text{annual}}\times\sqrt{\frac{\text{period}}{1\ \text{year}}}
zthe z-score for the chosen one-tailed confidence level (1.645 for 95%, 2.326 for 99%)
\sigmathe portfolio's return volatility (standard deviation) over the chosen period
Portfolio valuethe amount currently invested, in dollars

  2. In the VaR calculator, raise z from 1.645 toward 2.326: the estimated loss threshold grows, even though nothing about the portfolio itself changed — only the confidence level demanded did.
Common traps
  • VaR says nothing about how bad a loss could be beyond its threshold — only that the threshold is estimated not to be crossed, at a stated frequency. The 5% (or 1%) of outcomes it ignores can still be severe.
  • Parametric VaR assumes roughly normal returns; real market returns often have "fatter tails" (more extreme moves than a normal distribution predicts), so model-based VaR can understate true tail risk, especially during crises.
  • A low historical maximum drawdown is not a guarantee against a larger future one — it only describes what has happened so far in a particular stretch of history.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Fund fact sheets and prospectuses reporting "maximum drawdown" as a standard historical risk measure.
  • Banks and brokers using VaR models to size risk limits and required capital for trading desks.
  • Risk reports for mutual funds and ETFs that quote both a historical drawdown figure and a forward-looking VaR or similar risk estimate side by side.

Check yourself

1. A fund peaks at $80,000 and later bottoms at $68,000 before recovering. What's its maximum drawdown?
(68,000 − 80,000)/80,000 = −15%.
2. A $250,000 portfolio has 25% annual volatility. What's its 1-year 95% and 99% parametric VaR?
95%: 1.645 × 0.25 × 250,000 ≈ $102,813. 99%: 2.326 × 0.25 × 250,000 ≈ $145,375 — the stricter confidence level demands a larger threshold.
3. Does a 95% VaR of $100,000 mean the worst possible loss is $100,000?
No — it means losses worse than $100,000 are estimated to occur no more than 5% of the time; it says nothing about how large a loss in that remaining 5% could actually be.

Takeaways

  • Drawdown measures an actual, historical worst peak-to-trough decline; it requires no model.
  • VaR is a model-based, forward-looking threshold — scale volatility by a confidence z-score and portfolio value to get a dollar figure.
  • Neither measure describes the severity of losses beyond what it reports — both are risk summaries, not worst-case guarantees.

Options basics

The questionYou buy a call option, strike $50, paying a $3 premium. At expiry the stock is $65. What's your profit — and at what stock price would you have made exactly nothing?

Intuition

A call option gives the right (not the obligation) to buy a stock at a fixed strike price, on or before a set expiry. If the stock finishes above the strike, you exercise and pocket the difference; if it finishes below, you simply let the option expire worthless and walk away. Its payoff at expiry is the stock price minus the strike, if positive, and zero otherwise — which means the premium paid upfront is the most a buyer can ever lose, no matter how far the stock falls.

Worked example 1 — the profit calculation

Payoff = max(0, 65 − 50) = 15. Profit = 15 − 3 (premium) = $12 per share — the option paid out, and comfortably covered its own cost.

\text{Payoff}=\max(0,\,S-K)\qquad \text{Profit}=\text{Payoff}-\text{Premium}
Sstock price at expiry
Kstrike price
Premiumthe price paid upfront to buy the option

Build it up: breakeven, moneyness, and comparing two strikes

The question's second half asks for the breakeven price: the stock price at which profit is exactly zero. Since profit = (S − K) − Premium once the option is in the money, breakeven is simply strike + premium — below it you're still at a loss (though never worse than −premium); above it you're in genuine profit.

Options are also described by moneyness: in the money (ITM) when the stock is above the strike (the option has intrinsic value), at the money (ATM) when the stock equals the strike, and out of the money (OTM) when the stock is below the strike (worth only whatever time value remains before expiry). A further-OTM strike is cheaper to buy (less likely to pay off) but needs a bigger move to turn a profit — the classic trade-off between an option's cost and its probability of paying out.

Worked example 2 — comparing two strikes on the same stock

Call A: strike $50, premium $3 → breakeven $53. Call B: strike $60, premium $1 (cheaper, since it needs a bigger move) → breakeven $61. At expiry with the stock at $65: Call A profit = max(0,65−50)−3 = $12; Call B profit = max(0,65−60)−1 = $4. Call A cost more upfront but paid out more once the stock rallied this far past both strikes.

\text{Breakeven}=K+\text{Premium}

  2. In the two-strike plot below, find where each line crosses zero (its breakeven) — the farther-OTM strike needs a bigger stock move before it turns profitable.
Common traps
  • A call buyer's maximum loss is the premium — but a call seller's loss is, in principle, unlimited on the upside, since the stock has no ceiling.
  • Confusing payoff (what the option is worth at expiry) with profit (payoff minus the premium already paid) — a profitable-looking payoff can still be a net loss if it doesn't clear the premium.
  • Assuming a cheaper, further-OTM option is simply "better value" — it's cheaper precisely because it's less likely to pay off, not because it's a bargain.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • A call option used to bet on or participate in a stock's upside with limited, known downside.
  • Listed equity and index call options quoted with a range of strikes and expiries on options exchanges.
  • Employee stock options, which are economically a (typically long-dated) call on the company's own stock.

Check yourself

1. Strike $40, stock at expiry $55, premium $4. Profit, and breakeven price?
Profit: max(0,55-40)-4 = 15-4 = $11. Breakeven: 40+4 = $44.
2. What's the most a call buyer can lose, and is a call seller's potential loss also capped?
The buyer's maximum loss is the premium paid. The seller's loss is not capped — in principle it's unlimited as the stock price rises.
3. Two calls on the same stock: strike $30/premium $5, and strike $35/premium $2. Which has the higher breakeven, and why is the second one cheaper to buy?
Breakevens are $35 and $37 — the second (higher-strike) call has the higher breakeven. It's cheaper because a higher strike is further out of the money, so it needs a bigger stock move to pay off at all.

Takeaways

  • A call's payoff is max(0, stock price − strike); profit subtracts the premium already paid.
  • Breakeven = strike + premium; moneyness (ITM/ATM/OTM) describes where the stock sits relative to the strike.
  • Buyer risk is capped at the premium; seller risk is not — and a cheaper, further-OTM option needs a larger move to become profitable.

Futures basics

The questionA futures contract controls $100,000 of notional value, but you only need to post $5,000 of margin. What does that imply about risk — and exactly how small a move against you would wipe out enough of that margin to trigger a demand for more cash?

Intuition

A futures contract is an agreement to exchange an asset at a set price later (the same basic idea as Investment Fundamentals Lesson 2.5's futures/spot relationship), but trading it requires only a small margin deposit relative to the full contract value — creating substantial leverage. Leverage is a multiplier: it doesn't change the dollar size of a price move in the underlying asset, but it changes how large that move looks relative to the much smaller amount of your own cash actually posted.

Worked example 1 — the leverage ratio

100,000/5,000 = 20× leverage — a 5% move in the underlying asset moves your margin by roughly 100%.

\text{Leverage}=\frac{\text{Notional value}}{\text{Margin posted}}

Build it up: margin calls and mark-to-market

Futures accounts are marked to market daily (sometimes more often): gains and losses are credited or debited to your margin balance every day the position is open, not just when you close it. Exchanges also distinguish between initial margin (what you post to open the position) and a lower maintenance margin (the minimum balance you must keep). If daily losses push your balance below maintenance, the broker issues a margin call, demanding you top the account back up — or the position gets closed out, locking in the loss.

Worked example 2 — how small a move triggers a margin call

$5,000 initial margin, maintenance margin set at 75% of that = $3,750. The account can absorb a loss of 5,000 − 3,750 = $1,250 before a call is triggered. As a fraction of the $100,000 notional, that's just 1,250/100,000 = 1.25% — a surprisingly small adverse price move, directly because of the 20× leverage from Worked example 1.

\text{Loss to trigger call}=\text{Initial margin}-\text{Maintenance margin}\qquad \%\text{ move to trigger}=\frac{\text{Loss to trigger call}}{\text{Notional}}
Initial margincash required to open the futures position
Maintenance marginthe minimum balance that must be kept; falling below it triggers a margin call

  1. Raise required margin in the leverage calculator: leverage falls — less risk amplification per dollar posted.
  2. In the plot below, raise leverage further: the margin-value line gets steeper, so it crosses the maintenance-margin line (a margin call) after a smaller price move.
Common traps
  • Leverage amplifies losses exactly as much as gains, the same warning as margin accounts (Investment Fundamentals Lesson 1.4).
  • Forgetting that daily mark-to-market means losses are realized against your cash balance in real time, not only when you eventually close the position.
  • Posting only the bare initial margin with no buffer — a small adverse move can trigger a margin call far sooner than intuition about the "notional" size would suggest.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Commodity futures (oil, gold, agricultural products) used by institutions and speculators alike.
  • Equity index futures used by institutional investors to quickly adjust market exposure without trading every underlying stock.
  • Margin call notices from brokers and clearinghouses when a leveraged futures position moves against the holder.

Check yourself

1. Notional $200,000, margin $10,000. Leverage?
200,000/10,000 = 20×.
2. Does leverage amplify gains only, or losses too?
Both, equally — leverage has no opinion on direction.
3. Initial margin $8,000, maintenance margin $6,000, notional $160,000. What size adverse price move triggers a margin call?
Loss to trigger = 8,000 − 6,000 = $2,000. As a fraction of notional: 2,000/160,000 = 1.25%.

Takeaways

  • Futures require only a fraction of notional value as margin, creating leverage.
  • Leverage amplifies gains and losses equally, and daily mark-to-market applies that amplification in real time.
  • The gap between initial and maintenance margin, as a fraction of notional, is exactly the price move that triggers a margin call.

Hedging with derivatives

The questionYou own a stock at $100 and are worried about a near-term drop. A put option, strike $95, costs $4. How does that protect you — and what exactly does it cost you if the drop never happens and the stock instead rises?

Intuition

A put option gives the right to sell at the strike price, so it gains value as the stock falls — like insurance against a decline. Buying a put alongside owning the stock is hedging: using a derivative to offset risk rather than to speculate. This combination is called a protective put, and it's one of the simplest hedges in derivatives: own the asset, buy downside protection on it separately.

Worked example 1 — the effective floor

Below $95, losses on the stock are offset by gains on the put; the net effective floor is strike minus premium = 95 − 4 = $91, versus an unprotected position that could keep falling further.

\text{Effective floor}=\text{Strike}-\text{Premium paid}

Build it up: the full profit-and-loss picture, and an alternative hedge

A hedge's cost only becomes obvious once you track profit-and-loss (P&L) across every possible outcome, not just the drop you're worried about. Starting from a $100 cost basis, hedged P&L = (stock price − 100) + max(0, 95 − stock price) − 4, while unhedged P&L is simply (stock price − 100).

Worked example 2 — comparing outcomes

If the stock drops to $70: unhedged P&L = −$30, hedged P&L = (70−100) + max(0,25) − 4 = −$9 — the put absorbed most of the damage, flattening the loss at the $91 floor. If the stock instead rises to $120 (the drop never happens): unhedged P&L = +$20, hedged P&L = (120−100) + 0 − 4 = +$16 — the hedge gave up exactly $4 of upside, the premium, for protection it ended up not needing.

A short futures position (Lesson 0.3) is a different way to hedge the same underlying risk, without buying an option at all: short an amount of notional equal to the position being protected, so a loss on the stock is offset by a roughly equal gain on the short futures, and vice versa. Unlike a put, a futures hedge has no upfront premium — but it also gives up upside entirely, rather than only the cost of insurance, and it still carries the margin and leverage risks from Lesson 0.3.

Worked example 3 — a short futures hedge

A $100,000 stock portfolio hedged with a $100,000 notional short futures position (a 1:1 "hedge ratio"). The stock falls 5%: stock loss = 100,000 × (−0.05) = −$5,000; short futures gain ≈ +$5,000 (the short position profits as the price falls). Net P&L ≈ $0 — losses and gains cancel out almost exactly, ignoring small real-world frictions like the futures' basis and margin costs.

\text{Hedged P/L}=(S-S_0)+\max(0,K-S)-\text{Premium}\qquad \text{Unhedged P/L}=S-S_0
S₀original cost basis (purchase price) of the held stock
Sstock price at expiry
Kthe protective put's strike price

Common traps
  • A hedge has a cost (the premium) even if the drop never happens — like any insurance, you pay whether or not you file a claim.
  • Assuming a hedge is "free money protection" — it caps losses but also caps or reduces upside, as the $4-per-share gap in Worked example 2 shows.
  • Treating a futures hedge as perfectly offsetting — in practice, basis risk (the futures and the underlying not moving in perfect lockstep) and margin mechanics mean the offset is rarely exactly dollar-for-dollar.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Portfolio managers buying index puts ahead of an uncertain event (an earnings release, an election, a central bank decision) as portfolio insurance.
  • Airlines and other commodity-exposed businesses hedging fuel costs with futures, and farmers hedging a harvest's sale price the same way.
  • Exporters and importers hedging foreign-currency receivables or payables with FX forwards or futures, directly paralleling the currency risk from Financial Markets Lesson 0.5.

Check yourself

1. What does a protective put do, and what's its effective floor if strike is $80, premium $3?
It offsets losses on a held stock below the strike, acting like insurance. Effective floor = 80 − 3 = $77.
2. Stock bought at $100, protective put strike $95 premium $4. Stock rises to $130 instead of falling. What's the hedged P&L, and how much upside did the hedge give up versus unhedged?
Hedged P&L = (130−100) + 0 − 4 = $26. Unhedged P&L = $30. The hedge gave up exactly $4 — the premium — of upside.
3. Why might a short futures hedge be preferred over buying a put, and what's the trade-off?
It requires no upfront premium payment. The trade-off is it also gives up all upside (not just the cost of insurance), and still carries margin/leverage and basis risk.

Takeaways

  • A protective put sets an effective floor below which losses are offset, at the cost of the premium.
  • Hedging has a real, ongoing cost — comparing hedged vs. unhedged P&L across the full price range shows exactly where the hedge helps and where it's a pure drag.
  • A short futures position is an alternative, premium-free hedge that instead gives up upside entirely and carries its own margin and basis risk.

The Greeks, briefly

The questionAn option has a delta of 0.5. The stock moves up $2. Roughly how much does the option's price move — and does that same 0.5 still apply if the stock then moves another $4 from there?

Intuition

Delta approximates how much an option's price moves per $1 move in the underlying stock — a quick, approximate sensitivity measure. It's one of several "Greeks" that quantify an option's sensitivities: others measure sensitivity to time passing (theta), volatility (vega) and interest rates (rho), beyond this module's scope. Delta is the most quoted of them because it answers the single most common question a trader asks: "if the stock moves, how much does my option move with it?"

Worked example 1 — the linear approximation

0.5 × $2 = $1 estimated option price move.

\Delta\text{Option price}\approx \text{Delta}\times\Delta\text{Stock price}

Build it up: delta itself changes — that's gamma

The question's second half points at exactly why delta is only an approximation: an option's price isn't a straight line against the stock price, it's a curve (as the payoff diagrams in Lessons 0.2 and 0.4 show), and delta is just that curve's local slope at the current stock price. As the stock moves, the slope itself changes. Gamma measures exactly how fast delta changes per $1 move in the stock — in effect, delta's own sensitivity.

Worked example 2 — using gamma to update delta

Delta 0.5, gamma 0.05, stock rises $4: new delta ≈ 0.5 + 0.05×4 = 0.7. A naive estimate using only the original delta would give 0.5×4 = $2.00 of option price movement. A better, convexity-adjusted estimate averages the old and new delta: ((0.5+0.7)/2)×4 = $2.40 — noticeably more than the naive linear estimate, because delta was rising throughout the move, not fixed at 0.5 the whole way.

\text{New Delta}\approx\text{Delta}+\text{Gamma}\times\Delta\text{Stock price}\qquad \text{Convexity-adjusted move}\approx\frac{\text{Delta}+\text{New Delta}}{2}\times\Delta\text{Stock price}
Deltathe option's price sensitivity to a $1 stock move, at the current stock price
Gammathe rate at which delta itself changes per $1 stock move

This is exactly why the payoff diagrams from earlier lessons are curved rather than straight: a tangent line at today's stock price (today's delta) is a good local approximation, but it drifts away from the actual, kinked payoff curve the further the stock moves from where that tangent was drawn.

Common traps
  • Delta itself changes as the stock price moves — the plain linear approximation is only accurate for small moves.
  • Forgetting gamma entirely for larger moves understates the true price change, since delta was rising (or falling) throughout the move, not fixed.
  • Treating delta as a fixed property of an option rather than something that shifts continuously with the stock price, time to expiry, and other factors.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Delta is quoted alongside every listed option, and is widely used as a rough proxy for an option's probability of expiring in the money.
  • Options market-makers "delta-hedge" large positions, trading the underlying stock to stay roughly neutral to small price moves.
  • Gamma risk is specifically why delta-hedges need frequent rebalancing — a hedge that's neutral today can drift out of balance after even a moderate stock move.

Check yourself

1. What does delta approximate, and what does gamma measure?
Delta approximates how much an option's price moves per $1 move in the underlying stock. Gamma measures how fast delta itself changes per $1 stock move.
2. Delta 0.3, stock moves $5. Estimated option move (linear)?
0.3 × 5 = $1.50.
3. Delta 0.4, gamma 0.04, stock falls $5. What's the new delta, and the convexity-adjusted price-move estimate?
New delta ≈ 0.4 + 0.04×(−5) = 0.2. Convexity-adjusted estimate ≈ ((0.4+0.2)/2)×(−5) = −$1.50.

Takeaways

  • Delta is a quick sensitivity approximation for an option's price — the local slope of its payoff curve.
  • Gamma measures how fast delta itself changes, which is why the linear approximation is most accurate for small moves and drifts for larger ones.
  • An option's price curve is better approximated using both delta and gamma together than delta alone, especially over bigger stock moves.
