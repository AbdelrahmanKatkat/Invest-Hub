Anchoring & framing

The questionA stock you bought at $100 is now $70. Does "it's down 30% from where I bought it" matter to what it's worth going forward?

Intuition

Anchoring is letting an often-irrelevant reference point (your purchase price, a past high) skew a current judgement. Framing is how the same fact, phrased differently, changes the decision — "70% of people survive" feels different from "30% die," even though they're identical.

Worked example

A $100 purchase now at $70 is, going forward, simply a $70 asset — its future prospects don't know or care what you paid. Anchoring to $100 can cause holding on "to get back to even" for a reason unrelated to the asset's actual current value.

\text{anchoring gap}=\text{reference price}-\text{current price}
Try this
  1. Change the reference price: the "gap" changes, but the asset's actual current value hasn't.
Common traps
  • "Getting back to even" is an anchor-driven goal, not a forward-looking investment reason.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Holding a losing position specifically to "break even" rather than reassessing it fresh.

Check yourself

1. What is anchoring?
Letting an often-irrelevant reference point skew a current judgement.
2. Bought at $50, now $35. What's the anchoring gap?
$15 (30% of the reference price).
3. Does an asset's future value depend on your original purchase price?
No — its future prospects don't depend on what any particular holder paid.

Takeaways

  • Anchoring attaches too much weight to an often-irrelevant reference point.
  • Framing changes decisions without changing the underlying facts.

Overconfidence

The questionYou're 90% sure a stock will rise. How often would predictions you're "90% sure" about actually need to come true for that confidence to be justified?

Intuition

Overconfidence is being more certain of your own judgement than your actual track record justifies. If your "90% confident" calls are only right 70% of the time, your confidence is miscalibrated — a commonly observed pattern in both everyday judgement and investing.

Worked example

Stated confidence 90%, actual accuracy 70%: miscalibration gap = 90 − 70 = 20 percentage points of overconfidence.

\text{miscalibration}=\text{stated confidence}-\text{actual accuracy}
Try this
  1. Bring actual accuracy up to match stated confidence: the gap closes to 0 — well-calibrated.
Common traps
  • Feeling certain is not the same as being accurate — the two are only loosely related without tracking a real history.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Investors overestimating their own ability to time market tops and bottoms.

Check yourself

1. What is overconfidence, precisely?
Being more certain of a judgement than your actual accuracy justifies.
2. Stated confidence 80%, actual accuracy 55%. Gap?
25 percentage points.
3. Is feeling very certain proof of being right?
No — confidence and accuracy are only loosely related without a tracked record.

Takeaways

  • Overconfidence is a measurable gap between stated certainty and actual accuracy.
  • Tracking a real record is the only way to check calibration.

Mental accounting

The questionA $500 tax refund and $500 of extra salary are both exactly $500. Do people treat them the same way?

Intuition

Mental accounting is treating money differently depending on its source or mental "label" — a windfall often gets spent more freely than equivalent earned income, even though both spend identically. The same applies to investment "buckets" that are really all one pool of money.

Worked example

If 80% of a $500 windfall gets spent versus 20% of $500 in regular salary, that's a 60-percentage-point gap in spending behavior for an identical dollar amount.

\text{spending gap}=\%\text{ spent (windfall)}-\%\text{ spent (salary)}
Try this
  1. Bring both spending percentages together: the gap shrinks to 0 — the money is being treated the same regardless of label.
Common traps
  • Treating a "play money" investing account differently from a "serious" one, even though both carry identical real-world risk.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Treating dividend income as "spendable" while treating an equal capital gain as "off-limits," though both are the same net worth.

Check yourself

1. What is mental accounting?
Treating money differently based on its source or label, even though it's fungible.
2. Why is this considered an inconsistency, not just a preference?
Because the dollars are economically identical and interchangeable regardless of their mental label.
3. Give an investing example of mental accounting.
Treating a "play money" account more carelessly than a "serious" one, despite identical real risk.

Takeaways

  • Money is fungible; mental labels shouldn't change its real value.
  • This bias can creep into how accounts or "buckets" are treated.

Prospect theory

The questionA $1,000 loss and a $1,000 gain are the same size. Do they feel the same size?

Intuition

Prospect theory describes how people weigh gains and losses relative to a reference point, and commonly cited research finds losses are felt more strongly than equivalent gains — a pattern consistent with the recovery-math asymmetry from Investment Fundamentals Lesson 0.9 (a 20% loss needs a 25% gain to undo).

Worked example

If losses are felt about twice as strongly as equivalent gains (an illustrative, commonly-cited finding, not a universal constant), a $1,000 loss might feel subjectively like "losing" roughly $2,000 worth of good feeling compared to a $1,000 gain's positive feeling.

\text{subjective loss}\approx\lambda\times\text{dollar loss},\quad \lambda>1
λa loss-aversion multiplier (commonly cited estimates are roughly 2, illustrative and debated)
Try this
  1. Set λ to 1: losses and gains are treated symmetrically — no loss aversion.
Common traps
  • Treating λ as a precise, universal number — estimates vary across studies and contexts.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Why a drawdown often feels worse than an equivalent earlier gain felt good — directly motivating Lesson 0.9's panic-selling example.

Check yourself

1. According to prospect theory, are losses or gains typically felt more strongly?
Losses.
2. Is the loss-aversion multiplier a precise universal constant?
No — commonly-cited estimates vary and are illustrative, not exact.
3. How does this connect to the breakeven-gain math in Investment Fundamentals Lesson 0.9?
Both describe a real asymmetry between losses and gains, one mathematical and one psychological.

Takeaways

  • Losses are commonly felt more strongly than equivalent gains.
  • This compounds with the mathematical asymmetry of recovering from a loss.

Debiasing techniques

The questionIf biases are built into how people think, can anything practical actually defend against them?

Intuition

Checklists, rules written in advance, and automation reduce the room for an in-the-moment biased decision — you're deciding the rule when calm, not deciding the trade when emotional. This is the same principle behind Investment Fundamentals Module 4's automated contributions and written plan.

Worked example

An illustrative "decision quality" score: a plan made with a written checklist followed consistently might score 85/100 for process discipline, versus 40/100 for ad-hoc, in-the-moment decisions — a stand-in for "a documented process tends to reduce impulsive mistakes," not a literal measurement.

Try this
  1. Compare a high checklist-adherence score to a low one and consider what kinds of decisions each would tend to produce.
Common traps
  • Having a plan doesn't help if it's abandoned whenever it's inconvenient — the discipline is the point.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Investment policy statements and automated contributions (Investment Fundamentals Module 4).

Check yourself

1. Why does deciding a rule "in advance" help?
It removes the in-the-moment, emotionally-charged decision that biases tend to exploit.
2. Name a concrete debiasing technique.
Any of: a written checklist, a pre-committed rule, automated/scheduled contributions.
3. Does having a written plan help if it's ignored under stress?
No — the benefit depends on actually following it.

Takeaways

  • Pre-committed rules and automation reduce room for in-the-moment bias.
  • A plan only helps if it's actually followed.
