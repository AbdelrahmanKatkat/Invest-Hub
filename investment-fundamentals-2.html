2.1

Equities in depth

The questionTwo companies both trade at $80 a share. One pays $2.40 a year in dividends and is expected to barely grow; the other pays no dividend at all but is reinvesting everything into growth. A year later, both happen to trade at $88. Which one actually gave you the better return — and is "dividend yield" alone even the right number to compare them with?

Intuition

Common stock carries voting rights — one vote per share at the annual shareholder meeting, often cast by proxy rather than in person — and a residual claim on profits: whatever is left after everyone else (employees, lenders, suppliers, preferred shareholders) is paid, including nothing at all in a bad year. Preferred stock sits ahead of common stock in priority and pays a more fixed-like dividend, usually without voting rights. A few more distinctions matter in practice:

  • Cumulative preferred stock: if a dividend is skipped, it accrues as a debt that must be paid in full before common shareholders see a cent. Non-cumulative preferred: a skipped dividend is simply gone.
  • For any dividend-paying stock, the dividend yield measures only the income piece of return (recall Lesson 0.2's R = income + price change) as a percentage of price — it says nothing about price change at all.
Worked example 1 — dividend yield

A stock at $80 paying $2.40/year: dividend yield = 2.40 / 80 = 3%. That's the income portion only — price change still adds or subtracts on top.

Build it up: total return and the payout ratio

Dividend yield alone can't answer the opening question — it ignores price change entirely. Total return adds both pieces together: the price gain (or loss) plus whatever dividends were paid, all divided by the starting price. It's the full, honest number for "how much did this investment actually make me."

Worked example 2 — total return answers the question

The dividend payer: price $80 → $88 plus $2.40 in dividends = (8 + 2.40)/80 = 13% total return. The non-payer: price $80 → $88, no dividend = (8 + 0)/80 = 10% total return. Same price move, but the dividend payer actually did better once the income piece is counted — exactly the comparison dividend yield alone couldn't make.

How much of earnings a company pays out versus keeps is its payout ratio — dividends per share divided by earnings per share (EPS). A low payout ratio doesn't mean shareholders get nothing; it usually means the company is choosing to reinvest in growth, or to buy back shares instead.

Worked example 3 — payout ratio

The dividend payer earns $4.00 EPS and pays a $2.40 dividend: payout ratio = 2.40/4.00 = 60% — a mature, income-oriented profile. The growth company also earns $4.00 EPS but pays no dividend: payout ratio = 0/4.00 = 0% — it's retaining 100% of earnings, betting that reinvesting (or buying back shares) creates more value than a cash payout would.

The formal definition

\text{Dividend yield}=\frac{\text{Annual dividend}}{\text{Price}}\qquad \text{Total return}=\frac{\text{Price}_{\text{end}}-\text{Price}_{\text{start}}+\text{Dividends}}{\text{Price}_{\text{start}}}\qquad \text{Payout ratio}=\frac{\text{Dividend per share}}{\text{EPS}}
Annual dividendtotal dividends paid per share over a year
Pricecurrent share price
Price_end, Price_startshare price at the end and start of the period
EPSearnings per share — net income divided by shares outstanding
Try this
  1. Hold the dividend fixed and lower the price: yield rises mechanically — a falling price alone can make yield look more generous.
  2. In the total-return calculator, zero out the dividend: total return collapses to price return alone — exactly the growth-stock case above.
Common traps
  • A high dividend yield can simply reflect a falling price, not a generous or sustainable payout.
  • Preferred dividends have priority over common, but are still not legally guaranteed the way a bond's coupon is — and non-cumulative preferred dividends that are skipped are gone for good, unlike cumulative preferred.
  • A 0% payout ratio doesn't mean a company returns nothing to shareholders — price appreciation and share buybacks both create value without showing up in a payout ratio.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Blue-chip dividend payers vs. non-dividend growth stocks are a common contrast in equity investing.
  • Some companies and banks issue preferred shares alongside common stock.
  • Share buybacks are a widely used alternative (or complement) to dividends, especially among large technology companies that pay little or no dividend.

Check yourself

1. What's the key difference between common and preferred stock, and between cumulative and non-cumulative preferred?
Common has voting rights and a residual (last-in-line) claim; preferred has priority over common for dividends but usually no voting rights. Cumulative preferred: a skipped dividend accrues and must be paid before common dividends resume. Non-cumulative: a skipped dividend is simply forfeited.
2. A stock's price rises from $100 to $106 over a year and pays $3 in dividends. What's its total return, and how much came from price vs. income?
Total return = (106 − 100 + 3)/100 = 9%: 6 percentage points from price, 3 from the dividend.
3. A company earns $5 EPS and pays a $1 dividend. What's its payout ratio, and what does a low payout ratio typically suggest?
1/5 = 20% — a low payout ratio, suggesting the company is retaining most of its earnings, likely to reinvest in growth or buy back shares rather than pay them out.

Takeaways

  • Dividend yield is only the income piece of return; total return adds price change and dividends together.
  • Payout ratio shows how much of earnings is paid out versus retained — income-oriented stocks typically pay out more, growth stocks typically retain more.
  • Preferred stock has payment priority over common but usually no vote; cumulative vs. non-cumulative changes what happens to a missed payment.
2.2

Bonds & yield

The questionYou buy a bond with a 4% coupon on $1,000 face value for $950, due to mature in 10 years. Interest rates then move. Why does the bond's price change at all if the coupon payment never changes — and does the simple "coupon ÷ price" yield fully capture the return of holding it all the way to maturity?

Intuition

A bond pays a fixed coupon (based on its face value) regardless of what happens to interest rates. If rates elsewhere rise, new bonds offer more, so an existing bond with a now-less-attractive fixed coupon must trade at a lower price to offer a competitive yield — and vice versa. Price and yield move inversely.

Worked example 1 — current yield

$1,000 face value, 4% coupon = $40/year. Bought at $950: current yield = 40/950 ≈ 4.21%. If the price later rises to $1,050 (rates fell): current yield = 40/1050 ≈ 3.81% — same $40 coupon, lower yield, because you paid more for it.

Build it up: approximating yield to maturity

Current yield only counts coupon income relative to today's price — it ignores the fact that a bond bought below face value ($950) will also gain $50 as its price converges to $1,000 by maturity (or lose value if bought above face value). Yield to maturity (YTM) folds both pieces together: the coupon income and that built-in gain or loss, spread evenly over the years remaining. The exact YTM has no simple formula — it's the single discount rate that makes the present value (Lesson 0.3) of every future coupon plus the face value exactly equal today's price, solved by a bond calculator. A widely used approximation gets close without that machinery:

Worked example 2 — approximate YTM, a discount bond

Face $1,000, coupon $40, price $950, 10 years to maturity: YTM ≈ [40 + (1000−950)/10] / [(1000+950)/2] = [40+5]/975 = 45/975 ≈ 4.62% — higher than the 4.21% current yield, because this bond also has $50 of built-in price gain still to come.

Worked example 3 — approximate YTM, a premium bond

Same bond bought instead at $1,050 (above face value): YTM ≈ [40 + (1000−1050)/10] / [(1000+1050)/2] = [40−5]/1025 = 35/1025 ≈ 3.41% — lower than its 3.81% current yield, because this bond must lose $50 of price as it converges down to face value by maturity.

The formal definition

\text{Current yield}=\frac{\text{Coupon}}{\text{Price}}\qquad \text{Approx. YTM}\approx\frac{C+\frac{F-P}{n}}{\frac{F+P}{2}}
Coupon (C)the fixed annual dollar payment, set when the bond is issued
Price (P)the bond's current market price
Face value (F)the amount paid back at maturity, typically $1,000 per bond
nyears remaining until maturity
Try this
  1. Raise the price while the coupon stays fixed: current yield falls, demonstrating the inverse relationship.
  2. In the YTM calculator, set price equal to face value: approximate YTM equals the coupon rate exactly — no capital gain or loss left to add.
Common traps
  • Current yield isn't the same as yield to maturity, which also accounts for the bond's price converging to face value by maturity.
  • Rising interest rates push existing bond prices down, not up.
  • The approximation formula is for intuition, not precision — a bond desk or bond fund actually solves the exact equation iteratively; the approximation can be off by a noticeable amount for long maturities or large discounts/premiums.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • A central bank interest-rate hike tends to reprice already-issued bonds downward.
  • Bond fund fact sheets commonly report a current or distribution yield figure.
  • A bond fund's reported "SEC yield" or "yield to maturity" is conceptually much closer to YTM than to current yield alone.

Check yourself

1. If market interest rates rise, what typically happens to existing bond prices?
They fall — their fixed coupon becomes less attractive relative to new, higher-paying bonds.
2. Face value $1,000, coupon $50, price $900, 5 years to maturity. Approximate YTM?
[50 + (1000−900)/5] / [(1000+900)/2] = [50+20]/950 = 70/950 ≈ 7.37%.
3. Why can yield to maturity differ from current yield for the exact same bond?
Current yield only counts coupon income over today's price; YTM also folds in the capital gain or loss that happens as the bond's price converges to face value by maturity — a discount bond's YTM runs above its current yield, a premium bond's below.

Takeaways

  • A bond's coupon is fixed; its price and yield are not, and they move inversely.
  • Current yield counts only coupon income; approximate YTM adds the built-in gain or loss to maturity.
  • Discount bonds (price below face) have YTM above current yield; premium bonds (price above face) have YTM below current yield.

Cash & money market

The questionA money-market fund yields 5% while inflation runs at 3%. Are you really 5% ahead — and what if that gap runs negative and you leave a large balance sitting there for 20 years rather than one?

Intuition

Cash and near-cash instruments — T-bills, CDs, money-market funds — are very low risk in nominal dollar terms. But what those dollars can actually buy also matters: the real return adjusts the nominal return for inflation. A simple approximation subtracts inflation from the nominal rate; the exact calculation divides.

Worked example 1 — real return in one year

Nominal 5%, inflation 3%: the quick approximation gives 5% − 3% = 2%. The more exact figure is (1.05/1.03) − 1 ≈ 1.94% — close to the approximation, but not identical.

Build it up: the long-run cost of holding cash

A real return isn't just a one-year snapshot — like any other return, it compounds year after year (the same engine from Lesson 0.1, now potentially running in reverse). A real return that's only mildly negative can look harmless for one year, but left running for decades it steadily erodes purchasing power — exactly the way a small annual fee quietly compounds against a saver in Lesson 2.7.

Worked example 2 — compounding a negative real return

$10,000 held at a −1% real return (say, 3% nominal against 4% inflation) for 20 years: 10,000 × (0.99)²⁰ ≈ $8,179 of today's purchasing power — an 18.2% real loss, even though the nominal account balance never fell. Stretch it to 30 years at the same rate: 10,000 × (0.99)³⁰ ≈ $7,397 — the loss keeps compounding the longer the cash sits idle.

The formal definition

\text{Real return}=\frac{1+\text{nominal}}{1+\text{inflation}}-1\qquad \text{Purchasing power}_t=P\,(1+r_{\text{real}})^{t}
nominalthe stated return, before adjusting for inflation
inflationthe rate at which prices in general are rising
Pthe starting dollar amount
tyears held
Try this
  1. Set nominal equal to inflation: real return goes to exactly 0% — you've only kept pace, not gotten ahead.
  2. In the purchasing-power plot, try a small positive real rate and a small negative one over 30 years: the gap between the two curves grows far more than it looks like it should at year one.
Common traps
  • "Low volatility" is not the same as "good after inflation" — cash can lose purchasing power steadily even while its nominal value never drops.
  • The subtraction approximation slightly overstates real return compared to the exact formula.
  • A persistently negative real return, even a small one, compounds into a large cumulative loss of purchasing power over decades — the same math that grows wealth also erodes it when the rate runs negative.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • T-bills, CDs and money-market funds are commonly used as the cash-equivalent allocation discussed in Lesson 0.2.
  • Inflation-protected securities are often quoted directly in "real yield" terms.
  • Savers who park large balances in cash for many years (well beyond an emergency fund) have historically seen the biggest gap between nominal stability and real purchasing-power loss.

Check yourself

1. Can a cash investment have a negative real return even with a positive nominal return?
Yes — if inflation exceeds the nominal rate, purchasing power falls even though the dollar amount grew.
2. $10,000 earns a real return of −2% a year for 10 years. What's left in today's purchasing power?
10,000 × (0.98)¹⁰ ≈ $8,171 — about an 18.3% real loss, even though the nominal balance grew every single year.
3. Why is cash still useful despite low (or negative) real returns?
It offers stability and immediate liquidity — a very different job from growth, useful for near-term needs and emergency funds, not a substitute for long-term investing.

Takeaways

  • Real return adjusts nominal return for inflation, and it can be zero or negative even when nominal is positive.
  • Real returns compound over time just like any other return — a small negative real rate still erodes meaningful purchasing power over decades.
  • Cash's job is stability and liquidity, not growth — appropriate for near-term needs, not a long-term holding.

Real estate & REITs

The questionA REIT trades at $25 and pays $1.50/year in distributions. Is that the same kind of number as the dividend yield from Lesson 2.1? And a friend instead buys a similar building directly, using a mortgage — does borrowing money to buy property make their return better, worse, or just different?

Intuition

Direct property ownership is illiquid, management-intensive, and often financed with a mortgage. A REIT (real estate investment trust) is a company that owns income-producing property and trades like a stock on an exchange, while being required to distribute most of its taxable income to shareholders — giving real-estate-like income exposure with stock-like liquidity. The math is the same income/price-change split from Lesson 0.2.

Worked example 1 — distribution yield

A REIT at $25 paying $1.50/year: distribution yield = 1.50/25 = 6% — computed exactly like the dividend yield in Lesson 2.1.

Build it up: leverage changes the return, not just the size of the deal

Direct property is usually measured unlevered first, using the capitalization ("cap") rate: net operating income (rent collected minus operating costs, before any mortgage payment) divided by the property's value. It's the real-estate equivalent of an all-cash yield.

Worked example 2 — cap rate

A $300,000 property generating $21,000/year in net operating income: cap rate = 21,000/300,000 = 7% — what an all-cash buyer would earn, before financing enters the picture.

Now add a mortgage. Buying with 20% down ($60,000) and financing the rest ($240,000) at an interest-only 5% rate changes the cash-on-cash return — the return on just the cash actually put in:

Worked example 3 — leverage, both directions

Mortgage interest = 240,000 × 5% = $12,000/year. Cash-on-cash = (21,000 − 12,000)/60,000 = 15% — more than double the unlevered 7%, because the 5% borrowing cost is below the 7% cap rate ("positive leverage"). But if the mortgage rate were instead 8% (above the cap rate): interest = 240,000 × 8% = $19,200/year, cash-on-cash = (21,000 − 19,200)/60,000 = 3% — well below the unlevered 7%, because now leverage amplifies the cost instead of the return ("negative leverage").

The formal definition

\text{Distribution yield}=\frac{\text{Annual distribution}}{\text{Price}}\qquad \text{Cap rate}=\frac{\text{NOI}}{\text{Value}}\qquad \text{Cash-on-cash}=\frac{\text{NOI}-\text{Interest}}{\text{Down payment}}
Annual distributioncash paid out per share over a year
NOInet operating income — rent collected minus operating costs, before any mortgage payment
Valuethe property's current market value
Down payment, Interestthe cash paid upfront, and the annual interest on the financed remainder
Try this
  1. Raise the price with the distribution fixed: yield falls, the same pattern as Lesson 2.1's dividend yield.
  2. In the leverage calculator, push the mortgage rate above the cap rate: cash-on-cash falls below the unlevered cap rate — leverage flips from amplifying gains to amplifying cost.
Common traps
  • A REIT's share price can be volatile like a stock even when the underlying buildings' value is comparatively stable.
  • A high distribution yield can reflect a falling price rather than rising income, same as any dividend yield.
  • Leverage amplifies both directions — it raises cash-on-cash return when borrowing is cheaper than the cap rate, but just as mechanically shrinks it (or turns it negative) when borrowing costs rise above the cap rate.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Publicly-traded REIT index funds are a common way to access real estate without buying property directly.
  • Direct real-estate investors and analysts routinely quote cap rates to compare properties independent of how each deal happens to be financed.
  • A tightening cycle that raises mortgage rates (Economics path, Lesson 0.3) can flip a profitable levered property into a money-loser without the property's rents changing at all.

Check yourself

1. What makes a REIT more liquid than owning property directly?
It trades like a stock on an exchange, so shares can be bought and sold quickly, unlike selling a physical building.
2. A property has NOI of $40,000 and is worth $500,000. What's its cap rate?
40,000/500,000 = 8%.
3. With a $100,000 down payment, a $400,000 interest-only mortgage at 6%, and $40,000 NOI, what's the cash-on-cash return — and is this positive or negative leverage relative to the 8% cap rate?
Interest = 400,000 × 6% = $24,000. Cash-on-cash = (40,000 − 24,000)/100,000 = 16% — above the 8% cap rate, so this is positive leverage (the 6% borrowing cost is below the 8% cap rate).

Takeaways

  • A REIT trades real-estate income exposure for stock-like liquidity; distribution yield is computed the same way as dividend yield.
  • Cap rate (NOI ÷ value) is the standard unlevered measure of a direct property's income return.
  • Leverage amplifies cash-on-cash return above the cap rate when borrowing is cheap, and drags it below the cap rate when borrowing is expensive — it changes risk, not just size.
2.5

Commodities

The questionGold trades at $2,400/oz today. A contract for delivery in 3 months is priced at $2,430/oz. Why would the "same" gold cost more later? And a fund that holds these futures has to keep "rolling" from an expiring contract into the next one before delivery comes due — does that rolling process help or hurt the fund's return, separately from whatever the spot price does?

Intuition

The spot price is for buying and taking delivery now; the futures price is an agreement today to exchange at a set price later. The gap between them (the basis) reflects storage costs, financing costs and expectations about supply and demand. Commodities are physical, largely interchangeable goods often held for diversification or as a historical, imperfect inflation hedge.

Worked example 1 — basis

Spot $2,400, 3-month futures $2,430: basis = 2430 − 2400 = $30 — the futures price sits above spot, a pattern often called "contango".

Build it up: roll yield — a hidden return source (or cost)

A futures-based commodity fund can't hold a contract forever — before it expires, the fund sells the expiring ("near") contract and buys the next ("far") one, to stay continuously invested without ever taking physical delivery. That swap itself either costs or earns money, independent of what the spot price does:

  • In contango (far price above near price, as in the opening example), rolling means selling low and buying high — a drag called negative roll yield.
  • In backwardation (far price below near price), rolling means selling high and buying low — positive roll yield.
Worked example 2 — negative roll yield in contango

Near-month contract at $2,430 is expiring; the next (far) contract trades at $2,460. Rolling: roll yield = (2,430 − 2,460)/2,430 ≈ −1.23% — a cost paid just to stay invested, even if the spot price doesn't move at all.

Worked example 3 — positive roll yield in backwardation

Same near contract at $2,430, but now the far contract trades at $2,400 instead (backwardation): roll yield = (2,430 − 2,400)/2,430 ≈ +1.23% — this time rolling adds return, again independent of the spot price.

The formal definition

\text{Basis}=\text{Futures price}-\text{Spot price}\qquad \text{Roll yield}\approx\frac{\text{Near}-\text{Far}}{\text{Near}}
Spot priceprice for immediate delivery
Futures priceagreed price for delivery at a future date
Nearprice of the contract about to expire, which the fund is selling
Farprice of the next contract the fund rolls into
Try this
  1. Set futures below spot: basis goes negative — a pattern often called "backwardation".
  2. In the roll-yield calculator, set the far price below the near price: roll yield turns positive — backwardation rewards holding a long futures position through the roll.
Common traps
  • Commodities generally pay no income — unlike stocks or bonds, their return comes entirely from price change (and, for funds, futures-rolling mechanics).
  • "Inflation hedge" describes a historical tendency, not a guarantee in any given period.
  • Roll yield is separate from, and can offset or add to, whatever the spot price itself does — a futures-based commodity fund's total return is spot price change plus (or minus) accumulated roll yield, not spot price change alone.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Gold and oil are widely held commodities; many commodity ETFs gain exposure through futures contracts rather than physical storage.
  • Long stretches of contango in oil futures markets have historically been cited as a persistent drag on some oil ETFs' returns, even in periods when the spot oil price was roughly flat.

Check yourself

1. What's the difference between a spot price and a futures price?
Spot is for immediate delivery; futures is an agreed price for delivery at a later date.
2. Spot is $80, futures is $77. What's the basis, and what is this pattern called?
77 − 80 = −$3; futures below spot is called backwardation.
3. A fund rolls from a near contract at $90 into a far contract at $93. What's the roll yield, and is this contango or backwardation?
(90 − 93)/90 ≈ −3.33%; far above near is contango, producing a negative roll yield.

Takeaways

  • Basis is the gap between futures and spot prices.
  • Rolling from an expiring contract into the next creates roll yield — a cost in contango, a benefit in backwardation — separate from spot price moves.
  • Commodities pay no income; return comes from price change plus, for futures-based funds, accumulated roll yield.
2.6

Cryptocurrencies

The questionCryptocurrencies have historically swung in price far more than the stock market. How much more, roughly, and why should that change how large a position you'd hold? And if a highly volatile asset averages exactly 0% simple return across an up year and a down year, does that mean you actually broke even holding it?

Intuition

A cryptocurrency is a digital, decentralized asset with no central issuer. Historically, major cryptocurrencies have been among the most volatile widely-held asset classes — recall the risk/return map from Lesson 0.2, where crypto sat far to the right. Extreme volatility means both potential gains and potential losses are larger, so position size (how much of a portfolio is exposed to it) matters more than for calmer assets.

Worked example 1 — relative volatility

Using the illustrative figures from Lesson 0.2's map — diversified stocks at 16% volatility, crypto at 65% — crypto's volatility there is 0.65/0.16 ≈ 4.1 times that of diversified stocks.

Build it up: volatility drag — why wild swings cost more than they look like they should

Sequential returns don't average — they compound, multiplying together (Lesson 0.1). That has a surprising consequence: a symmetric up-swing followed by an equal-sized down-swing does not cancel out to zero. This gap between the simple average of a set of returns and what actually happens when they compound together is called volatility drag, and it grows rapidly — not just proportionally — with the size of the swings.

Worked example 2 — a severe swing

+50% one year, then −50% the next. Simple average of the two returns: (50% + (−50%))/2 = 0%. But compounded: (1.50)(0.50) = 0.75, a cumulative return of −25% — a real loss, directly answering the opening question: no, averaging to 0% does not mean you broke even.

Worked example 3 — a calmer swing, for comparison

+10% then −10% (a calmer, more stock-like pair): simple average is still 0%, but compounded: (1.10)(0.90) = 0.99, a cumulative return of only −1% — far smaller than the −25% from the ±50% pair. Volatility drag scales up fast as swings get larger, which is exactly why crypto's much bigger swings matter so much more than an ordinary stock's.

The formal definition

\text{Relative volatility}=\frac{\sigma_A}{\sigma_B}\qquad \text{Cumulative growth}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+r_i)
σA, σBthe standard deviation of returns for asset A and asset B (Lesson 0.5)
rᵢthe return in period i
nnumber of periods compounded together

  2. In the Π/Σ machine below, switch the operation to Π (multiply) and step through the alternating ±50% terms — watch the running product fall well below 1, even though the terms alternate by the same size each time.
Common traps
  • High volatility cuts both ways — large gains and large losses are both more likely than in a lower-volatility asset.
  • Past volatility is not a guarantee of future volatility, in either direction.
  • A string of symmetric-looking up/down swings does not cancel out to zero — compounding is multiplicative, so volatility itself creates a persistent drag on cumulative return, before even considering which direction the asset trends.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Bitcoin and other major cryptocurrencies have historically shown large price swings relative to broad equity indices.
  • Leveraged and inverse crypto-tracking products are especially exposed to volatility drag, since they rebalance and compound a multiplied daily return.

Check yourself

1. Does "highly volatile" mean "more likely to lose money"?
Not exactly — it means outcomes are more spread out in both directions, so both larger gains and larger losses are more likely than with a calmer asset.
2. If σA = 40% and σB = 10%, how many times more volatile is A?
40/10 = 4 times.
3. An asset returns +50% one year and −50% the next. What's its cumulative two-year return, and why isn't it 0% even though the average of +50% and −50% is 0%?
(1.50)(0.50) − 1 = −25%. It isn't 0% because compounding multiplies the returns together rather than averaging them — a 50% gain followed by a 50% loss leaves you with 75% of your original money, a real loss, not a break-even.

Takeaways

  • Cryptocurrencies have historically been among the most volatile widely-held asset classes.
  • Volatility drag means compounded sequential returns can fall well below the simple average of those returns — larger swings create disproportionately larger drag.
  • Higher volatility means both larger possible gains and larger possible losses, and argues for smaller position sizing.
2.7

Mutual funds & ETFs

The questionTwo funds track the same index. One charges a 0.03% annual fee; the other charges 1%. Does that tiny-looking gap actually matter? And separately — when you buy or sell a share of an ETF on an exchange, who's actually making sure its price doesn't drift far from the value of what it holds?

Intuition

Mutual funds and ETFs pool many investors' money into one diversified basket. Active funds try to beat a benchmark and typically charge more for the attempt; passive (index) funds simply track a benchmark, usually far more cheaply. The annual expense ratio is deducted every year, which means it compounds against you — the same compounding engine from Lesson 0.1, now working in reverse.

Worked example 1 — fee drag over decades

$10,000 at a 7% gross annual return for 30 years: with a 0.03% fee (net 6.97%) it grows to ≈ $75,485; with a 1% fee (net 6%) it grows to ≈ $57,435 — an $18,050 gap, purely from a fee difference that sounds small year to year.

Build it up: how an ETF actually stays priced near its NAV

A mutual fund is only priced and traded once per day, directly with the fund company, at its NAV (net asset value — the value of everything it holds, per share). An ETF trades continuously on an exchange, like a stock, which raises a question: what stops its market price from drifting away from the value of what it actually holds? The answer is the creation/redemption mechanism: large institutions called authorized participants (APs) can assemble the ETF's underlying basket of securities and hand it to the fund in exchange for new ETF shares ("creation"), or hand in ETF shares to receive the underlying basket back ("redemption"). Whenever the ETF's market price drifts from NAV, this creates a profitable arbitrage for an AP — and closing that arbitrage pushes the price back toward NAV.

Worked example 2 — a premium, and how it gets closed

An ETF's NAV is $50.00, but it's trading at $50.15 — a premium of (50.15 − 50.00)/50.00 = 0.3%. An AP can buy the underlying basket of stocks for about $50.00 worth, deliver it to the fund to create new ETF shares, then sell those shares on the exchange at $50.15 — a close-to-riskless profit. That creation increases the ETF's share supply, which pushes its market price back down toward NAV.

Worked example 3 — a discount, the mirror image

Now suppose the same ETF trades at $49.80 instead — a discount of (49.80 − 50.00)/50.00 = −0.4%. An AP can buy the cheap ETF shares on the exchange, redeem them with the fund for the underlying basket (worth $50.00), and come out ahead — this redemption shrinks ETF share supply, pushing the market price back up toward NAV.

The formal definition

FV=P\,(1+r_{\text{gross}}-\text{fee})^{t}\qquad \text{Premium/discount}=\frac{\text{Market price}-\text{NAV}}{\text{NAV}}
r_grossthe investment's return before fees
feethe annual expense ratio
tyears invested
NAVnet asset value — the per-share value of everything the fund holds
Try this
  1. Set both fees equal: the ending-value gap drops to zero.
  2. Stretch years out further: the gap between the two fee scenarios grows faster than years do.
  3. In the premium/discount calculator, set price below NAV: you get a negative (discount) number — the same arbitrage mechanism works in reverse to push price back up.
Common traps
  • A "1% fee" is not 1% of your money once — it's 1% of your money every single year, compounding.
  • Paying for active management doesn't reliably mean beating the benchmark after fees.
  • A persistent premium or discount is unusual for a liquid ETF with an active creation/redemption mechanism — it's far more common in less-liquid ETFs holding hard-to-trade underlying assets, or during periods of severe market stress when arbitrage temporarily breaks down.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Low-cost broad-market index ETFs vs. higher-fee actively managed mutual funds is a common comparison in fund research.
  • Every fund discloses its expense ratio in its fact sheet or prospectus.
  • An ETF's "premium/discount to NAV" and bid-ask spread are both commonly listed stats on its fact sheet or issuer website, alongside the expense ratio.

Check yourself

1. Is an expense ratio charged once or every year?
Every year, as long as the fund is held — which is what makes it compound against the investor over time.
2. Two funds both return 8% gross; one charges 0.1%, the other 0.8%. What are the net rates?
7.9% and 7.2%.
3. An ETF's NAV is $100 and it trades at $100.60. What's the premium, and how does the creation/redemption mechanism tend to correct it?
(100.60 − 100)/100 = 0.6% premium. An authorized participant can assemble the underlying basket (worth about $100), deliver it to create new ETF shares, and sell those shares at $100.60 — the resulting increase in supply pushes the market price back down toward NAV.

Takeaways

  • Active funds try to beat a benchmark and typically cost more; passive funds track it cheaply, and fees compound against you the same way returns compound for you.
  • A small-looking annual fee difference can become large over decades.
  • The creation/redemption mechanism, run by authorized participants, is what keeps an ETF's market price close to its NAV — a key structural difference from a traditional mutual fund.
