Modern portfolio theory

The questionYou've picked what look like five "good" individual investments. Is a portfolio built from five good picks automatically a good portfolio — or is portfolio construction its own separate problem, with its own separate math?

Intuition

Modern portfolio theory (MPT), developed by Harry Markowitz in 1952, treats portfolio construction as a whole-portfolio optimisation problem, not an asset-by-asset one. The insight sounds almost too simple once stated: an asset's risk and return in isolation matter less than how it behaves alongside everything else already in the portfolio. A volatile asset that tends to move opposite the rest of the portfolio can make the whole portfolio calmer, even though it is the riskiest single thing sitting inside it.

This is exactly the two-asset diversification math from Investment Fundamentals Lesson 0.7, generalised to many assets at once. MPT needs three kinds of input for every asset and pair of assets:

  • Each asset's expected return.
  • Each asset's risk (standard deviation, σ).
  • The correlation between every pair of assets — how closely they move together.

From those inputs, a portfolio's expected return is always just the weighted average of the individual returns. Its risk is not a weighted average — it depends on the correlations, which is the entire point of the exercise.

Worked example 1 — recap: two assets

Two assets, each σ = 20%, correlation ρ = 0.3, blended 50/50: portfolio σ = √(0.5²×0.2² + 0.5²×0.2² + 2×0.5×0.5×0.3×0.2²) ≈ 16.1% — lower than either asset's own 20%, with no sacrifice in expected return if both assets share the same expected return. MPT asks the next question: for a given target risk, which exact mix of many assets maximises expected return?

Build it up: from two assets to many — the risk floor

Diversifying across more assets keeps helping, but not without limit. For n equally-weighted assets, each with the same σ and the same average pairwise correlation ρ, the portfolio's variance is given below — and as n grows very large, it doesn't fall to zero. It falls toward a floor set entirely by ρ: the risk the assets all share in common and can never diversify away from each other.

Worked example 2 — risk shrinks, but toward a floor, not to zero

Holding σ = 20% and ρ = 0.3 fixed and only changing the number of equally-weighted assets n: n = 2 gives portfolio σ ≈ 16.1% (matches worked example 1); n = 10 gives σ ≈ 12.2%; n = 50 gives σ ≈ 11.2%. As n → ∞, σ approaches a floor of √(ρσ²) ≈ 11.0% — diversification keeps helping all the way up, but it can never fully cancel the risk that all the assets share in common once ρ > 0.

\text{Var}_n=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}+\frac{n-1}{n}\,\rho\,\sigma^2 \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} \rho\,\sigma^2
nnumber of equally-weighted assets
σeach asset's own standard deviation (assumed equal here, for simplicity)
ρaverage pairwise correlation between the assets
Varₙthe n-asset portfolio's variance; its square root is portfolio σ

  2. In the n-asset calculator below, push n very high with ρ fixed above 0: portfolio risk keeps falling but visibly levels off near the ρσ² floor rather than reaching zero.
Common traps
  • MPT relies on estimated returns, risks and correlations — the output is only as good as those estimates, and correlations tend to shift (often rising right when diversification is needed most, in a crisis).
  • Assuming more assets always means proportionally less risk — past a few dozen holdings, additional diversification runs into the ρσ² floor and delivers rapidly diminishing benefit.
  • Treating MPT as if it finds "the" single right portfolio — it finds the best portfolio given a particular set of input estimates, which is a narrower and more fragile claim.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Most target-date and robo-advisor allocation models trace back to MPT's mean-variance framework.
  • Broad index funds (a total U.S. stock market index fund, for example) are themselves an application of the "many assets, low pairwise correlation within the category" idea.
  • Institutional asset-allocation studies that estimate a full covariance matrix across asset classes before setting a policy mix.

Check yourself

1. Is MPT an asset-by-asset or whole-portfolio approach, and what three inputs does it need per asset/pair?
Whole-portfolio. It needs each asset's expected return, each asset's risk (σ), and the correlation between every pair of assets.
2. With σ = 20% and ρ = 0.3 for every asset, does portfolio risk ever reach 0% no matter how many assets you add?
No — it falls toward a floor of √(ρσ²) = √(0.3×0.04) ≈ 11.0% as n grows, because the shared (correlated) component of risk can't be diversified away.
3. MPT's output is only as good as what?
Its input estimates (expected returns, risks, correlations) — garbage in, garbage out.

Takeaways

  • MPT generalises two-asset diversification to a whole portfolio of many assets at once.
  • Adding more assets lowers risk toward a floor set by their average correlation, not to zero.
  • Its quality depends entirely on its input estimates, which are never perfectly known in advance.

The efficient frontier

The questionTwo portfolios have the same expected return, but one has lower risk. Is there ever a reason to hold the higher-risk one? And once you've picked the best mix of risky assets, should every investor then hold that exact same mix?

Intuition

Feed MPT's inputs (Lesson 0.1) into an optimiser across many candidate portfolios and plot each one's risk against its expected return. The efficient frontier is the upper-left edge of that whole cloud of points — the set of portfolios offering the best possible expected return for each level of risk. A portfolio sitting below the frontier is dominated: some other portfolio offers more return for the same risk, or less risk for the same return, so there's no good reason to hold the dominated one.

Worked example 1 — a stylised frontier shape

Return rising with the square root of risk: at risk 9% the frontier might offer about 6% return; at risk 16% about 8% — return keeps rising with risk, but at a decreasing rate, since the curve flattens as σ² swamps σ.

\text{Return}\approx a\sqrt{\text{Risk}}+b

Build it up: the one portfolio everyone should mix with cash

The frontier alone doesn't say which single point an investor should pick — that still depends on personal risk tolerance. But add one more asset, a risk-free one (Rf), and something remarkable happens: a straight line can be drawn from Rf, tangent to the curved frontier, touching it at exactly one point — the tangency portfolio. That line, the Capital Market Line (CML), dominates every point on the curved frontier below it, because mixing the tangency portfolio with the risk-free asset in different proportions lets an investor reach any risk level along that straight line — always with a better return-per-unit-of-risk than the curve itself offers. The line's slope is exactly the Sharpe ratio (Investment Fundamentals Lesson 0.6) of the tangency portfolio: excess return per unit of risk. This is why, under MPT's assumptions, every investor should hold the same tangency portfolio of risky assets, and only adjust their personal risk by changing how much cash or borrowing they mix in alongside it — a conclusion sometimes called the "two-fund separation theorem."

Worked example 2 — finding the tangency portfolio by Sharpe ratio

Risk-free rate 2%. Portfolio X: return 8%, risk 16% → Sharpe = (8−2)/16 = 0.375. Portfolio Y: return 10%, risk 25% → Sharpe = (10−2)/25 = 0.320. Even though Y has the higher raw return, X has the higher Sharpe ratio — X sits closer to the tangency portfolio, and combining X with cash or leverage can match Y's risk level while beating its return.

Worked example 3 — the same idea with the risk/return map's own categories

Using the risk/return map below: "Diversified stocks" (σ=16%, return 9%) has Sharpe = (9−2)/16 = 0.4375. "Single stock" (σ=30%, return 10%) has Sharpe = (10−2)/30 = 0.2667 — a noticeably higher raw return, but a much lower Sharpe ratio. Diversified stocks nearly dominates single stock here: only a little less return for much less risk, which is exactly the "dominated" idea from this lesson's opening question, made concrete.

E[R]=R_f+\text{Sharpe}_{\text{tangency}}\times\sigma \qquad \text{Sharpe}=\frac{E[R]-R_f}{\sigma}
Rfthe risk-free rate
Sharpeexcess return (over Rf) per unit of risk — the Capital Market Line's slope at the tangency portfolio
σportfolio risk (standard deviation)

Common traps
  • The frontier is built from estimates and shifts as those estimates change — it isn't a fixed, known curve sitting out in the real world waiting to be read off.
  • Picking the highest-return portfolio instead of the highest-Sharpe one — worked example 2 shows these are not the same portfolio.
  • Forgetting that the two-fund separation result depends on every investor sharing the same return/risk/correlation estimates — in practice, investors disagree, so the "one right tangency portfolio" is itself an estimate, not a universal fact.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Portfolio optimisation software (used by advisors and institutional allocators) plots and selects portfolios along an estimated frontier.
  • Fund fact sheets and fund-comparison tools that quote Sharpe ratio alongside raw return, exactly so investors can spot near-dominated choices.
  • Target-date funds gradually shifting their own risky-asset/cash mix along something like a capital market line as the target date approaches.

Check yourself

1. What does "dominated" mean for a portfolio?
Some other portfolio offers more return for the same risk, or less risk for the same return — so the dominated one has no good reason to be held.
2. Portfolio A: return 7%, risk 10%. Portfolio B: return 9%, risk 20%. Rf = 1%. Which has the higher Sharpe ratio?
A: (7−1)/10 = 0.60. B: (9−1)/20 = 0.40. A has the higher Sharpe ratio, even though B has the higher raw return.
3. Is the real-world efficient frontier a fixed, known curve, and why does the tangency portfolio matter?
No — it's built from estimates that shift over time. The tangency portfolio matters because mixing it with cash lets any investor reach a given risk level with a better return than the curved frontier itself offers at that same risk.

Takeaways

  • The efficient frontier shows the best return available at each risk level; it's an estimate, not a fixed fact.
  • The tangency portfolio (where a line from the risk-free rate touches the frontier) has the highest Sharpe ratio of any portfolio on the curve.
  • Mixing the tangency portfolio with cash or borrowing — not picking a different risky mix — is how an investor should dial risk up or down, under MPT's assumptions.

CAPM & beta

The questionRisk-free rate 2%, the market is expected to return 8%, and a stock has a beta of 1.3. What return does CAPM suggest is "fair" for that stock's risk — and what should you conclude if the stock actually delivers more, or less, than that?

Intuition

Beta (β) measures an asset's sensitivity to overall market moves: a beta of 1.3 means it tends to move about 30% more than the market, in the same direction; a beta of 0.5 means about half as much movement; a beta near 0 (or negative) means little relationship to the market at all, or even an opposite one. The Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) uses beta — and only beta — to estimate a "fair" expected return for that level of market-related risk: start from the risk-free rate, then add a premium scaled by how much market risk the asset carries.

Worked example 1 — the basic estimate

2% + 1.3×(8% − 2%) = 2% + 7.8% = 9.8%.

E[R]=R_f+\beta\,(R_m-R_f)
Rfrisk-free rate
Rmexpected market return
βsensitivity to market moves

Build it up: the Security Market Line, and alpha

Plot CAPM's expected return against beta for every possible value of beta, holding Rf and Rm fixed, and the result is a straight line: the Security Market Line (SML). Every asset should, in theory, sit exactly on that line. When a real asset's actual return differs from what the SML says it "should" earn for its beta, that gap has a name: alpha — the return left over after stripping out the part explained by market exposure. Positive alpha means an asset outperformed what its market risk alone would predict; negative alpha means it underperformed. A beta near zero — or negative — is also worth a closer look: CAPM says such an asset should earn close to (or even below) the risk-free rate, because it isn't being compensated for much (or any) market-related risk, even if its total volatility isn't small.

Worked example 2 — computing alpha

Same inputs as worked example 1 (CAPM expected return 9.8%), but the stock's actual realised return over the period was 11%. Alpha = 11% − 9.8% = +1.2 percentage points — it outperformed what its beta alone would predict (this says nothing about whether that outperformance will repeat, only that it happened over this period).

Worked example 3 — a negative-beta asset

Rf = 2%, Rm = 8%, β = −0.2 (an asset that tends to drift slightly against the market): E[R] = 2% + (−0.2)×(8% − 2%) = 2% − 1.2% = 0.8% — CAPM says this asset's "fair" expected return is actually below the risk-free rate, because its main value to a portfolio is as a hedge, not as a return engine.

\alpha = R_{\text{actual}} - \big(R_f+\beta\,(R_m-R_f)\big)
αalpha — realised return left unexplained by market beta
R_actualthe asset's actually realised return over the period
Try this
  1. Set beta to 1 in the calculator: CAPM gives exactly the market return — no more, no less.
  2. In the SML plot, compare where a beta-1.3 asset's point sits versus the line itself if you mentally mark its actual 11% return — that gap above the line is positive alpha.
Common traps
  • CAPM uses one factor (market beta) — it's a simplification, not a complete description of risk (the next lesson, factor investing, adds more).
  • Treating one period's positive alpha as proof of skill — a single lucky period and genuine, repeatable skill look identical in a short sample.
  • Assuming a low- or negative-beta asset is "safe" in the everyday sense — it can still be individually volatile; what's low is specifically its co-movement with the broad market.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Beta is reported on virtually every stock's profile on financial data platforms.
  • "Alpha" as the term actively managed funds use to justify their fees — claiming returns beyond what market exposure (beta) alone would explain.
  • Market-neutral and long/short hedge fund strategies that explicitly try to hold portfolio beta near zero while still seeking positive alpha.

Check yourself

1. What does a beta of 1.3 mean?
The asset tends to move about 30% more than the overall market, in the same direction.
2. Rf=3%, Rm=9%, beta=0.8. CAPM expected return? If the asset actually returned 9%, what's its alpha?
CAPM: 3 + 0.8×6 = 7.8%. Alpha = 9% − 7.8% = +1.2 percentage points.
3. Why might CAPM assign a very low, or even sub-risk-free, expected return to an asset with a negative beta?
Because CAPM only compensates for market-related risk; an asset that moves against the market provides a hedging benefit rather than bearing that risk, so it isn't rewarded with a risk premium for it — even though it may still be volatile on its own.

Takeaways

  • Beta measures sensitivity to the overall market; CAPM estimates a fair expected return from beta alone — a one-factor simplification.
  • The Security Market Line plots that relationship; alpha is the gap between an asset's actual return and what the line predicts for its beta.
  • A near-zero or negative beta reflects low or negative market co-movement, not necessarily low volatility on its own.

Factor investing, briefly

The questionCAPM uses one factor (the market). What if more than one shared characteristic helps explain returns across many stocks — and does adding more factors always make the estimate better?

Intuition

Factor investing extends CAPM's single market factor to several shared characteristics, each with its own exposure coefficient (β) and its own historical premium. Commonly cited factors include:

  • Value — cheaper stocks (relative to earnings or book value) have historically outperformed more expensive ones, on average and over long periods.
  • Size — smaller companies have historically shown a return premium over larger ones, plausibly as compensation for extra risk and lower liquidity.
  • Momentum and quality — stocks that have recently outperformed, or that show stronger profitability/balance-sheet characteristics, have also shown historical premia in various studies.

A stock's expected return becomes a combination of several factor exposures plus the risk-free rate, not just a single market exposure.

Worked example 1 — a simplified two-factor estimate

E[R] = 2% + 1.1×(market premium 6%) + 0.5×(value premium 3%) = 2 + 6.6 + 1.5 = 10.1%.

E[R]=R_f+\sum_k \beta_k\,(\text{factor premium}_k)

Build it up: the Fama-French three-factor model

The best-known academic extension of this idea, developed by Eugene Fama and Kenneth French, adds a size factor (SMB, "small minus big") alongside the market and value factors, producing a three-factor model. Each added factor is meant to capture a source of historical return variation that market beta alone leaves unexplained — but each added factor is also one more estimate that can be wrong, which is exactly this lesson's trap.

Worked example 2 — adding a size factor

Same stock as worked example 1, now also with a small-cap tilt: β(size) = 0.4, size premium = 2.5%. Three-factor estimate: E[R] = 2% + 1.1×6% + 0.4×2.5% + 0.5×3% = 2 + 6.6 + 1.0 + 1.5 = 11.1% — one percentage point higher than the two-factor estimate, entirely from adding the size exposure.

Worked example 3 — factor exposures can also be negative

A large, "expensive-relative-to-earnings" growth stock might instead have β(market)=1.0, β(size)=−0.2 (large-cap tilt), β(value)=−0.3 (growth tilt), with the same premia as above (6%, 2.5%, 3%): E[R] = 2% + 1.0×6% + (−0.2)×2.5% + (−0.3)×3% = 2 + 6 − 0.5 − 0.9 = 6.6% — lower than the plain market-only estimate (8%), because this stock's size and value tilts run against those historical premia.

E[R]=R_f+\beta_{\text{mkt}}(\text{mkt premium})+\beta_{\text{size}}(\text{SMB})+\beta_{\text{value}}(\text{HML})
SMB"small minus big" — the size factor's historical premium
HML"high minus low" (book-to-market) — the value factor's historical premium
β_size, β_valuethe stock's exposure to the size and value factors; can be negative (e.g. a large, growth-tilted stock)
Try this

  2. In the three-factor calculator, set the size exposure negative: the estimate falls relative to the two-factor case, illustrating worked example 3's large-cap, growth-tilted stock.
Common traps
  • More factors can fit historical data better while still not reliably predicting the future — a close cousin of overfitting (Quant Investing).
  • Assuming factor premia are guaranteed or constant — the value premium, for instance, has gone through long multi-year stretches of underperforming the market factor.
  • Confusing a factor exposure with a recommendation — "this stock has a positive value tilt" describes a statistical characteristic, not advice to buy it.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • "Smart beta" and factor ETFs built around specific, rules-based factor exposures (value, size, momentum, quality, low-volatility).
  • Academic factor-model research (Fama-French and its many extensions) that underpins how many quantitative funds are built (Quant Investing path).
  • Fund fact sheets that report a fund's factor "tilts" alongside its raw historical return.

Check yourself

1. How does factor investing extend CAPM?
By adding more than one shared-characteristic factor (such as size or value), each with its own exposure coefficient, instead of relying on market beta alone.
2. Rf=2%, β(market)=1.0, market premium=6%, β(size)=0.3, size premium=2%, β(value)=0.2, value premium=4%. Three-factor expected return?
2 + 1.0×6 + 0.3×2 + 0.2×4 = 2 + 6 + 0.6 + 0.8 = 9.4%.
3. Can a stock have a negative factor exposure, and what does that mean?
Yes — for example, a large, growth-tilted stock typically has negative size and value exposures, which subtract from (rather than add to) its factor-based expected return.

Takeaways

  • Factor models generalise CAPM to multiple shared characteristics, each with its own exposure and historical premium.
  • More factors isn't automatically better — each one is an added estimate that can be wrong or unstable over time.
  • Factor exposures can be negative, and describe a statistical tilt, never a buy or sell recommendation.

Rebalancing policies in depth

The questionShould you rebalance every month, every year, or only once drift crosses some threshold — and does a tighter threshold actually make the portfolio better off, or just busier?

Intuition

Building on Investment Fundamentals Lesson 4.2: as asset classes grow at different rates, a portfolio's actual weights drift away from its target weights. A calendar policy rebalances back to target on a fixed schedule (say, every January) regardless of how much drift has built up. A threshold policy instead rebalances only once drift exceeds a set amount, however long or short that takes. Both exist to answer the same trade-off: more frequent rebalancing controls drift more tightly, but costs more — in trading costs, bid-ask spreads, and, in a taxable account, potentially realised capital-gains taxes from selling the overweight winner.

Worked example 1 — does this drift trigger a rebalance?

Target stock weight 60%, current weight has drifted to 68% (stocks outperformed bonds this year). Drift = |68 − 60| = 8 percentage points. Against a 5pp threshold, 8 > 5, so this triggers a rebalance (sell stocks, buy bonds, back to 60/40). Against a looser 10pp threshold, 8 < 10, so the same portfolio would be left alone.

\text{trigger}: |\text{current weight}-\text{target weight}|>\text{threshold}

Build it up: putting a number on the cost side of the trade-off

A tighter threshold isn't free — each triggered rebalance has a real transaction cost. A rough illustrative model: suppose each full rebalancing trade costs about 0.1% of portfolio value (spreads and fees combined), and a tight 2% threshold happens to trigger about 6 times a year for a given portfolio's typical drift pattern, while a looser 5% threshold triggers only about 2 times a year for that same portfolio.

Worked example 2 — annual cost of tight vs. loose thresholds

Tight (2% threshold, ~6 triggers/year): 6 × 0.1% = 0.6% of portfolio value spent on rebalancing trades per year. Loose (5% threshold, ~2 triggers/year): 2 × 0.1% = 0.2% per year — three times less. The looser policy accepts more drift from target in exchange for a lower, steadier trading-cost drag; whether that trade is worth it depends on how much the extra drift itself is estimated to cost in expected return given up.

drift|current weight − target weight|, in percentage points
thresholdthe drift level, in percentage points, that triggers a rebalance
trades/yearhow often, on average, a given threshold is crossed for a given portfolio's typical drift

Common traps
  • Very tight thresholds can generate excessive trading costs relative to the drift they correct, as worked example 2 shows directly.
  • Ignoring taxes in a taxable account — rebalancing by selling the overweight (often the winning) asset can trigger capital-gains tax, a cost that doesn't exist inside a tax-advantaged retirement account.
  • Rebalancing purely mechanically without considering new contributions — directing new cash toward the underweight asset class can reduce or avoid the need to sell anything at all.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Target-date funds' internal rebalancing schedules, which commonly combine both calendar and threshold elements.
  • Robo-advisors that monitor account drift continuously and rebalance automatically once a stated threshold (often a few percentage points) is crossed.
  • "Rebalancing bonus" discussions in portfolio theory, where systematic rebalancing between imperfectly correlated assets can modestly add to long-run return, not just control risk.

Check yourself

1. What's the difference between calendar and threshold rebalancing?
Calendar rebalances on a fixed schedule regardless of drift size; threshold rebalances only once drift exceeds a set amount, whenever that happens.
2. Current weight 73%, target 60%, threshold 10pp. Does it trigger, and what's the drift?
Drift = |73 − 60| = 13pp > 10pp threshold, so yes, it triggers.
3. If a 2% threshold triggers 6 rebalances a year at 0.1% cost each, and a 5% threshold triggers only 2, what's the annual cost difference, and what's the underlying trade-off?
0.6% a year vs. 0.2% a year — a 0.4 percentage-point difference. The trade-off is tighter drift control (and closer tracking of the target allocation) against higher trading costs and, in taxable accounts, more realised gains.

Takeaways

  • Rebalancing policy trades drift control against trading cost (and, in taxable accounts, tax cost).
  • Calendar and threshold approaches can be combined, as many target-date funds do.
  • Directing new contributions toward underweight assets can reduce how often an outright sale-based rebalance is even needed.
