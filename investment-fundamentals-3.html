3.1

Reading a balance sheet

The questionA company reports $500,000 in assets and $300,000 in liabilities. Is that good or bad?

Intuition

A balance sheet is a snapshot at one moment: what a company owns (assets), what it owes (liabilities), and what's left over for owners (equity). Solvency asks whether a company can meet its long-term obligations; liquidity asks whether it can meet its near-term ones, often checked with the current ratio (current assets ÷ current liabilities).

Worked example

Assets $500,000, liabilities $300,000 → equity = $200,000. Debt-to-equity = 300,000/200,000 = 1.5. Separately, current assets of $120,000 against current liabilities of $80,000 gives a current ratio of 120,000/80,000 = 1.5 — comfortably above 1, meaning short-term obligations are well covered.

The formal definition

\text{Equity}=\text{Assets}-\text{Liabilities}\qquad \text{D/E}=\frac{\text{Liabilities}}{\text{Equity}}\qquad \text{Current ratio}=\frac{\text{Current assets}}{\text{Current liabilities}}
Assetseverything the company owns of value
Liabilitieseverything the company owes
Equitythe residual belonging to owners
Try this
  1. Raise liabilities while equity stays fixed: debt-to-equity climbs.
Common traps
  • More debt isn't automatically bad — it can fund productive growth; context (industry, rates) matters.
  • A very high current ratio can also signal cash sitting idle rather than financial strength.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Debt-to-equity is commonly compared across companies within the same industry.
  • "Working capital" discussions reference the current ratio.

Check yourself

1. What does equity represent?
What's left for owners after subtracting liabilities from assets.
2. Assets $800,000, liabilities $600,000. Debt-to-equity?
Equity = 200,000; D/E = 600,000/200,000 = 3.0.
3. Why might comparing D/E across different industries be misleading?
Typical, healthy debt levels vary a lot by industry (e.g. capital-intensive utilities vs. asset-light software firms), so an industry-agnostic comparison can mislead.

Takeaways

  • A balance sheet is a snapshot of assets, liabilities and equity.
  • Debt-to-equity and the current ratio summarise solvency and liquidity respectively.
  • Ratios should be judged in industry context, not in isolation.
3.2

Reading an income statement

The questionTwo companies both report $1,000,000 in revenue. How do you tell which one is actually running a tighter, more profitable business?

Intuition

An income statement runs from revenue (the top line) through costs to net income (the bottom line). Gross margin (gross profit ÷ revenue) shows how much is left after the direct cost of making the product; net margin (net income ÷ revenue) shows what's left after everything. Margins — percentages — let you compare profitability across companies of very different sizes; raw dollar profits do not.

Worked example

Revenue $1,000,000, cost of goods sold $600,000 → gross profit $400,000, gross margin = 400,000/1,000,000 = 40%. If net income is $80,000, net margin = 80,000/1,000,000 = 8% — operating expenses and other costs consumed the other 32 percentage points.

The formal definition

\text{Gross margin}=\frac{\text{Revenue}-\text{COGS}}{\text{Revenue}}\qquad \text{Net margin}=\frac{\text{Net income}}{\text{Revenue}}
COGScost of goods sold — the direct cost of producing what was sold
Net incomewhat's left after all expenses, interest and taxes
Try this
  1. Raise COGS toward revenue: gross margin shrinks toward 0%.
Common traps
  • Comparing raw net income dollars across very differently-sized companies says little — compare margins instead.
  • A high gross margin can still end in a low net margin if operating expenses are large.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Earnings reports regularly discuss "margin expansion" or "margin compression" from one period to the next.

Check yourself

1. Why compare margins instead of raw profit dollars?
Margins are percentages of revenue, which makes profitability comparable across companies of different sizes.
2. Revenue $2,000,000, COGS $1,200,000. Gross margin?
(2,000,000 − 1,200,000)/2,000,000 = 40%.
3. Can a company have a high gross margin but a low net margin?
Yes — large operating expenses, interest or taxes below the gross-profit line can erode a high gross margin down to a much lower net margin.

Takeaways

  • Income statements run from revenue down to net income.
  • Gross and net margin are percentage measures that allow fair comparison across company sizes.
  • A good gross margin doesn't guarantee a good net margin.
3.3

P/E and other multiples

The questionA stock trades at $60 with $4 of earnings per share — "P/E of 15". What does that number actually mean, and is 15 good?

Intuition

A multiple expresses price relative to some fundamental figure. The price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio says how much investors are paying today per dollar of annual earnings; the price-to-book (P/B) ratio compares price to the company's accounting net worth per share. A multiple is only meaningful in comparison — to the company's own history, to peers in the same industry, or to its expected growth.

Worked example

Price $60, EPS $4 → P/E = 60/4 = 15, meaning investors pay $15 for every $1 of current annual earnings. If book value per share is $30, P/B = 60/30 = 2.0.

The formal definition

P/E=\frac{\text{Price}}{\text{EPS}}\qquad\qquad P/B=\frac{\text{Price}}{\text{Book value per share}}
EPSearnings per share — net income divided by shares outstanding
Book value per shareequity (Lesson 3.1) divided by shares outstanding
Try this
  1. Raise EPS with price fixed: P/E falls — the same price now buys more earnings.
Common traps
  • A "low" P/E can mean cheap, or it can mean the market expects earnings to fall — context matters.
  • Comparing P/E across very different industries without adjusting for typical growth and risk is misleading.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • P/E is commonly compared across companies in the same sector.
  • P/B is especially common for valuing financial companies like banks.

Check yourself

1. What does a P/E of 20 mean, in plain words?
Investors are paying $20 today for every $1 of the company's current annual earnings.
2. Price $45, EPS $3. P/E?
45/3 = 15.
3. Why isn't a low P/E automatically a "buy signal"?
It can reflect the market genuinely expecting earnings to decline, not just an overlooked bargain.

Takeaways

  • A multiple expresses price relative to a fundamental like earnings or book value.
  • P/E and P/B are only meaningful compared to history, peers or growth expectations.
  • A low multiple is not automatically "cheap" in a good way.

Intro to discounted cash flow

The questionA project is expected to hand you $100,000 in 3 years. Worth $75,131 or $100,000 today — and why does the rate you pick change the answer so much?

Intuition

Discounted cash flow (DCF) extends Lesson 0.3's present-value idea from one future payment to a whole stream of projected cash flows: discount each one back to today and add them up. The arithmetic is simple; what actually drives the answer is the assumed discount rate and growth of future cash flows — small changes there can swing the result enormously.

Worked example

A single $100,000 cash flow in 3 years, discounted at 10%: PV = 100,000/1.1³ ≈ $75,131. A full DCF just repeats this for each year's projected cash flow and sums the results.

The formal definition

PV=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^{t}}
CFₜthe projected cash flow in year t
rthe discount rate
nnumber of years projected
Try this
  1. Raise the discount rate: present value falls — often sharply for cash flows further out.
  2. Push the horizon (x-axis, the discount rate itself here) higher and watch how quickly PV decays even for a single, fixed future cash flow.
Common traps
  • Small changes in the assumed discount or growth rate can swing a DCF valuation enormously — "garbage in, garbage out".
  • Projecting cash flows many years into the future is inherently uncertain, even with careful work.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • DCF is a standard tool in equity research and company valuation.
  • The same present-value math underlies bond pricing (Lesson 2.2) and real-estate valuation.

Check yourself

1. What does a DCF do that single-period present value (Lesson 0.3) doesn't?
It discounts and sums a whole stream of projected future cash flows, not just one.
2. PV of $50,000 in 2 years at 8%?
50,000/1.08² ≈ $42,867.
3. Why is a DCF described as sensitive to assumptions?
Because the discount rate and projected growth are estimates, and the formula raises them to a power over many years — small errors compound into large valuation differences.

Takeaways

  • DCF sums the present value of a projected stream of cash flows.
  • The assumptions (rate, growth) dominate the result more than the arithmetic.
  • The same discounting idea underlies bonds and real estate too.
3.5

Intro to chart reading

The questionA stock moves from $40 to $55 over 10 weeks. Is that an "uptrend", and what does a chart actually show to make that obvious?

Intuition

A candlestick shows a period's open, high, low and close at a glance. A trend is a series of higher highs and higher lows (up) or lower highs and lower lows (down); a simple trend line connects them. Support is a price level buyers have repeatedly stepped in at; resistance is a level sellers have repeatedly capped the price at. (The dedicated Technical Analysis path goes much deeper into indicators and the evidence for and against this kind of analysis.)

Worked example

From $40 to $55 over 10 weeks: slope = (55 − 40)/10 = $1.50/week, a positive slope — a simple, quantified way to describe an uptrend.

The formal definition

\text{slope}=\frac{P_1-P_0}{\text{weeks}}
P₀, P₁price at the start and end of the window
slopepositive = uptrend, negative = downtrend, by this simple measure
Try this
  1. Make the slope a (parameter a) negative: the line becomes a downtrend.
  2. Set a to 0: a flat, sideways trend — neither up nor down.
Common traps
  • A trend line fit to a short window can look convincing and reverse immediately afterward.
  • Support and resistance are observed patterns from past behaviour, not guaranteed floors or ceilings.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Candlestick charts appear on virtually every trading platform.
  • Support and resistance are referenced constantly in technical-analysis discussions.

Check yourself

1. What does a candlestick show for one period?
The open, high, low and close prices for that period.
2. A stock falls from $90 to $72 over 6 weeks. Slope?
(72 − 90)/6 = −$3/week — a downtrend.
3. Is support a guaranteed floor for the price?
No — it's a level where buyers have stepped in before; it can still be broken.

Takeaways

  • Candlesticks summarise a period's price action at a glance.
  • A trend can be roughly quantified as a slope.
  • Support and resistance are observed patterns, not guarantees.
