Introduction

The questionYou have $1,000. You could leave it in a drawer, put it in a savings account that barely keeps pace with inflation, or invest it in a diversified fund. Thirty years from now, does it really matter which you chose — and does it matter whether the money is left completely alone, versus touched along the way?

Intuition

Investing means giving up the use of money now so that it can work for you — by lending it (bonds), owning a piece of a business (stocks), or owning a productive asset (property) — in exchange for a claim on more money later. It is worth separating three words that get used loosely:

  • Saving — parking money somewhere very safe and liquid (a bank account, cash under a mattress), prioritizing getting it back intact over growing it.
  • Investing — accepting some uncertainty in exchange for a claim on a real future cash flow (a company's profits, a bond's interest, a property's rent) and the prospect of growth.
  • Speculating — betting on short-term price movement itself, often with little regard for any underlying cash flow, and sometimes with borrowed money that magnifies both gains and losses.

The engine behind "more money later" is compounding: each period's growth earns its own growth the next period, like a snowball that picks up more snow as it rolls. Money in a drawer earns nothing at all; investing lets time do part of the work for you.

Worked example 1 — simple vs. compound

$1,000 growing at 7% a year: with simple growth (interest paid out, not reinvested) you'd have 1000 + 1000×0.07×30 = $3,100 after 30 years. With compound growth (each year's gain stays invested) you'd have 1000×1.07³⁰ ≈ $7,612 — more than double, purely from letting gains generate their own gains.

Build it up: how often compounding happens

So far, "compound annually" meant growth is credited once a year. Real accounts often compound more often than that — monthly on a savings account, daily on some loans. Compounding more frequently at the same stated (nominal) annual rate always produces a slightly higher result, because gains start earning their own gains sooner, in smaller steps, throughout the year. The single annual rate that is truly equivalent to a given compounding frequency — the rate you'd need if compounding only once a year to match it exactly — is the effective annual rate (EAR).

Worked example 2 — monthly compounding

$1,000 at a nominal 8% a year for 20 years. Compounded annually: 1000×1.08²⁰ ≈ $4,660.96. Compounded monthly (the 8% is split into twelve 0.667% steps): 1000×(1+0.08/12)²⁴⁰ ≈ $4,926.80 — $265.84 more, from the same headline rate, purely because gains start compounding sooner. The equivalent effective annual rate is (1+0.08/12)¹² − 1 ≈ 8.30%, slightly above the 8% nominal figure quoted.

The formal definition

\text{Simple: } FV=P(1+rt) \qquad \text{Compound (annual): } FV=P(1+r)^{t} \qquad \text{Compound (m times/yr): } FV=P\left(1+\frac{r}{m}\right)^{mt}
EAR=\left(1+\frac{r}{m}\right)^{m}-1
Pprincipal — the amount invested today
rthe stated (nominal) annual growth rate, as a decimal (7% = 0.07)
ttime in years
mhow many times per year the rate compounds (12 = monthly, 365 = daily, 1 = annually)
FVfuture value — what the principal grows into
EAReffective annual rate — the single annual rate equivalent to compounding r at frequency m

Common traps
  • Investing is not the same as speculating or gambling: investing buys a claim on real future cash flows; speculating bets on price movement alone, often with little regard for any underlying cash flow.
  • No real-world rate is guaranteed or perfectly steady every year — the formulas above describe the destination, not the bumpy path to get there (Lesson 0.5 covers that bumpiness).
  • A "nominal rate" and its "effective annual rate" look like the same number but are not equal once compounding happens more than once a year — always check which one a product is quoting.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Broad stock market indices have historically compounded over long periods, though any specific future rate is never guaranteed.
  • Tax-advantaged retirement accounts let gains compound without yearly tax drag, which is part of why "contribute early" is repeated so often.
  • Credit cards and many loans advertise a nominal APR that compounds monthly or daily — the EAR actually paid can run noticeably above the headline number.

Check yourself

1. Which grows faster over many years at the same stated rate: simple growth, or growth compounded annually?
Compound growth, because each year's gain is reinvested and itself earns a return, while simple growth only ever earns a return on the original principal.
2. A hypothetical "investment" promises a fixed 40%-per-month payout with no underlying business or asset behind it. Investing, saving, or something else entirely?
Something else — with no real cash flow or productive asset behind the payout, this pattern looks like neither investing nor saving; it is a classic warning sign of a scheme rather than a return on a real asset.
3. A card quotes 24% APR, compounded monthly. Is the effective annual rate higher or lower than 24%, and roughly by how much?
Higher: (1+0.24/12)¹² − 1 ≈ 26.8%, about 2.8 percentage points above the quoted nominal rate.

Takeaways

  • Investing trades the use of money now for a claim on a real future cash flow; it differs from both saving (safety first) and speculating (price bets alone).
  • Compounding is growth on growth — it rewards both a higher rate and more time, and rewards more frequent compounding too, though with rapidly diminishing extra benefit.
  • A nominal rate and its effective annual rate (EAR) are not the same number once compounding is more frequent than yearly.

Asset classes overview

The questionYou want to invest $5,000 but "the stock market" is only one option. What else is actually out there — and why do two investments that both "went up 8% this year" sometimes feel like completely different experiences?

Intuition

An asset class is a group of investments that behave similarly because they represent the same basic kind of claim:

  • Lend money and you hold a bond — fixed interest along the way, principal back at the end.
  • Own a slice of a business and you hold an equity (a stock) — no fixed payment, but a share of whatever profit (or loss) the business makes.
  • Own a building and you hold real estate — rent as income, the property's value as price change.
  • Own a physical, interchangeable good like oil or gold and you hold a commodity — usually no income at all, only price change.
  • Hold a digital, decentralised asset and you hold a cryptocurrency — again typically no income, and historically very large price swings.
  • Hold money itself (or something that behaves like it, e.g. a money-market fund) and you hold cash & equivalents — minimal price change, modest interest income.

Whatever you hold, its total return splits into two pieces: the income it pays out along the way (interest, dividends, rent) and the price change of the asset itself.

Build it up: how the two pieces split differently by class

The income/price split is not just an accounting detail — it is characteristic of each asset class, and it is why a bond and a growth stock can post the same headline return yet feel completely different to hold. A bond's return usually comes almost entirely from its coupon (income); the price barely moves unless interest rates change (Economics Lesson 0.3). A non-dividend-paying growth stock's return, by contrast, usually comes almost entirely from price change — there is no income cushion if the price falls.

Worked example 1 — total return, one period

You bought a REIT-like holding at $1,000, it paid $40 in distributions, and its price ended the year unchanged at $1,000: R = (40 + 0) / 1000 = 4%, all from income.

Worked example 2 — the same headline return, opposite anatomy

A growth stock bought at $100, paying no dividend, ends the year at $112: R = (0 + 12) / 100 = 12%, entirely from price change. A second, income-style holding earning its return almost entirely from a steady 4% payout with a flat price has a far smoother ride to the same kind of total-return number than a holding whose entire return depends on the market re-pricing it higher — even when the headline percentages look comparable in a good year.

The formal definition

R=\frac{D+(P_1-P_0)}{P_0}
P₀, P₁price at the start and end of the period
Dincome received during the period (dividend, coupon, rent)
Rtotal return over the period, as a decimal

As a rough, historical ordering, cash is the lowest risk and lowest expected return; bonds sit above it; diversified equities above that; a single stock or crypto sit highest in typical risk. Lesson 0.5 makes "risk" precise. A third dimension worth naming here is liquidity — how quickly and cheaply an asset can be converted to cash without a forced price concession. Cash is maximally liquid; a diversified stock or bond fund is highly liquid (sellable in seconds at a visible price, Lesson 0.8); direct real estate is comparatively illiquid (a sale can take months and depends on finding a willing buyer).

Common traps
  • "The stock market" and "investing" are not synonyms — equities are one asset class among several.
  • Holding only cash feels safe but historically has often lost purchasing power to inflation over long periods (Lesson 2.3 in the Asset Classes path covers this in depth).
  • Treating liquidity as the same thing as risk — an asset can be low-risk but illiquid (a bank CD with an early-withdrawal penalty) or high-risk but highly liquid (a volatile stock that trades constantly).
Where it shows up in real investing
  • The S&P 500 and similar broad indices represent the equities asset class; a 10-year government bond represents fixed income; a money-market fund represents cash equivalents.
  • A diversified portfolio usually blends several of these classes rather than holding just one (Lesson 0.7).
  • Publicly-traded REITs are often held specifically for their income share of total return, much like the bond-like case above, while remaining exposed to real estate price cycles.

Check yourself

1. Name the two pieces that make up an asset's total return, and give one asset class whose return is usually almost entirely one of them.
Income (dividends, interest, rent) and price change; a bond's return is usually almost entirely income, while a non-dividend growth stock's or a commodity's is usually almost entirely price change.
2. A bond is a claim on what, exactly, and how does that differ from what an equity is a claim on?
A bond is a loan: the holder is owed fixed interest payments plus the return of principal. An equity is ownership: a share of whatever profit (or loss) the business makes, with no fixed payment guaranteed.
3. Why can an asset be low-risk yet illiquid, or high-risk yet highly liquid? Give one example of each.
Risk (how much value can swing) and liquidity (how quickly/cheaply it converts to cash) are different dimensions. A bank CD with an early-withdrawal penalty is low-risk but illiquid; a volatile, heavily-traded stock is high-risk but highly liquid.

Takeaways

  • Asset classes differ in what kind of claim you hold: lending, ownership, physical goods, or cash.
  • Total return = income + price change, and the split between the two is characteristic of the asset class, not incidental.
  • Risk, expected return and liquidity are three separate dimensions worth checking before assuming "safe" or "risky".

Time value of money

The questionA lottery advertises a "$1,000,000 jackpot" but offers you a smaller lump sum today instead of the full amount spread over 20 years. Why would the smaller number today ever be the fair choice — and how would you actually work out what that smaller number ought to be?

Intuition

A dollar today can be invested and start growing immediately; a dollar promised years from now cannot. So the two are not equal — the future dollar is "worth less" today by however much growth you gave up waiting for it. Turning a future amount into its equivalent value today is called discounting, and the rate used is the discount rate — essentially the return you could otherwise have earned elsewhere. Notice this is the exact mirror image of Lesson 0.1's compounding: compounding projects a present amount forward into the future; discounting pulls a future amount back to the present. Same relationship, opposite direction.

Worked example 1 — a single future payment

You're promised $1,000 in 5 years, and could otherwise earn 6% a year elsewhere. Its value today: 1000 / 1.06⁵ ≈ $747.26. That means you should be indifferent between $747.26 now and $1,000 in 5 years — assuming you could really earn 6%.

Build it up: discounting a whole stream of payments

The lottery question isn't really about one future payment — it's about twenty of them, one per year. An annuity is exactly that: a series of equal payments made at regular intervals. Its present value is simply the sum of discounting every individual payment back to today, each by however many years away it is. Doing that term by term is tedious, so there is a shortcut formula that adds up the whole geometric series at once.

Worked example 2 — present value of an annuity (the lottery)

Suppose the "$1,000,000" prize is actually paid as $50,000 a year for 20 years (50,000 × 20 = 1,000,000 — the advertised total), and the fair discount rate is 5%. Present value = 50000 × (1 − 1.05⁻²⁰) / 0.05 ≈ $623,110.52 — noticeably less than $1,000,000, simply because most of those dollars arrive many years from now. A lump-sum offer anywhere above $623,110.52 would actually be the better deal at a 5% discount rate, even though it is a smaller headline number than "$1,000,000".

The formal definition

PV=\frac{FV}{(1+r)^{t}}\qquad\qquad PV_{\text{annuity}}=C\times\frac{1-(1+r)^{-n}}{r}
FVfuture value — a single amount received later
rdiscount rate per period (the return given up by waiting)
tperiods until a single payment arrives
PVpresent value — today's equivalent of a future amount
Cthe fixed payment made each period of an annuity
nthe number of equal payments in the annuity
Try this
  1. Keep r fixed and slide the viewing years out: present value shrinks the farther away the payment is.
  2. Raise r: every future dollar becomes worth less today, because the opportunity you're giving up is bigger.
Try this
  1. Set C to 50,000, n to 20 and r to 5%: confirm the present value matches the worked example, ≈ $623,110.52.
  2. Raise r: the annuity's present value falls, just like a single payment's did — but notice it falls by more in dollar terms, since there are many payments being discounted, not just one.
Common traps
  • A higher discount rate makes the present value smaller, not bigger — it's easy to get this backwards.
  • Discounting and inflation are related but distinct: discounting also reflects risk and the opportunity cost of waiting, not just rising prices.
  • Adding up an annuity's raw payments ($50,000 × 20 = $1,000,000) and treating that as its "real" value ignores that every payment except the first one is worth less than its face amount today.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Bond and mortgage pricing are built entirely from discounting future payments (a bond's coupons are, structurally, an annuity) back to today.
  • Lottery "cash value" vs. "annuity value" options are a direct, real-world application of this exact lesson.
  • Comparing a lump-sum payout to an annuity recurs in Module 3's intro to discounted cash flow, applied to a company's projected cash flows instead of a lottery.

Check yourself

1. Does raising the discount rate increase or decrease present value, and does this apply to an annuity as well as a single payment?
Decrease, in both cases — a higher rate means more was given up by waiting, so every future amount (whether one payment or a whole stream) is worth less today.
2. Which is worth more today: $500 in 1 year or $500 in 10 years, at any positive discount rate?
$500 in 1 year — the sooner payment is discounted less.
3. A prize pays $20,000 a year for 10 years at an 6% discount rate. Is its present value closer to $200,000, $147,000, or $100,000?
20000 × (1 − 1.06⁻¹⁰)/0.06 ≈ $147,201.74 — closest to $147,000, well below the $200,000 sum of the raw payments.

Takeaways

  • Money available sooner is worth more than the same amount available later; discounting is compounding run in reverse.
  • A single future payment discounts with PV = FV/(1+r)ᵗ; a whole stream of equal payments (an annuity) discounts with PV = C×(1−(1+r)⁻ⁿ)/r.
  • A higher discount rate shrinks present value, and shrinks an annuity's present value by even more in dollar terms, since many payments are affected at once.

Measuring return

The questionFund A says it returned "34% total" over a few messy years. Fund B says it returned "a steady 10% a year" for 5 years. Which one actually grew your money more — and does the answer change once you account for inflation along the way?

Intuition

A simple return describes one period: how much you gained or lost relative to what you put in, including any income received. To compare investments held for different lengths of time fairly, you need a single annual rate that would reproduce the same overall result — the CAGR (compound annual growth rate). CAGR is exactly the future-value compounding formula from Lesson 0.1, solved backwards for the rate instead of the ending amount.

Worked example 1 — simple return and CAGR

You bought a share at $50, collected $2 in dividends, and sold at $65: simple return = (65 − 50 + 2) / 50 = 34% — but that's over however many years it took, not "per year".

Separately, $10,000 grew to $16,000 over 5 years. CAGR = (16000/10000)^(1/5) − 1 ≈ 9.86% per year — this is the single steady annual rate that gets you from $10,000 to $16,000 in exactly 5 years.

Build it up: CAGR in nominal terms can still mislead

A CAGR puts two investments on the same annual footing, but it is still a nominal number — it says nothing about how much purchasing power actually grew, which depends on inflation over the same stretch of time (Economics Lesson 0.2). The same Fisher relationship that converts a nominal return into a real one applies just as well to a CAGR as to a single year's return.

Worked example 2 — real CAGR

The 9.86% nominal CAGR above, with inflation running at 3% a year over the same 5 years: real CAGR = (1.0986 / 1.03) − 1 ≈ 6.66% per year — noticeably below both the 9.86% headline figure and the 6.86% a quick (and slightly wrong) "just subtract" shortcut would suggest.

The formal definition

R=\frac{P_1-P_0+D}{P_0}\qquad\qquad CAGR=\left(\frac{FV}{PV}\right)^{1/t}-1\qquad\qquad \text{real CAGR}=\frac{1+CAGR}{1+\text{inflation}}-1
Rsimple (holding-period) return
PV, FVbeginning and ending value
tnumber of years held
CAGRthe constant annual (nominal) rate that turns PV into FV over t years

  2. Keep the same PV and FV but raise years: CAGR falls — the same total growth spread over more time is a smaller annual rate.
Try this
  1. Enter the 9.86% CAGR from the worked example with 3% inflation and confirm real CAGR ≈ 6.66%.
  2. Set inflation equal to the CAGR: real CAGR falls to exactly 0% — all of that "growth" was just keeping up with rising prices, buying no extra purchasing power at all.
Common traps
  • Averaging yearly percentage returns with plain arithmetic overstates the true result — a +50% year followed by a −50% year is not "0% average", it's a 25% loss overall (1.5 × 0.5 = 0.75).
  • A "total return" headline figure is meaningless for comparison without also knowing the time period it covers.
  • Quoting a strong nominal CAGR without mentioning inflation can make a period of weak real growth look stronger than it was.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Fund fact sheets routinely quote "5-year annualized return" — that number is a CAGR, almost always in nominal terms unless stated otherwise.
  • Savings account and CD "APY" figures are the same annualising idea applied to interest.
  • Comparing a market's nominal long-run return across very different inflation eras (e.g. the 1970s vs. the 2010s) without adjusting for inflation is a common, misleading shortcut.

Check yourself

1. Why isn't "34% total return" directly comparable to "10% per year"?
They cover different (and in the first case, unstated) time periods; only an annualized rate like CAGR puts them on the same footing.
2. $5,000 grows to $9,000 over 6 years. What is the CAGR (to one decimal place)?
(9000/5000)^(1/6) − 1 ≈ 10.3%.
3. A fund posts a 7% nominal CAGR while inflation runs at 3% a year over the same period. What is the real CAGR, and is it close to the "quick" 4% subtraction?
(1.07/1.03) − 1 ≈ 3.88% — close to, but a bit below, the quick 7%−3%=4% approximation; the exact Fisher calculation divides rather than subtracts.

Takeaways

  • Simple return describes one period; CAGR annualizes a multi-year result for fair comparison, using the same compounding relationship as Lesson 0.1 solved in reverse.
  • CAGR = (FV/PV)^(1/t) − 1, and percentage gains and losses do not average arithmetically — compound them instead.
  • A nominal CAGR can still mislead; the Fisher equation converts it into a real CAGR that reflects actual purchasing-power growth.

Risk & volatility

The questionTwo funds both average 8% a year. One bounces between −20% and +35% each year; the other stays between +4% and +12%. Are they really "the same" investment — and does knowing the average and the spread tell you everything you'd want to know before investing?

Intuition

The average return tells you the destination; it says nothing about how bumpy the ride was. Volatility — measured as the standard deviation of returns — captures how far actual yearly returns typically stray from the average. A low-volatility investment's returns cluster tightly around its average; a high-volatility one swings widely.

Worked example 1 — mean and standard deviation

Five years of returns: 8%, −3%, 12%, 5%, −6%. Mean = (8−3+12+5−6)/5 = 3.2%. Each deviation from the mean, squared, averaged, then square-rooted gives the standard deviation: ≈ 6.73%.

Build it up: risk that only looks at the downside

Standard deviation has a known blind spot, already flagged in the curriculum for this lesson: it punishes a surprise gain exactly as much as a surprise loss of the same size, even though most investors only really dread the loss. Two related measures look specifically at the downside: downside deviation (like σ, but only counting returns below some target) and maximum drawdown — the largest peak-to-trough decline in cumulative value over the whole period, regardless of how many ups and downs happened in between.

Worked example 2 — maximum drawdown

Using the same five yearly returns on a $100 starting value: the running value is 100 → 108.00 → 104.76 → 117.33 → 123.20 → 115.81. The running peak before the final dip is $123.20 (reached after year 4); the value then falls to $115.81. Drawdown = (123.20 − 115.81) / 123.20 ≈ 6.00% — the worst peak-to-trough loss an investor actually lived through, which is a different number from σ ≈ 6.73% and answers a different question ("how bad did it get" vs. "how spread out were the yearly numbers").

The formal definition

\bar r=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_i \qquad\qquad \sigma=\sqrt{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}(r_i-\bar r)^2}
\text{Max drawdown}=\max_{t}\left(\frac{\text{Peak}_{\le t}-V_t}{\text{Peak}_{\le t}}\right)
rᵢthe return in year i
nnumber of years in the sample
r̄the mean (average) return
σstandard deviation of returns — the main risk measure used throughout this path
Vₜcumulative portfolio value at time t
Peak≤ₜthe highest value reached at or before time t

Common traps
  • Standard deviation treats a surprise gain and a surprise loss of the same size identically — it does not by itself distinguish "good" volatility from "bad".
  • A short history (a few years) gives a noisy, unreliable σ estimate.
  • Comparing σ figures computed over different-length histories or different return frequencies (monthly vs. annual) without adjusting for that difference.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • ETF and mutual fund fact sheets commonly report a "standard deviation" or "volatility" figure.
  • The VIX index is often described as the market's expectation of near-term volatility.
  • Fund marketing material and news coverage of a downturn often cite "maximum drawdown" (e.g. a broad index's largest historical peak-to-trough decline) alongside or instead of σ.

Check yourself

1. Can two investments have the same average return but different risk? Can they also have the same σ but different maximum drawdown?
Yes to both — average return says nothing about spread, and σ (which looks at every period's deviation) can be similar for two return sequences whose worst peak-to-trough decline differs a lot, depending on exactly when the losses land.
2. What does a standard deviation of 0 mean?
Every return in the sample was identical to the mean — no variation at all.
3. Why does standard deviation treat a great year and a terrible year of the same size as "equally risky", and what measure specifically addresses that?
σ squares every deviation from the mean regardless of sign, so a +20% surprise and a −20% surprise contribute identically; downside deviation and maximum drawdown instead focus only on declines, which is usually closer to what an investor actually worries about.

Takeaways

  • Average return and risk are different questions — always ask both.
  • Standard deviation σ measures how much returns typically deviate from their average, treating gains and losses symmetrically.
  • Downside deviation and maximum drawdown look only at declines, and often tell a more relevant (and different) risk story than σ alone.

Risk-adjusted return

The questionFund X returned 9%/year with 15% volatility. Fund Y returned a lower 7%/year but with only 6% volatility. The risk-free rate (e.g. short-term government bills) is 2%. Which fund did the better job — and would the answer change if Fund X's volatility mostly came from large upside surprises rather than losses?

Intuition

Raw return alone can't answer "which investment was better managed", because a bigger number is easy to get by simply taking more risk. The Sharpe ratio measures reward per unit of risk taken — like miles per gallon instead of just miles.

Worked example 1 — comparing two funds

Fund X: (0.09 − 0.02) / 0.15 ≈ 0.467. Fund Y: (0.07 − 0.02) / 0.06 ≈ 0.833. Despite the lower raw return, Fund Y earned nearly double the reward per unit of risk.

Build it up: what if the volatility is mostly good news?

The Sharpe ratio uses σ, which (Lesson 0.5) treats a big upside surprise exactly like a big downside one. The Sortino ratio fixes that by replacing σ with downside deviation — a standard-deviation-style calculation that only counts returns falling below a chosen target (often 0%, or the risk-free rate). A fund whose "volatility" comes mostly from occasional big up-years, rather than down-years, looks worse than it deserves under the Sharpe ratio and better once Sortino is used instead.

Worked example 2 — Sharpe vs. Sortino

A fund's five yearly returns: 12%, −8%, 15%, 10%, −5%. Mean = 4.8%. Standard deviation (counting every year) ≈ 9.41%. Downside deviation (counting only the two negative years, each measured against a 0% target) ≈ 4.22% — much smaller, because three of the five years were large gains that σ penalises but downside deviation ignores. With a 2% risk-free rate: Sharpe = (0.048 − 0.02)/0.0941 ≈ 0.30, Sortino = (0.048 − 0.02)/0.0422 ≈ 0.66 — more than double. The fund looks meaningfully better once only its down-years count against it.

The formal definition

S=\frac{R_p-R_f}{\sigma_p}\qquad\qquad \text{Sortino}=\frac{R_p-R_f}{\sigma_{\text{downside}}}
Rpthe portfolio's (or fund's) return
Rfthe risk-free rate — the return of a very low-risk benchmark
σpthe portfolio's standard deviation (Lesson 0.5)
σdownsidedownside deviation — like σ, but only returns below the target count
Sthe Sharpe ratio — higher means more reward per unit of total risk

Common traps
  • The Sharpe ratio penalises all volatility, including sharp upside moves, which is not always what an investor actually minds.
  • Comparing Sharpe ratios computed over different, non-overlapping time periods can be misleading.
  • Comparing one fund's Sharpe ratio to another fund's Sortino ratio as if they were the same measure — they use different denominators and are not directly comparable.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Fund comparison tools and manager performance reviews routinely list Sharpe ratio alongside raw return.
  • Short-term government Treasury bills are a common stand-in for the risk-free rate.
  • Sortino ratio appears alongside Sharpe ratio in many fund fact sheets, especially for strategies (e.g. some option-selling or trend-following styles) whose return pattern is notably asymmetric.

Check yourself

1. A fund with a higher return always has a higher Sharpe ratio. True or false?
False — a high enough volatility can make the Sharpe ratio lower despite a higher raw return.
2. Compute the Sharpe ratio for return 11%, risk-free rate 3%, σ 20%.
(0.11 − 0.03) / 0.20 = 0.40.
3. Two funds have identical Sharpe ratios. Could their Sortino ratios still differ, and why?
Yes — Sharpe only sees total σ, not how that risk splits between upside and downside surprises; a fund whose volatility is mostly big up-years will have a much higher Sortino ratio than one whose equal total σ comes mostly from down-years, even with the same Sharpe ratio.

Takeaways

  • Raw return alone doesn't tell you if the risk taken was worth it.
  • Sharpe ratio = excess return over the risk-free rate, divided by total volatility (σ).
  • Sortino ratio swaps σ for downside deviation, rewarding funds whose volatility skews toward gains rather than losses.

Diversification & correlation

The questionYou hold one stock with 20% volatility. A friend says "add a second stock, also 20% volatile, and your overall risk will actually go down." How can adding a risky asset reduce risk — and does that trick keep working forever if you just keep adding more stocks?

Intuition

If two assets don't move in perfect lockstep, their bad days don't always coincide — one asset's dip is partly offset by the other holding up. How closely two assets move together is their correlation (from −1, perfectly opposite, to +1, perfectly together). The less correlated two assets are, the more their combination's ups and downs cancel out, even though each individually is just as volatile as before.

Worked example 1 — two assets

Two assets, each with σ = 20%, correlation ρ = 0.3, blended 50/50: portfolio variance = 0.5²×0.2² + 0.5²×0.2² + 2×0.5×0.5×0.3×0.2² = 0.026, so portfolio σ = √0.026 ≈ 16.1% — lower than either asset alone, simply from combining them.

Build it up: what happens with many assets, not just two?

Does adding a third, fourth, fifth similar asset keep helping as much as the first one did? With n equally-weighted assets that each have the same σ and the same pairwise correlation ρ to every other asset, portfolio variance splits cleanly into two pieces: a part that shrinks as n grows (unsystematic or diversifiable risk — the part specific to each individual holding) and a part that does not shrink no matter how many assets you add (systematic risk — the part all the assets share, driven by ρ). This is why diversification has a limit: you can cancel out company-specific risk by holding more positions, but you can never diversify away the risk all the assets have in common.

Worked example 2 — diminishing returns from adding more assets

n equally-weighted assets, each σ = 20%, each pair correlated at ρ = 0.3: at n = 2, portfolio σ ≈ 16.13%; at n = 3, ≈ 14.61%; at n = 10, ≈ 12.17%; at n = 50, ≈ 11.21%. As n keeps growing, portfolio σ approaches √ρ × σ = √0.3 × 0.20 ≈ 10.95% and essentially stops falling — the systematic-risk floor. Most of the benefit from this kind of diversification shows up by around 10–20 holdings; adding the 100th similar stock buys very little extra risk reduction beyond that floor.

The formal definition

\sigma_p=\sqrt{w_1^2\sigma_1^2+w_2^2\sigma_2^2+2w_1w_2\rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2}
\sigma_p(n)=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^2}{n}+\frac{n-1}{n}\,\rho\,\sigma^2}\ \xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\ \sigma\sqrt{\rho}
w₁, w₂the weight (share of the portfolio) held in each asset
σ₁, σ₂each asset's own standard deviation
ρthe correlation between the assets' returns
σpthe resulting portfolio's standard deviation
nnumber of equally-weighted, equally-correlated assets held

  1. Set correlation to 1: the two simulated series move in lockstep and portfolio σ equals the individual σ — no diversification benefit at all.
  2. Lower correlation toward −1: the simulated green portfolio line flattens dramatically as the assets' moves cancel.
Try this
  1. Raise n from 2 toward 50 with σ and ρ fixed at the worked-example values: portfolio σ keeps falling but by less and less each time — confirm it approaches, but never goes below, ≈10.95%.
  2. Set ρ to 0: the systematic-risk floor also falls to 0, and with a large enough n, portfolio σ can in principle be driven very close to zero — real portfolios rarely see ρ this low across many assets at once.
Common traps
  • Owning twenty stocks in the same industry is not real diversification — they tend to move together (high ρ).
  • Correlations between assets often rise toward 1 exactly during market crises, reducing the protection diversification normally provides when it's needed most.
  • Believing that simply holding "more stocks" always keeps reducing risk — beyond a few dozen reasonably uncorrelated holdings, the remaining risk is mostly systematic and does not go away by adding more.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • A classic 60/40 stocks-and-bonds portfolio relies on stocks and bonds historically having relatively low (sometimes negative) correlation.
  • Adding international stocks or other asset classes is a common way to lower correlation further.
  • Broad index funds hold hundreds or thousands of stocks specifically to diversify away company-specific (unsystematic) risk, while still carrying the market's systematic risk.

Check yourself

1. Does diversification reduce each asset's own volatility?
No — each asset's own σ is unchanged; diversification reduces the combined portfolio's volatility through partial cancellation.
2. At correlation ρ = 1, does combining two equally volatile assets lower risk?
No — with perfect correlation the portfolio's σ equals the individual σ; there is no cancellation.
3. Does adding a 100th similar, positively-correlated stock to a 50-stock portfolio reduce risk by roughly as much as adding the 2nd stock did to a single holding?
No — most of the diversification benefit is captured by the first several dozen holdings; beyond that, portfolio σ is approaching the systematic-risk floor σ√ρ and adding more similar assets barely moves it.

Takeaways

  • Correlation measures how closely two assets' returns move together.
  • Combining imperfectly correlated assets lowers portfolio risk without lowering the weighted-average expected return.
  • Diversification has a floor: unsystematic (asset-specific) risk shrinks as holdings increase, but systematic risk (driven by correlation) does not.

How markets work

The questionYou click "buy" on a $50 stock. Who's actually on the other side of that trade, why might your order fill at $50.05 instead of exactly $50.00 — and does that tiny-looking gap actually add up to real money?

Intuition

Markets match buyers and sellers — retail investors, institutions and professional market makers who stand ready to trade. A market order says "fill me now at the best available price"; a limit order says "only fill me at my chosen price or better", which might not fill at all. The gap between the best available buy price (the bid) and sell price (the ask) is the bid-ask spread — a small built-in cost of trading.

An index summarises "the market" as one number from many stocks. Two common designs: price-weighted (simply average the prices) and capitalization-weighted (weight each stock by the total value of all its shares).

Worked example 1 — price-weighted vs. cap-weighted

Three toy stocks — A: $100 × 10 shares (cap $1,000); B: $20 × 100 shares (cap $2,000); C: $10 × 500 shares (cap $5,000). Price-weighted index = (100+20+10)/3 ≈ 43.3. Cap-weighted weights: A 12.5%, B 25%, C 62.5%. If A's price doubles to $200, the price-weighted index jumps 76.9% — dominated by the one high-priced stock — while the cap-weighted total value rises only 12.5%, reflecting A's actual (small) share of total size.

Build it up: the spread is a real, measurable cost

The bid-ask spread looks like pocket change on a $50 stock, but it is paid on every trade, in both directions, and it is worth putting an actual number on. Buying at the ask and immediately selling at the bid — a "round trip" — always loses the spread, before any price movement at all.

Worked example 2 — the cost of the spread

A stock quotes bid $49.95 / ask $50.05 (a $0.10 spread, midpoint $50.00). Buying 100 shares costs 100 × $50.05 = $5,005; immediately selling the same 100 shares at the bid raises only 100 × $49.95 = $4,995 — a round-trip cost of $10.00, or 0.20% of the $5,000 traded, with the stock's "price" never having moved at all. A stock with a wider, 1%-of-price spread would cost roughly 5× as much to round-trip for the same trade size — which is exactly why spread, not just visible commissions, matters more for thinly-traded stocks (Lesson 0.2's liquidity idea, made concrete).

The formal definition

\text{Price-weighted: } I=\frac{\sum_i P_i}{d} \qquad\qquad \text{Cap-weighted: } w_i=\frac{P_i\times S_i}{\sum_j P_j\times S_j}
\text{Spread}=\text{Ask}-\text{Bid}\qquad \text{Round-trip cost}=(\text{Ask}-\text{Bid})\times\text{Shares}
Pᵢ, Sᵢthe price and shares outstanding of stock i
dthe index divisor (adjusted over time for splits and changes)
wᵢstock i's weight in a cap-weighted index
Bid, Askthe best available price to sell at, and to buy at, right now

  2. Move pc (the biggest company) instead: now the cap-weighted total moves the most — it responds to size, not price level.
Try this

  2. Widen the spread to bid 49.50 / ask 50.50 with shares unchanged: the round-trip cost rises roughly 10× as well, directly illustrating why trading a wide-spread, thinly-traded stock is costlier than trading a liquid one.
Common traps
  • A stock's price level says nothing about how big the company is — it depends entirely on how many shares exist.
  • A price-weighted index (like the classic Dow Jones style) lets expensive stocks dominate, unlike a cap-weighted index (like the S&P 500 style).
  • Treating the bid-ask spread as too small to matter — it is a real, recurring cost paid on every trade, and it widens exactly when a stock is hardest to trade (low liquidity, high volatility).
Where it shows up in real investing
  • The Dow Jones Industrial Average is price-weighted; the S&P 500 is capitalization-weighted — the same underlying stocks can move each index differently.
  • NYSE and Nasdaq are examples of exchanges where this matching happens.
  • Thinly-traded small-cap stocks typically show noticeably wider spreads than a heavily-traded broad-market ETF, directly raising the round-trip cost of trading them.

Check yourself

1. What is the bid-ask spread, and roughly what does it cost to buy then immediately sell 200 shares at a $0.05 spread?
The gap between the best available buy (bid) and sell (ask) price; the round-trip cost is 0.05 × 200 = $10, before any price movement at all.
2. In a cap-weighted index, which matters more: a stock's price or its total market value?
Total market value (price × shares outstanding) — that's what determines its weight.
3. A market order vs. a limit order — which guarantees a fill, and which guarantees a price?
A market order is very likely to fill (at whatever the current price is); a limit order guarantees a price (or better) but may not fill at all.

Takeaways

  • Markets match buyers and sellers through orders; the bid-ask spread is a small but real, recurring trading cost.
  • An index is just one way of summarising many stocks into one number — price-weighted and cap-weighted give different pictures.
  • A stock's price level is not the same as the company's size, and a wider spread is a direct sign of lower liquidity.

Behavioral biases

The questionThe market drops 20% in a month. Every instinct says "sell before it gets worse." Why does acting on that instinct often cost more than riding it out — and is panic-selling the only bias-driven mistake, or does the opposite instinct (trading too much, too confidently) carry its own cost?

Intuition

Humans aren't wired to process market swings calmly:

  • Loss aversion makes losses feel worse than equivalent gains feel good, pushing people to sell at the worst moments.
  • Recency bias overweights whatever just happened, extrapolating a bad month into "it will keep getting worse".
  • Herding makes "everyone is selling" feel like information, even when it's just panic feeding on itself.

Together they push people toward the opposite of "buy low, sell high".

Worked example 1 — the cost of panic-selling

Investor A rides out a 20% drop, then a 25% recovery: 1 × 0.80 × 1.25 = 1.00 — back to even. Investor B panic-sells at the bottom (locking in −20%, down to $8,000 from $10,000), waits in cash while the market claws back the first 15% from the bottom, then buys back in. B still only has $8,000 working for them going forward — a 20% permanent gap versus A, purely from the timing of selling and re-entering, even though B wasn't "wrong" that there was a drop.

Build it up: the opposite mistake — overconfidence

Overconfidence is the mirror-image bias: feeling more certain about a forecast, a stock pick, or your own skill than the evidence actually supports. It shows up as overtrading — buying and selling far more often than a stated strategy requires, each time convinced "this trade is the one that matters." Every trade carries a real cost (Lesson 0.8's spread, plus commissions or taxes where they apply), so a high trading frequency quietly compounds a cost drag against returns, year after year, exactly like a fee would.

Worked example 2 — the cost of overtrading

$100,000 invested for 10 years at a gross 8%/year. An overconfident trader turns the whole portfolio over 6 times a year, paying about 0.10% per side each time — a cost drag of 6 × 2 × 0.10% = 1.2% a year, shrinking the net return to 6.8%/year: 100000×1.068¹⁰ ≈ $193,068.99. A buy-and-hold investor incurring that same 0.10%-per-side cost only twice in the whole decade (one buy, one sell) keeps essentially the full 8%/year: 100000×1.08¹⁰ ≈ $215,892.50. The gap — about $22,823, over 10% of the buy-and-hold investor's ending balance — comes purely from trading frequency, with no difference in market timing skill assumed either way.

The formal definition

g=\frac{L}{1-L}
La loss, as a fraction (a 20% loss is L = 0.20)
gthe gain needed afterward just to get back to even

This asymmetry is mathematical, not just psychological: a 20% loss needs a 25% gain to recover (0.2/0.8), and a 50% loss needs a 100% gain — one more reason panic-selling into a loss is costly. Overtrading's cost is just as mechanical: each round trip's spread and fees are a small, certain cost paid in pursuit of an uncertain edge, and those small costs compound exactly like a fee drag (Module 4 covers fees in depth).

  2. Lower turnover toward 1 (roughly a once-a-year rebalance): the cost drag shrinks toward a fraction of a percent a year, and the ending value approaches the buy-and-hold result.
Common traps
  • "I'll get back in once things calm down" often means re-entering only after much of the recovery has already happened.
  • Treating a familiar news headline as a sound reason for a specific trade, rather than part of a planned process.
  • Mistaking a string of past lucky trades for genuine skill, which is exactly the feedback loop that feeds overconfidence and overtrading.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Sharp historical drawdowns (e.g. 2008, 2020) were followed by recoveries that caught many who had sold near the bottom.
  • Automatic, scheduled contributions (dollar-cost averaging, Module 4) are a rule-based defence precisely because they remove the in-the-moment decision.
  • Studies of retail brokerage accounts have repeatedly found that more frequently traded accounts have tended to underperform less frequently traded ones, consistent with a cost-of-overtrading effect.

Check yourself

1. Name two of the biases described here, and say whether each one tends to cause too much trading or too little.
Loss aversion and recency bias tend to drive panic-selling (an unplanned exit); herding can drive either panic-selling or chasing a rally; overconfidence tends to drive overtrading (too much activity overall).
2. What gain is needed to recover from a 40% loss?
0.4 / 0.6 ≈ 66.7%.
3. Two investors earn the same 8% gross annual return for a decade. One trades six times a year at 0.10% cost per side; the other trades twice in the whole decade. Roughly how much does the frequent trader give up?
About $22,800 on a $100,000 starting balance over 10 years (≈$193,069 vs. ≈$215,893) — purely from the recurring 1.2%-a-year cost drag of frequent trading, with no difference in underlying market timing assumed.

Takeaways

  • Loss aversion, recency bias and herding push decisions in exactly the wrong direction at market extremes.
  • Recovering from a loss requires a proportionally larger gain — losses and gains are not symmetric.
  • Overconfidence drives overtrading, whose small, recurring costs compound into a real and avoidable drag on long-run returns, just like panic-selling does.

Building your first portfolio

The questionYou're saving for a goal decades away and have a moderate risk tolerance. Using everything in this module, what does a simple first portfolio actually look like — and should it look the same at age 25 as it does at age 65?

Intuition

Building a portfolio means combining the module's ideas into one decision: pick asset classes (0.2) weighted so the blended expected return and risk (0.5–0.6) match your horizon and risk tolerance, diversify across them so they aren't all the same bet (0.7), and write the plan down so the biases in 0.9 are less likely to derail it.

Worked example 1 — a moderate starting mix

A simple starting mix — 55% broad stock index, 30% broad bond index, 10% cash, 5% alternatives — has an expected return of 0.55×9% + 0.30×4.5% + 0.10×2% + 0.05×7% ≈ 6.85% (a weighted average of each class's assumed expected return; the lab below computes an illustrative risk figure the same way the diversification lesson did, extended to four assets).

Build it up: horizon changes the mix, by a rule of thumb

Risk tolerance and time horizon aren't fixed for life. A long horizon can absorb more short-term swings (there's time to recover before the money is needed), so younger investors are commonly guided toward a higher stock weight; a short horizon has less room to recover from a downturn, so older investors (or anyone close to their goal) are commonly guided toward a lower stock weight. One widely-cited, deliberately simple rule of thumb is "110 minus your age" as a rough starting stock percentage — a starting point to adjust, not a precise formula, and it ignores plenty (actual risk tolerance, other income sources, how the "non-stock" portion itself is allocated).

Worked example 2 — the rule of thumb across three ages

At age 25: 110 − 25 = 85% stocks, 15% bonds/cash. At age 45: 110 − 45 = 65% stocks, 35% bonds/cash. At age 65: 110 − 65 = 45% stocks, 55% bonds/cash. Using the 45-year-old's 65/35 split with stocks at 9%/16% and bonds at 4.5%/7% (correlation ρ = 0.2 between them): expected return = 0.65×9% + 0.35×4.5% ≈ 7.42%, and illustrative portfolio risk = √(0.65²×0.16² + 0.35²×0.07² + 2×0.65×0.35×0.2×0.16×0.07) ≈ 11.15% — both lower than an all-stock portfolio's 9%/16%, and higher than the 65-year-old's more conservative split would produce.

The formal definition

E[R_p]=\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i\,E[R_i]\qquad\qquad \text{Stock } \%\approx 110-\text{age (a rule of thumb, not a formula)}
wᵢthe weight (share of the portfolio) in asset class i
E[Rᵢ]the expected return of asset class i
E[Rp]the portfolio's expected return — a weighted average, unlike risk, which benefits from diversification (0.7)

Common traps
  • Picking an allocation once and never revisiting it, even as goals, horizon or risk tolerance change.
  • Chasing whichever asset class performed best last year into next year's allocation — recency bias from Lesson 0.9 in portfolio form.
  • Treating "110 minus age" (or any single rule of thumb) as a precise, personalised answer rather than a rough starting point to adjust for your own situation.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Target-date retirement funds automate this kind of age-based blend, gradually shifting the mix (the "glide path") as the target date approaches.
  • A simple "three-fund portfolio" (a broad domestic stock index, an international stock index, and a bond index) is a commonly cited beginner structure.
  • Financial planning tools and advisors routinely start the allocation conversation from a horizon-based rule of thumb before layering in an individual's actual risk tolerance and goals.

Check yourself

1. Is a portfolio's expected return a weighted average, like risk is?
Expected return is a simple weighted average of the components' expected returns; risk is not — diversification can make portfolio risk lower than the weighted average of the individual risks (Lesson 0.7).
2. Using "110 minus age," what stock percentage does the rule suggest for a 30-year-old, and what's the main thing this rule leaves out?
110 − 30 = 80% stocks; it leaves out the investor's actual risk tolerance, other income or assets, and how the non-stock portion itself should be allocated — it's a rough starting point, not personalised advice.
3. Why might a 65-year-old's sensible allocation look meaningfully different from a 25-year-old's, even with identical risk tolerance quiz answers?
Time horizon differs: the 25-year-old has decades to recover from a downturn before likely needing the money, while the 65-year-old has much less time to recover, which commonly argues for a lower stock weight regardless of stated risk tolerance.

Takeaways

  • A portfolio turns the module's ideas into one concrete, written decision.
  • Expected return is a weighted average; risk is not, thanks to diversification.
  • Horizon (often approximated by age) commonly shifts a sensible allocation over time, and a good process (write it down, revisit periodically) defends against the biases from 0.9.
