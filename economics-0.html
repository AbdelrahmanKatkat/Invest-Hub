Supply & demand

The questionA bad harvest cuts the coffee supply. What happens to coffee prices?

Intuition

The demand curve slopes down (lower price, more is bought); the supply curve slopes up (higher price, more is offered). They cross at an equilibrium price and quantity. A shift of a curve (the harvest fails, supply shrinks at every price) moves that equilibrium; a movement along a curve is just price responding to the existing curves.

Q_d(P)=Q_s(P)\ \text{at equilibrium}
Qd(P), Qs(P)quantity demanded / supplied at price P
Try this
  1. Raise the supply line's intercept (a worse harvest): the crossing point moves to a higher price, lower quantity.
Common traps
  • Confusing a shift of a curve with a movement along it.
  • Price controls (floors/ceilings) can prevent a market from ever reaching its natural equilibrium.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Commodity price shocks (coffee, oil) from supply disruptions.

Check yourself

1. A bumper harvest shifts supply which way?
Right/outward — more is offered at every price, pushing the equilibrium price down.
2. Is a price rise caused by rising demand a "shift" or a "movement along" the supply curve?
A movement along the supply curve — suppliers respond to the new price; the supply curve itself hasn't moved.
3. What keeps a price ceiling from working as intended?
If set below equilibrium it creates a shortage — quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at that price.

Takeaways

  • Equilibrium is where supply and demand curves cross.
  • Shifts move the equilibrium; movements along a curve don't.

Inflation

The questionA basket of goods costs $200 this year and $206 next year. What's the inflation rate, and why does it matter to an investor?

Intuition

Inflation is the rate of change of a basket of goods' price (tracked as the CPI). It erodes the purchasing power of a fixed amount of cash — the same idea behind the real-return calculation in Investment Fundamentals Lesson 2.3.

Worked example

(206 − 200)/200 = 3% inflation.

\text{Inflation}=\frac{\text{Basket}_1-\text{Basket}_0}{\text{Basket}_0}
Basket₀, Basket₁the price of the same fixed basket of goods, one year apart
Try this
  1. Confirm the 3% result, then try a basket that falls in price — inflation turns negative (deflation).
Common traps
  • CPI is an average across a basket — personal experience of inflation can differ a lot.
  • Slowing inflation (disinflation) is not the same as falling prices (deflation).
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Central bank inflation targets widely referenced in markets; wage and bond negotiations citing CPI.

Check yourself

1. Basket goes from $150 to $153. Inflation rate?
(153-150)/150 = 2%.
2. What does inflation do to the purchasing power of idle cash?
Erodes it — the same dollar amount buys less over time.
3. Is disinflation the same as deflation?
No — disinflation is inflation slowing down (prices still rising, just more slowly); deflation is prices actually falling.

Takeaways

  • Inflation is the % change in a fixed basket's price.
  • It erodes purchasing power, which is why real return (Lesson 2.3) matters.

Interest rates

The questionA central bank raises its policy rate. You have a $10,000 loan whose rate rises from 5% to 8%. What's the extra yearly cost?

Intuition

A central bank's policy rate is effectively the price of money. It ripples into mortgage rates, savings yields, and the discount rate used to value future cash flows (the same r from Investment Fundamentals Lesson 0.3).

Worked example

10,000 × (0.08 − 0.05) = $300 more interest per year.

\text{Extra interest}=\text{Principal}\times(r_2-r_1)
r₁, r₂the old and new interest rate
Try this
  1. Raise the rate gap further: the extra cost grows proportionally.
Common traps
  • Fixed-rate borrowers aren't immediately affected by a rate change; variable-rate borrowers are.
  • Rate changes take time to work through the whole economy.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • A rate hike raising mortgage and savings rates, and the discounting link to Lesson 0.3's present value.

Check yourself

1. A $5,000 loan's rate rises from 4% to 6%. Extra yearly interest?
5000 × 0.02 = $100.
2. Who feels a rate change immediately: fixed- or variable-rate borrowers?
Variable-rate borrowers.
3. What financial-math concept uses the same "r" as an interest rate?
The discount rate in present value (Investment Fundamentals Lesson 0.3).

Takeaways

  • The policy rate is the price of money and ripples outward.
  • Fixed vs. variable rate exposure changes who feels it, and when.

GDP & growth

The questionNominal GDP grows 6% while inflation runs at 4%. How much did the economy actually produce more of?

Intuition

GDP measures total economic output. Nominal GDP growth mixes real output growth with price inflation; real GDP growth strips inflation out — the same nominal/real split as Lesson 0.2's inflation idea. A widely-cited (rough) recession signal is two consecutive quarters of shrinking real GDP.

Worked example

(1.06/1.04) − 1 ≈ 1.92% real growth — close to, but not exactly, the simple 6% − 4% = 2% approximation.

\text{Real growth}=\frac{1+\text{nominal}}{1+\text{inflation}}-1
nominalGDP growth before adjusting for inflation
Try this
  1. Set nominal equal to inflation: real growth goes to 0% — no actual increase in output.
Common traps
  • GDP doesn't capture everything of value (unpaid work, environmental costs).
  • Total GDP vs. per-capita GDP tell different stories about living standards.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Quarterly GDP releases and recession calls reported in financial news.

Check yourself

1. Nominal growth 8%, inflation 5%. Approximate real growth?
About 3% (exactly (1.08/1.05)-1 ≈ 2.86%).
2. What's a commonly-cited rough definition of a recession?
Two consecutive quarters of shrinking real GDP.
3. Why might per-capita GDP matter more than total GDP for living standards?
Total GDP can rise just from population growth without each person being better off; per-capita adjusts for that.

Takeaways

  • Real GDP growth strips inflation out of nominal growth.
  • GDP is a useful but incomplete measure of economic well-being.

The business cycle

The questionUnemployment is low and factories are near full capacity. Is the economy more likely headed toward a peak or a trough?

Intuition

Economies move through expansion (growing, unemployment falling) → peak → contraction (shrinking, unemployment rising) → trough → a new expansion. Leading indicators (new orders, building permits) tend to turn before the cycle does; lagging indicators (like unemployment) confirm a turn after the fact — near-full capacity and low unemployment are themselves late-cycle, lagging signs.

\text{cycle}(t)\approx A\sin(2\pi f t)
Aamplitude — how deep the swings are
ffrequency — how often a full cycle repeats
Try this
  1. Raise frequency: cycles repeat faster. Raise amplitude: swings get more extreme. No two real cycles are this regular — this is a simplified illustration, not a forecast.
Common traps
  • No two real cycles are the same length or severity.
  • Any single indicator can send a false signal — they're usually read together.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • PMI surveys, unemployment claims and GDP releases used together to gauge where the cycle stands.

Check yourself

1. Name the four phases of the business cycle in order.
Expansion, peak, contraction, trough.
2. Is unemployment generally a leading or a lagging indicator?
Lagging — it tends to confirm a turn in the cycle after it has already begun.
3. Why read multiple indicators together instead of just one?
Any single indicator can send a false signal; combining several reduces that risk.

Takeaways

  • The cycle moves through expansion, peak, contraction and trough.
  • Leading indicators turn first; lagging indicators confirm afterward.
