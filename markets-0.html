Market types

The questionA company sells $500M of new shares in its IPO, then those same shares trade $50M/day on an exchange for years afterward. Which of those is "the stock market" — and are equities, bonds, currencies and derivatives really all the same kind of thing?

Intuition

Each market type has its own participants and purpose: equity markets trade ownership stakes in companies; bond markets trade loans (a bondholder is a lender, not an owner); the FX (foreign exchange) market trades one currency for another; derivatives markets trade contracts whose value derives from something else (a stock, a rate, a commodity). They differ enormously in size, structure, and who participates — central banks and multinational corporations dominate FX; retail and institutional investors both participate heavily in equities; derivatives are used by everyone from farmers hedging a harvest to hedge funds speculating on rate moves.

Build it up: where a security is born vs. where it trades afterward

A crucial distinction cuts across every market type: the primary market is where a security is first created and sold — a company's IPO, or a government issuing new bonds — and the money raised goes to the issuer. The secondary market is everything that happens afterward: investors trading that same security among themselves. After an IPO, the company doesn't receive a cent from secondary-market trades — but secondary-market liquidity (Investment Fundamentals Lesson 1.2) is exactly what makes investors willing to buy in the primary market in the first place, since they know they can sell later.

Worked example 1 — primary vs. secondary

An IPO raises $500M directly for the company (primary market). Years later, that stock trades roughly $50M of value per day between investors (secondary market) — none of which reaches the company; it only changes which investors hold the shares, at a price set by the two sides of each trade.

Worked example 2 — a quick market-cap sanity check

2,000,000,000 shares outstanding × $45/share = $90 billion market capitalization — the total value the secondary market currently places on all of the company's equity, distinct from how much money it ever raised in primary offerings.

Worked example 3 — comparing market sizes

The FX market's daily turnover has been commonly cited (illustrative, order-of-magnitude figures that change over time and vary by source) at roughly $7.5 trillion/day, versus roughly $0.5 trillion/day for global equity trading — about 15× larger. Derivatives markets are often quoted in even larger "notional" amounts — but notional value (the size of the underlying contract) is not the same as money actually at risk, which is usually far smaller; comparing raw notional figures across market types can be misleading.

\text{Market cap}=\text{Shares outstanding}\times\text{Price per share}\qquad \text{ratio}=\frac{\text{Market A volume}}{\text{Market B volume}}
volumevalue of trading activity over a period (here, one day)
notionalthe size of the underlying contract a derivative references — not the amount of money actually at risk
Try this
  1. Confirm the ≈15× ratio in the size calculator, then try equal volumes — ratio becomes 1.
  2. In the market-cap calculator, double the share price with shares outstanding unchanged: market cap doubles — it's a pure product of the two inputs, not a measure of cash raised.
Common traps
  • Treating "the market" as one thing — equity, bond, FX and derivatives markets behave quite differently.
  • Confusing primary-market proceeds (money that reaches the issuer) with secondary-market trading value (money changing hands between investors, none of which reaches the company).
  • Comparing derivatives' notional size directly to equity or bond market size — notional overstates actual capital at risk.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Stock exchanges, the bond market, the FX market and futures exchanges.
  • IPOs and follow-on share/bond offerings (primary market) vs. day-to-day trading (secondary market).
  • Market capitalization used throughout investing to size and compare companies (Fundamental Analysis path).

Check yourself

1. What does the bond market trade, and who is the bondholder relative to the issuer?
Loans — the bondholder is a lender with a claim to fixed interest and principal repayment, not an owner (unlike a shareholder).
2. A company's IPO raises $200M. Two years later its shares trade $30M/day. Does any of that daily trading value go to the company?
No — that's secondary-market trading between investors; only the original $200M IPO (primary market) went to the company.
3. Why can comparing derivatives' "notional" value directly to equity market size be misleading?
Notional measures the size of the underlying contract referenced, not the actual money at risk, which is typically much smaller — so raw notional figures overstate comparable exposure.

Takeaways

  • Different market types trade fundamentally different claims, with different participants.
  • Primary markets raise money for the issuer; secondary markets just move ownership between investors afterward.
  • Market size comparisons need care — notional derivatives figures and secondary-market turnover aren't directly comparable to market capitalization.

Exchange vs. OTC

The questionYou want to sell a thinly-traded corporate bond. There's no single "exchange price" — why not, and what happens if the dealer on the other side of your trade goes bankrupt before settlement?

Intuition

A centralized exchange matches orders publicly in one order book, so everyone sees the same best bid/ask at any instant (Investment Fundamentals Lesson 1.1–1.2). Over-the-counter (OTC) trading is negotiated directly between two parties — a dealer quotes you a price privately — so prices can genuinely differ dealer to dealer at the same moment, since no single public order book exists to anchor them together.

Worked example 1 — price dispersion

Three dealer quotes for the same bond: 99.0, 99.2, 99.5 (per $100 face). The spread between the best and worst is 99.5 − 99.0 = $0.50 — real price dispersion that simply doesn't exist on a single, centralized exchange quote.

Build it up: who bears the risk if a counterparty fails?

On most modern exchanges, a central counterparty (clearinghouse) steps into the middle of every trade, becoming the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer — so you're never actually exposed to the specific person on the other side; if they default, the clearinghouse absorbs it. Classic OTC trades are bilateral: you're directly exposed to whichever specific dealer or counterparty you traded with, which is called counterparty risk. (In practice, many OTC derivatives today also use central clearing — the bilateral case described here is the simpler, traditional picture that still applies to many OTC instruments.)

Worked example 2 — quantifying counterparty risk

A $10,000 OTC position with a counterparty estimated to have a 2% chance of defaulting before settlement: expected loss = 0.02 × $10,000 = $200. This is exactly the kind of risk a central clearinghouse is designed to remove — on a cleared exchange trade, this specific exposure effectively isn't there.

\text{dispersion}=\text{max quote}-\text{min quote}\qquad E[\text{loss}]=P(\text{default})\times\text{exposure}
P(default)the estimated probability the counterparty fails to honor the trade
exposurethe amount at risk if that happens
Try this
  1. Bring all three quotes closer together: dispersion shrinks toward 0.
  2. In the counterparty-risk calculator, push default probability toward 0: expected loss shrinks toward 0 even though exposure hasn't changed.
Common traps
  • Assuming an OTC instrument has one "true" price at a given moment the way an exchange-listed stock appears to.
  • Assuming "OTC" always means uncleared and bilateral — many OTC markets (especially since post-2008 reforms) now route through central clearing too.
  • Ignoring counterparty risk entirely when it is genuinely present — it's a real, quantifiable cost, not just a theoretical concern.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • NYSE/Nasdaq (exchange, centrally cleared) vs. most bonds and many bilateral derivatives (OTC).
  • Clearinghouses (central counterparties) underpinning modern futures and options exchanges.
  • Counterparty risk as a factor in swaps, forwards and other bespoke OTC contracts (Risk & Derivatives path).

Check yourself

1. What's the key structural difference between an exchange and OTC trading?
An exchange matches orders publicly with one visible best price; OTC is negotiated bilaterally and can show different prices from different dealers.
2. Quotes of 50.1, 50.4, 49.9 — what's the dispersion?
50.4 − 49.9 = 0.5.
3. A central counterparty sits between every buyer and seller on an exchange. What specific risk does that reduce?
Counterparty risk — the risk that the specific party on the other side of your trade fails to honor it; the clearinghouse absorbs that exposure instead.

Takeaways

  • Exchanges centralize price discovery; OTC trading is negotiated and can disperse.
  • Central clearing removes bilateral counterparty exposure by stepping into the middle of every trade.
  • Counterparty risk is quantifiable — default probability × exposure — not just a vague worry.

Market efficiency

The questionOut of 100 fund managers with no actual skill — pure coin-flip luck each year — how many would you expect to beat the market 10 years in a row by chance alone? If one manager does it, does that prove skill?

Intuition

The efficient market hypothesis (EMH) says prices already reflect available information, in three commonly-discussed forms: weak (reflects past prices — technical patterns shouldn't give an edge), semi-strong (reflects public information — public fundamental analysis shouldn't either), and strong (reflects even private information — generally the least supported form). None of this is fully settled; it's a spectrum of evidence, not a proven law, and it's actively debated against behavioral finance (its own path on this site), which documents systematic ways real investors deviate from the "rational" picture EMH assumes.

Worked example 1 — a coin-flip "fair game"

A coin-flip-like move of +1% or −1% with equal probability: expected value = 0.5×(+1%) + 0.5×(−1%) = 0% — a stylized "fair game" with no predictable edge, the kind of behavior EMH predicts for a well-priced, liquid market.

Build it up: separating luck from skill

Here's the sharper version of the opening question, and it's exactly why EMH is hard to definitively prove or disprove: if beating the market each year were pure 50/50 luck, the chance any one manager strings together 10 winning years in a row by luck alone is 0.5¹⁰. Out of 100 managers, though, you'd still expect roughly that fraction to appear to be "skilled" purely by chance — because with enough people flipping coins, someone gets a long streak.

Worked example 2 — the luck-vs-skill math

0.5¹⁰ ≈ 0.098% chance for any one manager. Across 100 managers, the expected number who'd show a "10-year beat-the-market streak" from pure luck alone ≈ 100 × 0.00098 ≈ 0.1 managers — genuinely rare, but not literally zero. This is the core challenge in judging any single track record: a good run is consistent with skill, but it's also consistent with simply being the lucky one out of a large enough group (a selection effect sometimes called survivorship bias).

E[\text{gain}]=p\times\text{gain}+(1-p)\times\text{loss}\qquad P(\text{streak by luck})=p^{\,n}
pprobability of beating the market in a single year (0.5 under pure luck)
nnumber of consecutive years in the streak
Try this
  1. Make the probability unequal (e.g. 60/40) in the expected-value calculator: expected value moves away from 0 — no longer a "fair game".
  2. In the streak calculator, raise the number of years required: the probability of a lucky streak shrinks fast — but raise the number of managers in the pool too, and the expected count of lucky-looking "winners" can stay surprisingly non-trivial.
Common traps
  • "Efficient" doesn't mean "always correctly priced" — it means hard to exploit consistently, not infallible.
  • Treating a single strong track record as proof of skill without asking how large the original pool of competitors was.
  • Ignoring survivorship bias — funds that perform badly often shut down and vanish from "all funds" performance data, flattering the average of what's left.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • The case for low-cost index investing (Investment Fundamentals Lesson 2.7) partly rests on an efficiency-based argument — if persistent outperformance is hard to identify in advance, minimizing costs becomes the more reliable lever.
  • Fund performance comparisons and "manager of the year" narratives, which should be read alongside how many managers were in the original comparison pool.

Check yourself

1. Name the three commonly-discussed forms of market efficiency.
Weak, semi-strong, strong.
2. Does "efficient" mean prices are always correct?
No — it means hard to consistently exploit, not infallible.
3. With p=0.5 and 6 years, what's the chance any one manager strings together a lucky streak, and why doesn't one such manager out of 200 prove skill?
0.5⁶ ≈ 1.56%; with 200 managers you'd expect roughly 200 × 0.0156 ≈ 3 to show the streak purely by chance, so one appearing is fully consistent with luck alone.

Takeaways

  • Efficient market hypothesis has weak/semi-strong/strong forms and is debated evidence, not a settled fact.
  • A long winning streak is consistent with both skill and luck — the size of the original competitor pool matters for telling them apart.
  • Survivorship bias (failed funds disappearing from the data) can flatter the apparent track record of "the market" for active managers as a group.

Regulators & oversight

The questionWhy would a regulator fine a company for late or inaccurate disclosure, rather than only for actually losing investors money — and why is insider trading punished so much more harshly than an ordinary bad investment?

Intuition

Securities regulators — national bodies like the U.S. SEC, the UK's FCA, or the EU's ESMA framework overseeing national regulators — exist mainly to ensure fair, accurate disclosure and to deter fraud and unfair information advantages, not to guarantee investment outcomes. Their core jobs generally fall into a few buckets: requiring companies to disclose material information on a level playing field, policing market conduct (manipulation, insider trading), licensing and supervising market participants (brokers, exchanges, fund managers), and — at larger regulators — watching for risks to the broader financial system. Markets work better when everyone has access to the same timely, truthful information at roughly the same time.

Worked example 1 — a late-disclosure penalty

An illustrative penalty structure: a $50,000 base fine plus $5,000 per day a required disclosure is late. Ten days late: 50,000 + 10×5,000 = $100,000.

Build it up: why insider trading gets treated differently

Late disclosure is a process failure — information eventually reached the market, just not on time. Insider trading — trading on material information before it's public — is treated more severely because it directly exploits the information gap the entire disclosure system exists to prevent: one party profits specifically because others don't yet have the same information. Penalties for this often go beyond a flat fine to include disgorgement (giving back the illicit profit itself) plus an additional multiple of that profit as a punitive civil penalty.

Worked example 2 — an illustrative insider-trading penalty

Illicit profit of $100,000: disgorgement returns the full $100,000, plus a civil penalty illustratively set at 2× the profit ($200,000) — total $300,000, three times the original gain. (Actual penalty structures and multiples vary by jurisdiction and case; this is a simplified illustration of the shape of such penalties, not a specific legal rule.)

\text{fine}=\text{base}+\text{per-day}\times\text{days late}\qquad \text{total penalty}=\text{profit}+(k\times\text{profit})
kan illustrative civil-penalty multiple applied on top of returning the original illicit profit
Try this
  1. Raise "days late" in the first calculator: the fine grows linearly.
  2. In the insider-trading calculator, raise the penalty multiple: total penalty grows well beyond just returning the original profit.
Common traps
  • Regulation reduces certain risks (fraud, unequal information) — it does not eliminate the risk of a bad investment outcome.
  • Assuming all regulatory penalties are simple flat fines — many combine disgorgement with a separate punitive multiple.
  • Confusing "regulated" with "guaranteed safe" — regulation targets fairness and conduct, not investment performance.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • National securities regulators (e.g. the SEC, FCA) overseeing exchanges and listed companies' disclosures.
  • Insider-trading enforcement actions regularly reported in financial news.
  • Disclosure requirements that make fundamental analysis (its own path) possible in the first place — analysts rely on regulated, standardized company filings.

Check yourself

1. What is a securities regulator's main job?
Ensuring fair, accurate disclosure and deterring fraud/unfair information advantages — not guaranteeing investment outcomes.
2. Base fine $20,000, $2,000/day, 5 days late. Total?
20,000 + 5×2,000 = $30,000.
3. Why is insider trading typically penalized more severely than an honest but poorly-timed disclosure?
It directly exploits an information gap for personal profit, rather than merely delaying information that was going to reach everyone anyway — it attacks the fairness the whole disclosure system is built to protect.

Takeaways

  • Regulation targets fairness, disclosure and conduct, not investment success.
  • Insider trading is punished more severely because it directly exploits the information asymmetry regulation exists to prevent.
  • Penalties often combine returning the illicit gain (disgorgement) with an additional punitive multiple.

Globalization of markets

The questionCan "the market" really be traded at 2am your local time? And if you invest abroad and the foreign stock rises 8% in local currency, could you still lose money once you convert back home?

Intuition

As one region's trading session closes, another's opens, so some market somewhere is trading nearly around the clock. Sessions also overlap for a few hours, which tends to be when activity (and liquidity) is heaviest — multiple major markets are watching the same news at once.

Worked example 1 — a two-session overlap

If one region's session runs 03:00–11:30 and another's runs 09:30–16:00 (same time reference), they overlap from 09:30–11:30 — a 2-hour overlap window.

Build it up: capital crosses borders, and so does currency risk

Globalization isn't just about trading hours — investors increasingly hold assets priced in currencies other than their own. That introduces a return component entirely separate from the investment's own performance: the exchange rate between the two currencies can move for reasons that have nothing to do with the asset itself.

Worked example 2 — currency risk eating into a foreign gain

A foreign stock rises 8% in its local currency, but that currency weakens 5% against your home currency over the same period: your return at home ≈ (1.08 × 0.95) − 1 = 2.6% — much less than the 8% the headline local-currency return suggested, purely from the currency move. (If the foreign currency had instead strengthened, your home-currency return would have been boosted above 8% — currency risk cuts both ways.)

\text{overlap}=\min(\text{end}_1,\text{end}_2)-\max(\text{start}_1,\text{start}_2)\qquad r_{\text{home}}=(1+r_{\text{local}})\times(1+r_{\text{fx}})-1
r_localthe asset's return in its own (foreign) currency
r_fxthe % change in the foreign currency's value against your home currency
Try this
  1. Move the two sessions apart in the overlap calculator until they no longer overlap: overlap hours fall to 0.
  2. In the currency-return calculator, set the currency move to 0%: home-currency return equals the local return exactly — currency risk has fully disappeared.
Common traps
  • "Always open somewhere" doesn't mean every asset is equally liquid at every hour — thin off-peak-hours trading can widen spreads (Investment Fundamentals Lesson 1.6).
  • Looking only at a foreign investment's local-currency return and forgetting the currency conversion can change the picture substantially.
  • Assuming currency moves always hurt returns — they can just as easily help.
Where it shows up in real investing
  • Asian markets closing as European markets open, overlapping with US trading hours.
  • Currency-hedged vs. unhedged international fund share classes, which differ in exactly how much currency risk they pass through to the investor.
  • Cross-listed shares (the same company trading on exchanges in more than one country), each in its local currency.

Check yourself

1. Why is some market "always open"?
Different regions' trading sessions are staggered across time zones, so as one closes another opens.
2. Sessions of 2:00–10:00 and 8:00–16:00. Overlap hours?
min(10,16) − max(2,8) = 10 − 8 = 2 hours.
3. A foreign asset returns 10% locally, but its currency strengthens 3% against yours. Approximate home-currency return?
(1.10 × 1.03) − 1 = 13.3% — currency strength added to, rather than subtracted from, the local return this time.

Takeaways

  • Global sessions overlap, creating near-continuous trading, but "open" doesn't guarantee equally deep liquidity at every hour.
  • International investing adds a currency-return component on top of the asset's own local performance.
  • Currency moves can help or hurt a foreign investment's home-currency return — it's a genuine two-sided risk, not a one-way cost.
